Control is the use of feedback to make a robot follow a reference, such as a [motion plan](/classical/motion-planning), despite model errors and pushes: measure the state, compare it with the reference, correct <Cite id="fbs-2020" />. Feedback loops run beneath every learned policy, from motor drivers to whole-body torque control.

## PID: three ways to use an error

Picture an elbow told to hold 30 degrees while a bag hangs from the hand. With error $e(t) = r(t) - y(t)$, the gap between the reference angle $r$ and the measured angle $y$, a PID controller commands <Cite id="fbs-2020" />

$$
u(t) = K_p\, e(t) + K_i \int_0^{t} e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d\, \frac{de}{dt}
$$

The first term pushes in proportion to the error now. The second grows while an error persists, removing the steady sag the bag would leave. The third reacts to how fast the error changes, damping overshoot. Ziegler and Nichols, who called the terms proportional response, automatic reset and pre-act, published the classic tuning rules in 1942: push the loop to oscillation, then back off <Cite id="ziegler-nichols-1942" />. In one survey of over eleven thousand controllers in refining, chemicals and paper mills, 97% used PID <Cite id="fbs-2020" />.

A pole balanced upright by a motor obeys <Cite id="tedrake-underactuated" />

$$
\ddot{\theta} = \frac{g}{\ell} \sin\theta + \frac{u + \tau_{bias}}{m \ell^2}
$$

where $\theta$ is the lean, $m$ and $\ell$ the pole's mass and length, $u$ the motor torque and $\tau_{bias}$ a payload's constant torque. Near upright the loop is stable only when $K_p > m g \ell$: the motor must push back harder than gravity tips the pole <Cite id="tedrake-underactuated" />.

## LQR: choose the gains by pricing error and effort

The linear quadratic regulator (LQR) derives gains from a model. For a linear model $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$, with state $x$ (angles and speeds) and motor commands $u$, it finds the feedback $u = -Kx$ that minimizes <Cite id="tedrake-underactuated" />

$$
J = \int_0^{\infty} \left( x^{\top} Q x + u^{\top} R u \right) dt
$$

$Q$ prices state error and $R$ prices effort: a heavier angle penalty stiffens the controller, a heavier torque penalty softens it. The gain comes from one matrix equation, the algebraic Riccati equation; for the pendulum it is a PD controller. The guarantee is local: swinging the pole up from hanging needs energy-shaping control, with LQR catching it at the top <Cite id="tedrake-underactuated" />.

## MPC: re-solve the plan at every step

<Term id="mpc">Model-predictive control</Term> works like a driver who looks a few seconds ahead and re-decides every moment. At each step it plans the next $N$ inputs from the measured state, minimizing a cost subject to the model $f$ and limits $\mathcal{U}$ and $\mathcal{X}$ on inputs and states <Cite id="mayne-2000" />:

$$
\min_{u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}} \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left( x_k^{\top} Q x_k + u_k^{\top} R u_k \right) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k), \; u_k \in \mathcal{U}, \; x_k \in \mathcal{X}
$$

Only the first input is applied before the next solve, so the fresh measurement absorbs part of the model error <Cite id="tedrake-underactuated" /><Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Limits such as torque bounds and friction cones, the range of directions a foot can push without slipping, enter the optimization directly <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" /><Cite id="neunert-2018" />.

MPC grew out of 1970s and 1980s refineries and power plants, whose slow, constrained, coupled processes defeated PID <Cite id="garcia-1989" /><Cite id="qin-badgwell-2003" />. On the MIT Cheetah 3, Di Carlo and colleagues' convex MPC solved each problem in under a millisecond, 20 to 30 times a second, and one set of gains served every gait from standing to gallop <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Whole-body MPC ran on the HRP-2 humanoid with MuJoCo as its model <Cite id="koenemann-2015" />, and DIAL-MPC, optimizing full quadruped dynamics without training, beat RL policies by 50% on climbing tasks <Cite id="dial-mpc-2024" />. Solvers include acados <Cite id="acados-2019" />, Crocoddyl <Cite id="crocoddyl-2019" /> and MuJoCo MPC <Cite id="mjpc-2022" />; [reward design and the MPC debate](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc) compares MPC with learned policies.

## Whole-body QP: the bottom of the stack

A humanoid reaching for a shelf must keep its feet planted, stay balanced and move its hand at once. Its whole-body controller turns those goals into joint torques every tick. Khatib's 1987 operational-space formulation writes the dynamics in terms of the hand's motion, mapped to joints by the [kinematic](/classical/kinematics) <Term id="jacobian">Jacobian</Term> <Cite id="khatib-1987" />. Sentis and Khatib ranked tasks, contact and joint limits first and posture in the leftover <Term id="degrees-of-freedom">degrees of freedom</Term> <Cite id="sentis-khatib-2005" />; hierarchical QP solves such priorities at control rate <Cite id="escande-2014" />. Weighted controllers solve one quadratic program (QP) per tick:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\ddot{q},\, \tau,\, f} \quad & \sum_i w_i \, \lVert A_i \ddot{q} - b_i \rVert^2 \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & M(q)\, \ddot{q} + h(q, \dot{q}) = S^{\top} \tau + J_c(q)^{\top} f \\
& \tau_{min} \le \tau \le \tau_{max}, \qquad f \in \mathcal{F}
\end{aligned}
$$

The unknowns are joint accelerations $\ddot{q}$, torques $\tau$ and contact forces $f$. Each task is a weighted error term, the body's physics is the equality constraint, and $\mathcal{F}$ keeps contact forces inside friction cones <Cite id="kuindersma-2015" />. Featherstone's recursive algorithms compute the dynamics quickly <Cite id="featherstone-2008" />.

<Image id="atlas-darpa-testing-2013" />

Learned and model-based <Term id="whole-body-control">whole-body control</Term> both ship. Figure reports that Helix 02's System 0 handles whole-body balance and contact at 1 kHz <Cite id="helix-02-2026" />; Boston Dynamics collects Atlas behavior data by pairing its model-predictive controller with a VR teleoperation interface <Cite id="bd-atlas-lbm-2025" />. [Humanoid whole-body control](/rl-sim2real/humanoid-wbc) covers learned policies.

## Impedance control: making contact a design variable

Picture an arm wiping a tabletop that sits 2 mm higher than its model says. Under stiff position control that error becomes a hard collision. Hogan's 1985 <Term id="impedance-control">impedance control</Term> instead commands how force relates to motion, so the arm acts like a programmable mass on a spring and damper; at contact a controller sets force or motion, and the surface fixes the other <Cite id="hogan-1985" />. Salisbury's earlier stiffness controller set the arm's springiness along six axes <Cite id="salisbury-1980" />.

<Term id="admittance-control">Admittance control</Term> is the mirror image: measure force with a wrist sensor and command motion. A backdrivable direct-drive arm realizes impedance directly; a stiff geared arm with a force-torque sensor runs admittance in software. Ott, Mukherjee and Nakamura blended the two on a KUKA-DLR lightweight robot <Cite id="ott-2010" />. Mason's and Raibert and Craig's <Term id="hybrid-force-position-control">hybrid force/position control</Term> splits the task by contact geometry, controlling force along the constrained axes and position along the free ones, never both on one axis <Cite id="mason-1981" /> <Cite id="raibert-craig-1981" />. Polishing controls force into the surface and position along it.

Learned policies now set compliance too. Adaptive Compliance Policy learns compliance that varies in space and time from human demonstrations, reporting over 50% improvement over state-of-the-art visuomotor policies <Cite id="acp-2024" />, and a diffusion model co-authored by Hogan adapts stiffness and damping online on a KUKA LBR iiwa <Cite id="geiger-impedance-2025" />. FLORE (October 2026), a force-reactive humanoid policy, succeeded in 80% of rollouts on three contact-rich tasks, against 26.7% for a position-only policy with the same data and controller <Cite id="flore-2026" />. ForceTwin (September 2026) models an object's inertia, friction and springs from human probing; driving impedance control on a Spot and a Franka FR3, it reached 87% of goals across nine object-robot pairs, against 60% with vision-language-model priors <Cite id="forcetwin-2026" />. Hand-written routines still won a 2026 industrial contest: in the real-workcell phase of Intrinsic's AI for Industry Challenge, 90% of top teams inserted the connector with rules-based routines and force-torque feedback <Cite id="intrinsic-ai-for-industry-challenge-2026" />.

## The hardware decides what force control is possible

A geared position-controlled arm that cannot be pushed by hand and has no force sensor cannot regulate force: position error, gear ratio and the surface set it, a limit in the contact-rich tasks of [dexterity](/frontier/dexterity). <Term id="series-elastic-actuator">Series elastic actuation</Term> adds a deliberate spring for shock tolerance and more accurate, stable force control <Cite id="pratt-williamson-1995" />. For flexible joints, Ott and colleagues separate torque and link dynamics inside an impedance controller, tested in a preliminary single-joint experiment <Cite id="albu-schaffer-2003" />.

<Image id="baxter-rethink-innorobo-2015" />

<Term id="backdrivability">Backdrivability</Term> is an actuator decision that fixes which schemes the hardware can attempt. Franka exposes a 1 kHz torque layer with Cartesian impedance controllers <Cite id="franka-fci-docs" />; Universal Robots' force mode takes a task frame, compliant axes, target forces and deviation limits <Cite id="ur-force-mode-docs" />.

Near people, contact force has limits. The contact lab's limit comes from measured pain thresholds <Cite id="han-force-pain-2024" />. ISO/TS 15066:2016 held the original collaborative-robot tables <Cite id="iso-ts-15066" />; the February 2025 editions of ISO 10218-1 and 10218-2 replaced the 2011 ones <Cite id="iso-10218-1-2025" /><Cite id="iso-10218-2-2025" /> and, a 2026 comparison says, fully absorb it <Cite id="hartmann-iso-2026" />. ISO 25785-1, for legged and other self-balancing industrial robots, is a committee draft <Cite id="iso-cd-25785-1" />.

## The policy-to-controller contract

A learned action reaches the motors through a lower controller, so its type, rate, frame and limits are part of the interface. Peng and van de Panne showed that the action type, from torques to target joint angles, affects learning, robustness and motion quality <Cite id="peng-action-space-2016" />. VICES lets an RL policy choose its own compliance along with hand motion, for tasks such as door opening and wiping <Cite id="martin-martin-2019" />.

Deployed stacks run at several rates. Berkeley Humanoid runs its RL policy at 50 Hz, its state estimator at 1 kHz and its PD controller at 25 kHz <Cite id="berkeley-humanoid-2024" />. Model latency is part of the loop: a 3-billion-parameter VLA spends 46 ms reading its inputs before acting, on an RTX 4090 <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. So deadlines and hard bounds sit outside the model, holding or stopping the robot when inference is late or an action is out of range, and the weak guarantees of data-driven controllers motivate separate safety filters <Cite id="safety-filter-2023" /> (see [action spaces](/manipulation/action-spaces)).

## What is still hard

Models are never exact. MPC keeps a [sim-to-real](/rl-sim2real/sim2real-transfer) gap even when it re-solves against the [measured state](/classical/state-estimation) <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />, and that gap and the computing cost still limit whole-body MPC on real humanoids. One fix learns a residual network that corrects the model <Cite id="ishihara-2024" />; another, from Foster and colleagues (October 2026), estimates a carried payload's inertia online with an extended Kalman filter, on a real humanoid as well as in simulation <Cite id="foster-wbmpc-payload-2026" />. Boston Dynamics added reinforcement learning to Spot's existing MPC stack <Cite id="bd-spot-rl-2024" />.
