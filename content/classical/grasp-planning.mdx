Grasp planning chooses where a hand touches an object and how hard it pushes, so that the object stays held when something knocks it <Cite id="bicchi-kumar-2000" />. Its central test, force closure, asks whether the contacts together can resist a push or twist from any direction <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />, and the standard quality score is the size of the smallest disturbance that breaks the grasp <Cite id="ferrari-canny-1992" />.

## Contact models: what a fingertip can push

Picture two fingers pinching a book. Each fingertip presses into the cover, and friction lets it push sideways a little before it slides. A contact model says which forces a fingertip can transmit; Salisbury and Roth modeled several such contact types for three-fingered hands <Cite id="salisbury-roth-1983" />. A frictionless contact pushes only straight in, along the surface normal. A contact with friction obeys Coulomb's law: the sideways force $f^t$ can reach $\mu$ times the pressing force $f^n$, where $\mu$ is the friction coefficient, before the finger slips <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
\lVert f^t \rVert \le \mu \, f^n
$$

The allowed forces form a <Term id="friction-cone">friction cone</Term> around the inward normal, with half-angle $\arctan \mu$: a wedge in the plane, a round cone in space, often approximated by a pyramid to keep the analysis linear <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" /> <Cite id="prattichizzo-trinkle-2016" />. A soft fingertip can also twist about the normal, up to a limit proportional to how hard it presses <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. Kao, Lynch and Burdick extend the analysis to pressure spread over a patch, which also describes pushing an object without grasping it <Cite id="kao-lynch-burdick-2016" />.

<ExplainerFigure id="grip" />

Cutkosky's taxonomy separates power grasps, which wrap the object for stability, from precision grasps, which hold it in the fingertips for dexterity <Cite id="cutkosky-1989" />. GraspQP reports that simplified force-closure analysis favors power grasps <Cite id="graspqp-2025" />.

## The grasp wrench space

A wrench is a force and a torque stacked into one vector. Given the object's pose from [perception](/classical/perception), each contact force $f_i$ at position $r_i$ both pushes the object and twists it about its center <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
w_i = \begin{bmatrix} f_i \\ r_i \times f_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

In the plane this is three numbers: two force components and one torque <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. The set of all wrenches the grasp can produce is built from the edges of every contact's friction cone, at unit pressing force. If each finger's force is limited separately, the <Term id="grasp-wrench-space">grasp wrench space</Term> is the convex hull of the sum of the contacts' edge wrenches; if only the total force is limited, it is the convex hull of all the edge wrenches together <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. Testing whether that hull contains the origin is a ray-shooting problem dual to a linear program <Cite id="liu-closure-lp-1999" />, and Han, Trinkle and Li write friction cones as linear matrix inequalities, which makes closure tests and contact-force optimization convex <Cite id="han-trinkle-li-2000" />.

## Force closure

A grasp has <Term id="force-closure">force closure</Term> when its contacts can resist any external wrench, so the object cannot move <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. In terms of the hull, it must contain a small ball $B_\varepsilon(0)$ around the origin, some radius $\varepsilon > 0$ of push or twist in every direction <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. An origin on the boundary leaves one disturbance the grasp cannot resist, and uncertainty in friction or contact position can break closure entirely <Cite id="zheng-qian-2005" />. Bicchi separates form closure, a matter of geometry, from force closure, which depends on the forces the hand can apply <Cite id="bicchi-1995" />.

How many fingers does it take? Without friction, <Term id="form-closure">form closure</Term> needs at least four contacts in the plane and seven in space, as Reuleaux noted in 1875 and Somoff in 1897. Markenscoff, Ni and Papadimitriou showed that four suffice for any planar object with a piecewise smooth boundary except a circle, twelve for any spatial object without rotational symmetry, and seven under very general conditions <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" />; Mishra, Schwartz and Sharir gave algorithms to place them <Cite id="mishra-1987" />. With friction, three fingers are necessary and sufficient for any planar object and four in space, under the most relaxed assumptions, though a particular object can need fewer <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" /> <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

For two fingers there is an exact test. Nguyen's <Term id="antipodal-grasp">antipodal grasp</Term> theorem says a two-finger planar grasp with friction is force closure if and only if the line between the contacts lies inside both friction cones <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />: the book pinch works when the fingers face each other squarely enough. A differentiable estimator later made force closure an optimization objective, testing it within milliseconds for arbitrary hands <Cite id="differentiable-force-closure-2021" />.

## Grasp quality: the epsilon metric

Ferrari and Canny turned the yes-or-no test into a number: the quality $\varepsilon$ is the radius of the largest ball around the origin that fits inside the grasp wrench space <Cite id="ferrari-canny-1992" />, which for a polytope is the distance to its nearest face <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. With unit pressing forces, it is the worst-case disturbance the grasp absorbs per unit of finger force. A pyramid in place of the round cone makes it an approximation <Cite id="pokorny-kragic-2013" />, and Kirkpatrick, Mishra and Yap bound how much of the ball a limited subset of wrenches can keep <Cite id="kirkpatrick-mishra-yap-1992" />. The number depends on modeling choices: Roa and Suárez show that it changes with the point torques are measured about and with how force and torque are weighted, and a grasp best under a total-force limit need not be best under per-finger limits, in a mostly quasi-static analysis <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />.

On its own, the nominal score predicts real outcomes poorly. In FIRMGrasp's tests, 53% of nominally force-closed grasps lost closure in the unlucky range of friction, and in a shake test the nominal margin ranked a successful grasp above a failed one with probability only 0.53, barely better than a coin flip <Cite id="firmgrasp-2026" />. In most cases the grasp most robust to pose error is not the one with the highest $\varepsilon$ <Cite id="weisz-allen-2012" />, and predicting stability well took a combination of metrics <Cite id="rubert-grasp-metrics-2017" />. Li and Sastry's task-oriented metric instead judges a grasp against the loads the task will actually apply, drawn as an ellipsoid in wrench space <Cite id="li-sastry-1988" />, and FRoGGeR approximates $\varepsilon$ differentiably to synthesize grasps in a median 0.834 s <Cite id="frogger-2023" />.

## Reading the hull

A worked planar example shows the tests at work. A tripod of top, right and bottom contacts on a square is force closure at $\mu = 0.7$. Remove the bottom contact and the top-right pair's connecting line sits 45 degrees from each normal, outside friction cones of half-angle $\arctan 0.7 \approx 35$ degrees, so it fails Nguyen's test <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. Moving the right contact onto the bottom edge makes the pair antipodal and restores closure for any positive friction. Lowering $\mu$ then shrinks the hull and lowers $\varepsilon$.

## Learned grasping is trained on these tests

Learned grasp detectors train on labels from the analytic tests. Dex-Net 2.0 generated 6.7 million synthetic examples from 1,500 object models, labeled good when the expected $\varepsilon$ under pose and friction uncertainty exceeded 0.002, and trained a network to predict that robustness from a depth image around a candidate grasp <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />. Its planner executes the most robust [reachable](/classical/kinematics), [collision-free](/classical/motion-planning) candidate, assuming isolated rigid objects on a flat surface and one calibrated depth camera, and counts a success only if the object stays held through lifting, transport and shaking. One sampling variant reported 94% over 100 trials on 40 novel household objects; missing depth on thin parts and collisions with objects remained failure modes <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />.

| Contact-GraspNet (2021) | Trained on 17 million simulated grasps; over 90% success on unseen objects in clutter <Cite id="contact-graspnet-2021" /> |
| AnyGrasp (2022) | Cleared bins of over 300 unseen objects, 93.3% success, over 900 mean picks per hour <Cite id="anygrasp-2022" /> |

The analytic checks still catch what end-to-end models get wrong. In a May 2026 simulation study, pipelines that first estimated object pose and shape and then sampled antipodal grasps reached 1.6 to 2 times the grasp success of AnyGrasp, whose grasps failed force closure 61.3% of the time <Cite id="pose-shape-grasp-2026" />. Even learned [diffusion policies](/manipulation/diffusion-policy), which take contacts from data, depend on data pipelines that solve contact mechanics: BODex synthesizes over 49 grasps per second on one RTX 3090 GPU <Cite id="bodex-2024" />, though DexEvolve's authors note that such synthesis rests on simplifying assumptions that yield infeasible grasps, which high-fidelity simulation must filter out <Cite id="dexevolve-2026" />. A 2026 [controller](/classical/control) enforces the quality margin during execution, keeping a median 94% of it at 0.09 ms per solve <Cite id="grasp-distance-fields-2026" />.

## Multi-finger hands still struggle on hardware

[Sim-to-real transfer](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion) remains hard for dexterous grasping: Get a Grip found most multi-finger methods degrading on real hardware <Cite id="get-a-grip-2024" />. Recent systems narrow the gap. RobustDexGrasp, trained in simulation, reached 94.6% on 512 real objects <Cite id="robustdexgrasp-2025" />, DexGraspVLA reports over 90% success in thousands of unseen cluttered scenes <Cite id="dexgraspvla-2025" />, and adding wrench and touch feedback to vision raised one policy from 14 to 24 successes in 25 real trials <Cite id="touch-dexterous-grasping-2026" />. For handing objects to people, GENESIS-Handover (October 2026) chooses how a legged manipulator presents an object by matching generated grips to the person's live hand, raising handovers that needed no adjustment from 6 to 16 of 60 against the AFT-Handover baseline <Cite id="genesis-handover-2026" />.
