Grasp planning is the choice of contact locations and forces that let a hand hold an object against disturbances <Cite id="bicchi-kumar-2000" />. Its central test, force closure, asks whether the origin lies inside the convex hull of the contact wrenches <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />; the distance to that hull's boundary is the standard quality metric <Cite id="ferrari-canny-1992" />.

A contact model says which forces a fingertip can transmit; Salisbury and Roth modeled several finger-contact types for three-fingered hands <Cite id="salisbury-roth-1983" />. A frictionless point contact pushes only along the surface normal. A point contact with friction obeys Coulomb's law: the tangential force can reach $\mu$ times the normal force before the contact slides <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

$$
\lVert f^t \rVert \le \mu \, f^n
$$

The admissible forces form the <Term id="friction-cone">friction cone</Term> around the inward normal, with half-angle $\alpha = \arctan \mu$. In the plane the cone is a wedge; in space it is a quadratic cone, and approximating it by a polyhedral one keeps the analysis linear <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" /> <Cite id="prattichizzo-trinkle-2016" />. Written as linear matrix inequalities, the exact cone turns force-closure tests into convex programs <Cite id="han-trinkle-li-2000" />. A soft-finger contact adds a torsional moment about the normal, bounded by a torsional coefficient times the normal load <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. Kao, Lynch and Burdick construct the friction limit surface for such a soft contact <Cite id="kao-lynch-burdick-2016" />.

Cutkosky's taxonomy separates power grasps, which wrap the object for stability, from precision grasps, which hold it in the fingertips for dexterity <Cite id="cutkosky-1989" />. Dexonomy synthesized 9.5M grasps on 10.7k objects across 31 types of the GRASP taxonomy <Cite id="dexonomy-2025" />. GraspQP reports that simplified force-closure analysis favors power grasps <Cite id="graspqp-2025" />.

## The grasp wrench space

[Perception](/classical/perception) supplies the object pose. A contact force pushes and twists the object. The wrench stacks the force over its moment about the object's reference frame <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
w_i = \begin{bmatrix} f_i \\ r_i \times f_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

In the plane this is $(f_x, f_y, \tau)$ with $\tau = r_x f_y - r_y f_x$ <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. The primitive wrenches lie along the cone edges at unit normal force. If each contact's force is limited separately, the <Term id="grasp-wrench-space">grasp wrench space</Term> is the convex hull of the Minkowski sum of the contacts' primitive wrenches <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. If the total force is limited, it is the convex hull of their union <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />:

$$
\mathcal{W} = \operatorname{conv}\left\{ w_{ij} \right\}, \qquad w_{ij} = \begin{bmatrix} n_i \pm \mu \, t_i \\ r_i \times (n_i \pm \mu \, t_i) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Testing whether the origin lies inside this polytope is a ray-shooting problem dual to a linear program <Cite id="liu-closure-lp-1999" />. Chen and colleagues compare the grasp wrench space directly with a task wrench space by gradient descent <Cite id="task-wrench-boundary-2023" />.

## Force closure

A grasp has <Term id="force-closure">force closure</Term> when the contacts can resist any external wrench, so the object's motion is fully constrained <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. That holds exactly when the hull contains a neighborhood of the origin <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
\exists \, \varepsilon > 0 : \quad B_\varepsilon(0) \subset \mathcal{W}
$$

An origin on the boundary leaves a disturbance the grasp cannot resist. Uncertainty in friction and contact position can be disastrous for closure <Cite id="zheng-qian-2005" />.

Without friction the cone collapses to the normal ray. <Term id="form-closure">Form closure</Term>, closure by geometry alone, then needs at least four wrenches in the plane and seven in space, as Reuleaux noted in 1875 and Somoff in 1897 <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" />. Markenscoff, Ni and Papadimitriou showed that four fingers suffice for any planar object with a piecewise smooth boundary except a circle. Twelve suffice for any spatial object without rotational symmetry, and seven under very general conditions <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" />. Mishra, Schwartz and Sharir gave tight finger-count bounds for frictionless grips and linear-time synthesis on polyhedral objects <Cite id="mishra-1987" />. With Coulomb friction, three fingers are necessary and sufficient to grasp any planar object, and four in space, under the most relaxed assumptions; a particular object can need fewer <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" /> <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

Nguyen's <Term id="antipodal-grasp">antipodal grasp</Term> theorem is the cleanest sufficient condition: a planar two-finger grasp with friction is force closure if and only if the line between the contacts lies inside both friction cones <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. A differentiable estimator later made force closure an optimization objective that tests closure within milliseconds for arbitrary hands <Cite id="differentiable-force-closure-2021" />.

## Grasp quality: the epsilon metric

Ferrari and Canny turned the binary test into a number: the quality $\varepsilon$ is the radius of the largest origin-centered ball inside the grasp wrench space <Cite id="ferrari-canny-1992" />.

$$
\varepsilon = \min_{F_k \in \partial \mathcal{W}} \; \operatorname{dist}\!\left( 0, F_k \right)
$$

Because the hull is a polytope, $\varepsilon$ is the distance from the origin to its nearest facet <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. Kirkpatrick, Mishra and Yap formalized this inscribed-ball notion of grasp efficiency with a quantitative Steinitz theorem <Cite id="kirkpatrick-mishra-yap-1992" />. With an $l$-edge polyhedral cone, $\varepsilon$ is an approximation, and Pokorny and Kragic proved it Lipschitz-continuous in the grasp parameters <Cite id="pokorny-kragic-2013" />.

Under unit normal force, $\varepsilon$ is the worst-case disturbance the grasp absorbs per unit of contact force, under the nominal friction model. In FIRMGrasp's tests, 53% of nominally force-closed grasps lost closure in the adverse friction tail <Cite id="firmgrasp-2026" />. In a shake test, the nominal margin ranked a successful grasp above a failed one with probability only 0.53 <Cite id="firmgrasp-2026" />. In most cases the grasp most robust to pose error is not the one with the highest $\varepsilon$ <Cite id="weisz-allen-2012" />. Predicting grasp stability well required a combination of metrics <Cite id="rubert-grasp-metrics-2017" />.

Li and Sastry's task-oriented measure models tasks as ellipsoids in wrench space <Cite id="li-sastry-1988" />. Borst, Fischer and Hirzinger trace most metrics' drawbacks to mixing force and torque dimensions <Cite id="borst-task-wrench-2004" />. Roa and Suárez review measures based on contact locations and hand configuration and show that the largest-minimum resisted wrench depends on the torque origin and on the force-torque metric. A grasp optimal under a shared total-force limit need not be optimal under per-finger limits, and most of their analysis is quasi-static <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. FRoGGeR's min-weight metric approximates $\varepsilon$ differentiably and synthesizes grasps in a median 0.834 s <Cite id="frogger-2023" />.

## Reading the hull

The lab draws the object with each contact's friction cone beside the grasp wrench space projected from $(f_x, f_y, \tau)$. The origin ring turns green under force closure, and the readout reports $\varepsilon$. The default tripod of top, right and bottom contacts is force closure at $\mu = 0.7$. Without the bottom contact, the top-right pair's connecting line sits 45 degrees from each normal, outside cones of half-angle $\arctan 0.7 \approx 35$ degrees, so it fails Nguyen's test <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. Sliding contact 2 onto the bottom edge makes the pair antipodal and restores closure for any positive friction. Lowering $\mu$ lowers $\varepsilon$.

## Where this meets the learned stack

Dex-Net 2.0 labels synthetic data with analytic metrics: 6.7 million datapoints from 1,500 object models, positive when expected $\varepsilon$ under object-pose, gripper-pose and friction uncertainty exceeds 0.002 and no collision is modeled. Its GQ-CNN takes a grasp-aligned depth crop and the gripper depth, and predicts robustness without regressing $\varepsilon$ <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />.

The planner samples antipodal parallel-jaw candidates from depth gradients and executes the most robust [reachable](/classical/kinematics), [collision-free](/classical/motion-planning) one. It assumes isolated rigid objects on a planar worksurface and one depth camera with known intrinsics. On an ABB YuMi with silicone fingertips and a Primesense Carmine 1.08, a success meant the object stayed held through lifting, transport and shaking <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />.

On eight 3D-printed adversarial objects, GQ-L-Adv, fine-tuned on synthetic examples of them, succeeded in 93% of 80 trials. A separate CEM-augmented run of 100 trials on 40 novel household objects, including articulated and deformable ones, reported 94% success and 99% precision: 68 of 69 grasps rated above 50% robustness succeeded. The two evaluations cannot be pooled, and missing depth on thin parts and object collisions remained failure modes <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />.

Dex-Net 4.0 cleared bins of up to 25 novel objects with over 95% reliability at more than 300 mean picks per hour <Cite id="dexnet-4-2019" />. GraspNet-1Billion scores over one billion grasp poses on 97,280 RGB-D images by analytic computation <Cite id="graspnet-1billion-2020" />, and DexGraspNet 2.0 holds 427 million grasps <Cite id="dexgraspnet-2-2024" />. NVIDIA's GraspGen labels over 53 million grasps by simulating a shaking motion in Isaac <Cite id="graspgen-2025" />. GraspGen-X conditions a diffusion grasp generator on a swept-volume encoding of the gripper; trained on procedural grippers and 2 billion grasps, it generalized zero-shot to novel real grippers <Cite id="graspgen-x-2026" />.

Learned [diffusion policies](/manipulation/diffusion-policy) take contacts from data, yet dexterous data pipelines still solve contact mechanics. BODex approximates friction cones with 8-vertex pyramids and synthesizes over 49 grasps per second on one RTX 3090 GPU <Cite id="bodex-2024" />. DexEvolve's authors note that analytic synthesis needs simplifying assumptions that yield infeasible grasps, which must be filtered in high-fidelity simulation <Cite id="dexevolve-2026" />. A 2026 [controller](/classical/control) enforces the quality margin during execution, keeping a median 94% of it at 0.09 ms per solve <Cite id="grasp-distance-fields-2026" />.

[Sim-to-real transfer](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion) remained hard for dexterous grasping: Get a Grip found most multi-finger methods degrading on hardware <Cite id="get-a-grip-2024" />. DexGraspNet 2.0 transferred zero-shot to real clutter at 90.7% success <Cite id="dexgraspnet-2-2024" />, and RobustDexGrasp, trained in simulation, reached 94.6% on 512 real objects <Cite id="robustdexgrasp-2025" />. GraspVLA pretrains on SynGrasp-1B, a billion-frame synthetic dataset <Cite id="graspvla-2025" />, and DexGraspVLA reports over 90% success in thousands of unseen cluttered scenes <Cite id="dexgraspvla-2025" />. Adding wrench and tactile feedback to vision raised one policy from 14 to 24 successes in 25 real trials <Cite id="touch-dexterous-grasping-2026" />.

GENESIS-Handover, from Marco Hutter (ETH Zurich) and colleagues (October 2026), chooses how a robot presents an object by matching generated task-specific grips to the person's live hand <Cite id="genesis-handover-2026" />. On a legged manipulator, handovers needing no adjustment rose from 6 to 16 of 60 against AFT-Handover <Cite id="genesis-handover-2026" />.
