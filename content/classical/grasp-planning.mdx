Grasp planning involves determining the placement of fingers on an object and the magnitude of force that should be applied so that the object is securely grasped and will not slip out of the hand if perturbed <Cite id="bicchi-kumar-2000" />. Its central test, force closure, refers to whether or not the contact forces generated by a set of contacts can resist any external perturbation, both forces and torques <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

Contact models describe the set of forces that can be exerted by a finger on an object <Cite id="salisbury-roth-1983" />. If contacts are modeled as frictionless, then fingers can only exert forces along the surface normal; with friction modeled by Coulomb's law, the magnitude of tangential forces $f^t$ that a fingertip can exert before slipping increases proportionally to the normal force $f^n$, with proportionality constant $\mu$ <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
\lVert f^t \rVert \le \mu \, f^n
$$

The contact forces allowed at a point form a cone about the inward normal direction with half-angle $\arctan \mu$, the <Term id="friction-cone">friction cone</Term>, often approximated by a pyramid to retain linearity <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" /> <Cite id="prattichizzo-trinkle-2016" />. If the fingertip is soft, it can also twist about the normal direction subject to a torque limit which depends on the normal force <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

<ExplainerFigure id="grip" />

Cutkosky classifies grasps as either power or precision: power grasps enclose the grasped object and precision grasps contact the object using just the fingertips <Cite id="cutkosky-1989" />. Simplified force-closure analysis favors power grasps <Cite id="graspqp-2025" />.

## The grasp wrench space holds every wrench the contacts can make

A wrench is a combination of a force and a torque in one vector. Given the pose of the object from [perception](/classical/perception), if a force $f_i$ acts on the object at position $r_i$, the wrench induced on the object is <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />:

$$
w_i = \begin{bmatrix} f_i \\ r_i \times f_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

The <Term id="grasp-wrench-space">grasp wrench space</Term> is the convex hull of the wrenches generated by all contacts along the edges of their friction cones, at unit pressing force <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. Whether or not this space contains the origin can be checked by solving a linear program <Cite id="liu-closure-lp-1999" />.

Also: convex closure tests with linear matrix inequalities <Cite id="han-trinkle-li-2000" />.

## Force closure needs the origin strictly inside the hull

A grasp has <Term id="force-closure">force closure</Term> if the contacts can apply contact forces to counteract any external wrench <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. Force closure can be characterized geometrically in terms of the convex hull of contact wrenches: the hull must contain a small ball $B_\varepsilon(0)$ centered at the origin <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />. If the origin is on the boundary, then there exists at least one disturbance that cannot be countered by the grasp; in practice, however, even grasps with force closure can fail to counteract perturbations because of uncertainty in the friction or contact location <Cite id="zheng-qian-2005" />.

Without friction, <Term id="form-closure">form closure</Term> grasps require four contacts to grasp any planar object except for circles and twelve contacts to grasp any spatial object without rotational symmetry <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" />. In the presence of friction, three fingers suffice in the plane and four fingers in space <Cite id="markenscoff-1990" /> <Cite id="murray-li-sastry-1994" />.

A necessary and sufficient condition for two-fingered grasps is given by Nguyen's <Term id="antipodal-grasp">antipodal grasp</Term> theorem: a two-fingered grasp with friction has force closure in the plane if and only if the line through the contact points is inside both friction cones <Cite id="nguyen-1988" />. On a square at $\mu = 0.7$, the half-angle of the friction cones is 35 degrees. Thus, the top-right pair does not have force closure, since the line through the contact points makes an angle of 45 degrees with both contact normals.

Also: form versus force closure <Cite id="bicchi-1995" />, placing contacts <Cite id="mishra-1987" />, differentiable closure tests <Cite id="differentiable-force-closure-2021" />.

## The epsilon metric scores the weakest direction

Ferrari and Canny's quality $\varepsilon$ is the radius of the largest ball, centered at the origin, that fits inside the grasp wrench space <Cite id="ferrari-canny-1992" />; equivalently, it is the distance from the origin to the nearest face of the grasp wrench space <Cite id="roa-suarez-2015" />. Assuming unit pressing forces, it is the magnitude of the largest disturbance wrench in the worst direction that can be opposed by the grasp.

The nominal score does not correlate well with how well a grasp performs. In FIRMGrasp's shake test, the probability that a successful grasp has a higher nominal margin than a failed one is 0.53, barely better than a coin flip <Cite id="firmgrasp-2026" />. The grasp that is most robust to object pose error is usually not the one that maximizes $\varepsilon$ <Cite id="weisz-allen-2012" />.

Also: pyramid approximation <Cite id="pokorny-kragic-2013" />, wrench subsets <Cite id="kirkpatrick-mishra-yap-1992" />, combined metrics <Cite id="rubert-grasp-metrics-2017" />, task wrench ellipsoids <Cite id="li-sastry-1988" />, differentiable FRoGGeR <Cite id="frogger-2023" />.

## Learned grasping is trained on these tests

Dex-Net 2.0 labeled 6.7 million synthetic depth images good when expected $\varepsilon$ exceeded 0.002 under pose and friction uncertainty <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />. One variant selected the most robust [collision-free](/classical/motion-planning) and [reachable](/classical/kinematics) grasp and had a 94% success rate over 100 trials with 40 novel household objects <Cite id="dexnet-2-2017" />.

| System | Reported result |
| --- | --- |
| Dex-Net 4.0 (2019) | Cleared bins of up to 25 novel objects with over 95% reliability, over 300 mean picks per hour <Cite id="dexnet-4-2019" /> |
| Contact-GraspNet (2021) | Trained on 17 million simulated grasps; over 90% success on unseen objects in clutter <Cite id="contact-graspnet-2021" /> |
| AnyGrasp (2022) | Cleared bins of over 300 unseen objects, 93.3% success, over 900 mean picks per hour <Cite id="anygrasp-2022" /> |
| DexGraspNet 2.0 (2024) | 427 million dexterous grasps; 90.7% zero-shot in real clutter <Cite id="dexgraspnet-2-2024" /> |
| GraspGen-X (2026) | 2 billion grasps on procedural grippers; zero-shot to novel real grippers <Cite id="graspgen-x-2026" /> |
| OmniDex (2026) | Benchmark of about half a billion dexterous grasps over 2.6 million scenes <Cite id="omnidex-2026" /> |
| T(R,O) Grasp++ (2026) | Benchmark of about half a billion grasps, eight hands, about 50,000 objects <Cite id="tro-grasp-plus-2026" /> |

The analytic checks still catch what end-to-end models miss. In simulation, grasps generated by AnyGrasp did not satisfy force closure 61.3% of the time, while methods that sampled antipodal grasps on estimated object shapes reached 1.6 to 2 times its success rate <Cite id="pose-shape-grasp-2026" />.

Even learned [diffusion policies](/manipulation/diffusion-policy) depend on data pipelines that solve contact mechanics: BODex is able to synthesize over 49 grasps per second on one RTX 3090 GPU <Cite id="bodex-2024" />. Fast contact analysis can also be performed at control time; a [controller](/classical/control) from 2026 retains a median 94% of the quality margin during execution while taking only 0.09 ms per solve <Cite id="grasp-distance-fields-2026" />.

Also: filtering synthesized grasps in high-fidelity simulation <Cite id="dexevolve-2026" />.
