Perception for manipulation turns camera and depth data into object poses in the robot's own frame, so that [grasp planning](/classical/grasp-planning) can compute grasps against them. The classical pipeline calibrates the camera, senses depth, detects and segments objects and estimates their pose. Done well, it lets a robot grasp known household objects in clutter <Cite id="dope-2018" />.

## Errors add up across the pipeline

<PerceptionErrorBudget className="my-6" />

Picture a robot picking a mug off a cluttered table. The camera's placement on the robot, the depth reading and the pose estimate each carry some error, and the gripper sees their sum. Adding independent errors as a root-sum-of-squares gives a rough budget, and the camera-placement term grows with working distance. Tolerances can be tight: a 90 mm gripper opening around a 60 mm part leaves 15 mm per side, and one reported assembly task had only 0.5 mm of contact clearance <Cite id="play2perfect-2026" />.

## Calibration: what a pixel means

A camera projects the 3D world onto a flat sensor. Its <Term id="camera-intrinsics">intrinsics</Term> describe that projection: focal length in pixels on each axis, the image center and lens distortion <Cite id="hartley-zisserman-2004" />. Zhang's 2000 method recovers them from a flat printed pattern shown at a few unmeasured angles, with a closed-form solution refined by maximum likelihood, and needs no special equipment <Cite id="zhang-2000-calibration" />.

### Hand-eye calibration: what a pixel means to the robot

To act on what it sees, the robot also needs to know where the camera is: relative to the flange for a wrist camera, or to the base for a fixed one. That is <Term id="hand-eye-calibration">hand-eye calibration</Term>. Shiu and Ahmad cast it as $AX = XB$: move the arm by a known motion $A$, watch the camera's view of a fixed target change by $B$, and solve for the unknown camera mount $X$ <Cite id="shiu-ahmad-1989" />. Tsai and Lenz gave an efficient closed-form solution, rotation first, then translation <Cite id="tsai-lenz-1989" />, and Park and Martin derived exact and least-squares solutions with Lie theory <Cite id="park-martin-1994" />. OpenCV defaults to Tsai and Lenz, needs at least two motions about non-parallel axes, and strongly recommends many more <Cite id="opencv-hand-eye-docs-2026" />.

Small rotation errors become large position errors at a distance. A rotation error $\theta$ shifts a point at distance $d$ by about $d \tan \theta$, so one degree gives about 1.7 mm at 10 cm and 17 mm at 1 m. Tsai and Lenz's own error analysis gives a 15 mil (0.4 mm) error from a 3 milliradian rotation error at 5 inches <Cite id="tsai-lenz-1989" />.

## Depth sensing: what fails, and on what

Every depth sensor fails on some surfaces, and those failures decide how a work cell is built. Stereo cameras triangulate features matched between two images, so a blank wall gives them nothing to match; Intel's RealSense cameras add a projector that paints texture onto the scene <Cite id="keselman-2017-realsense" />. Structured-light scanners project known patterns and trade speed for precision. Time-of-flight cameras measure the round trip of modulated near-infrared light at each pixel and fail where light reaches a pixel by several routes, as in a corner <Cite id="azure-kinect-depth-docs-2026" />.

| Sensor | Principle | Listed specification | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RealSense D415, D43x | Active stereo | ±2% depth accuracy to 2 m, on a texture-less white target <Cite id="realsense-d400-datasheet-2026" /> | Error grows with the square of distance <Cite id="realsense-tuning-2026" /> |
| Photoneo PhoXi L | Structured light | 0.200 mm calibration accuracy, 870 to 2150 mm range, 250 to 2750 ms per scan <Cite id="photoneo-phoxi-l-2026" /> | No target condition given for the accuracy figure |
| Azure Kinect DK | Time of flight | Five documented causes of invalid (zero) depth <Cite id="azure-kinect-depth-docs-2026" /> | Production ended August 2023, SDK support August 2024 <Cite id="azure-kinect-sdk-retirement-2024" /> |

Neither the RealSense nor the PhoXi documents give accuracy on transparent or shiny surfaces <Cite id="realsense-d400-datasheet-2026" /> <Cite id="photoneo-phoxi-l-2026" />. Transparent objects "often appear as noisy or distorted approximations of the surfaces that lie behind them"; ClearGrasp predicts surface normals, glass masks and occlusion edges and rebuilds the depth from them <Cite id="cleargrasp-2020" />. Shiny surfaces can saturate the image, and the newer D400f model lists saturation as mitigated <Cite id="realsense-d400-datasheet-2026" />. Dark surfaces weaken the signal until Azure Kinect discards the pixel <Cite id="azure-kinect-depth-docs-2026" />, and fences and wire grids give stereo two equally good matches, which RealSense's processor can reject as zero depth <Cite id="realsense-tuning-2026" />. A surface lost from one viewpoint can reappear from another <Cite id="azure-kinect-depth-docs-2026" />. The business is moving too: RealSense spun out of Intel in July 2025 <Cite id="realsense-spinout-2025" />, and in September 2026 Cognex agreed to buy it for about \$500 million, with closing expected in the fourth quarter of 2026 <Cite id="cognex-realsense-2026" />.

<Image id="kinect-xbox-one-sensor" />

Learned models now estimate depth from ordinary images. Depth Pro predicts metric depth from one image without knowing the camera's intrinsics, a 2.25-megapixel map in 0.3 seconds on a standard GPU <Cite id="depth-pro-2024" />. FoundationStereo trains on varied synthetic stereo pairs with a single-image depth prior to work in unseen scenes <Cite id="foundationstereo-2025" />, and Fast-FoundationStereo runs over 10 times faster while closely matching its zero-shot accuracy <Cite id="fast-foundationstereo-2025" />. Prompt Depth Anything feeds sparse readings from low-cost LiDAR to a depth model as a metric prompt <Cite id="prompt-depth-anything-2024" />.

Depth yields a <Term id="point-cloud">point cloud</Term>, an unordered set of 3D points, so a network must give the same answer whatever order they arrive in. PointNet encodes each point with shared weights and pools them with an order-independent function <Cite id="pointnet-2017" />; PointNet++ repeats this within neighbourhoods at growing scales <Cite id="pointnet-plus-plus-2017" />.

## Detection: any object a word can name

Classical detectors draw boxes around a list of classes fixed at training time; Mask R-CNN adds an object mask to each box <Cite id="mask-rcnn-2017" />. Open-vocabulary detectors accept any name. OWLv2 scales training by letting an existing detector label web image-text pairs <Cite id="owlv2-2023" />. Grounding DINO fuses language and vision inside a detector and accepts category names or phrases such as "the red mug on the left"; its largest model reports 52.5 average precision (AP) on COCO 2017 validation without COCO training images, though it is weak on such phrases without matching training data <Cite id="grounding-dino-2024" />. Grounding DINO 1.5 adds a larger Pro model and a faster Edge variant <Cite id="grounding-dino-1-5-2024" />.

Many of these systems build on self-supervised features. DINOv2 learns image and patch features from the curated LVD-142M dataset, and its frozen features transfer to classification, segmentation and depth with simple heads <Cite id="dinov2-2023" />; DINOv3 keeps dense features sharp during long training <Cite id="dinov3-2025" />.

## Segmentation: the grounding layer

A box around a mug also contains the table behind it, so a grasp planner needs the exact pixels, which <Term id="semantic-segmentation">per-pixel labelling</Term> provides. Segment Anything (SAM, 2023) made segmentation promptable: click a point or draw a box and it returns a mask. It was trained on 1.1 billion automatically generated masks on 11 million images; fine structures remain hard, and its heavy image encoder is not real-time <Cite id="segment-anything-2023" />. SAM 2 extends prompting to video with a streaming memory, though tracks fail after long occlusions and on thin, fast-moving details <Cite id="sam2-2024" />. SAM 3 accepts a noun phrase or example image and segments every matching instance <Cite id="sam3-2025" />, and SAM 3.1 (March 2026) tracks up to 16 objects in one pass, doubling video throughput from 16 to 32 frames per second on one H100 GPU <Cite id="sam3-1-2026" />. Grounded SAM feeds Grounding DINO's boxes to SAM <Cite id="grounded-sam-2024" />, and CNOS segments unseen objects by matching DINOv2 features of SAM masks against views rendered from CAD models <Cite id="cnos-2023" />.

<Term id="promptable-segmentation">Promptable segmentation</Term> also feeds [keypoint interfaces](/manipulation/hierarchical), where a vision-language model (VLM) picks points and classical code does the rest. MOKA marks candidate points on GroundedSAM masks and asks a pretrained VLM to choose grasp, function and target points; calibrated depth lifts them into the robot frame and a separate grasp sampler and motion pipeline act, so MOKA trains no robot policy <Cite id="moka-2024" />. ReKep proposes keypoints from DINOv2 features inside SAM masks, and a VLM writes Python constraints over them that a solver turns into hand motion. The solver penalizes violations without guaranteeing physical feasibility, rigidity assumptions and tracking failures remain limits, and some evaluations use human annotations or disable tracking <Cite id="rekep-2024" />. RoboPoint tunes a VLM to predict affordance points directly, with no detector <Cite id="robopoint-2024" />. Dense Object Nets instead learns a descriptor for every pixel, self-supervised from RGB-D reconstructions, that finds the same spot across viewpoints and across similar hats, shoes and mugs <Cite id="dense-object-nets-2018" />.

## 6-DoF pose estimation, and how to read a claim about it

<Term id="pose-estimation">Pose estimation</Term> turns a mask into a rigid object with a position and an orientation, six numbers in all. Methods differ in how much they must know about the object beforehand, and the trend has been toward knowing less:

| Method | Needs for a new object | Approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PoseCNN (2018) | Training on that object | Finds the center, predicts distance, regresses rotation, handles symmetry <Cite id="posecnn-2018" /> |
| DOPE (2018) | Training on that object, synthetic data only | Competitive with a network trained on real and synthetic data <Cite id="dope-2018" /> |
| NOCS (2019) | Training on its category, no exact model | Pose and size of unseen instances <Cite id="nocs-2019" /> |
| SAM-6D (2023) | An object model | SAM masks matched to the model as point clouds <Cite id="sam6d-2023" /> |
| MegaPose (2022) | A CAD model at test time | Render and compare <Cite id="megapose-2022" /> |
| FoundationPose (2024) | A CAD model or a few reference images | Results comparable to instance-level methods <Cite id="foundationpose-2024" /> |
| Any6D (2025) | One RGB-D view, no CAD model | Pose from a single anchor view <Cite id="any6d-2025" /> |
| SAM 3D (2025) | One image | Infers geometry, texture and layout, including unseen surfaces <Cite id="sam3d-2025" /> |

A pose claim means what its metric means. Hinterstoisser and colleagues place the object's model points at the true pose $(R,T)$ and at the estimate $(\tilde R,\tilde T)$ and average the distances between matching points <Cite id="hinterstoisser-2012" />:

$$
m = \operatorname{avg}_{x \in \mathcal M}
\lVert (Rx+T)-(\tilde R x+\tilde T) \rVert
$$

An estimate counts as correct when $m$ is under a chosen fraction of the object's diameter, 0.1 in their LINEMOD evaluation. A cup looks the same at many rotations, so the <Term id="add-s-metric">symmetry-aware pose error</Term> matches each point to its nearest neighbour under the estimate instead <Cite id="hinterstoisser-2012" />, and CosyPose handles symmetries while matching pose guesses across several images <Cite id="cosypose-2020" />.

The BOP benchmark has tracked 6D pose methods since 2017, scoring three symmetry-aware recalls over seven core datasets on a 0 to 100 scale <Cite id="bop-challenge-2023" />. BOP 2020 supplied physically based synthetic training images, making training data part of the comparison <Cite id="bop-2020" />. By the organizers' count, accuracy on objects seen in training rose from 56.9 to 86.0 between 2017 and 2024, and for unseen objects, which methods must onboard from CAD models within 5 minutes on one GPU, the best 2024 entry reached 82.5 <Cite id="bop-2025-challenge" />. Speed still separates the two: the best unseen-object method, FreeZeV2.1, took 24.9 seconds per image against 2.7 for GPose2023, and 2D detection was the main bottleneck <Cite id="bop-2024" />. BOP 2025 added industrial tracks <Cite id="bop-2025-challenge" />, including XYZ-IBD's metallic, symmetric and shiny bin-picking parts, on which state-of-the-art methods degrade markedly compared with household objects <Cite id="xyz-ibd-2025" />.

## Visual servoing: steer by the image

<Term id="visual-servoing">Visual servoing</Term> closes the control loop on vision: instead of estimating a pose once and moving blind, the robot keeps adjusting until the image looks right. Hutchinson, Hager and Corke's 1996 tutorial splits it into two kinds <Cite id="hutchinson-hager-corke-1996" />. Image-based servoing (IBVS) drives image features, such as the corners of a box, to where they should appear; position-based servoing (PBVS) drives an estimated pose to its goal. Espiau, Chaumette and Rives's 1992 formulation relates how the features move to the camera's velocity through an interaction matrix <Cite id="espiau-1992" />, written $\dot{s} = L_s v_c$ for feature rates $\dot{s}$ and the camera's six-part velocity $v_c$; even IBVS needs depth and intrinsics to build it <Cite id="chaumette-hutchinson-2006" />.

Each has its own failures. IBVS is locally stable under rank and positivity conditions, but coarse estimates bend the camera's path, poor ones can destabilize it, and large displacements can meet local minima or singularities. In PBVS features can leave the field of view, and pose errors set the final accuracy <Cite id="chaumette-hutchinson-2006" />. Chaumette and Hutchinson's 2007 sequel adds switching and planning methods for visibility, occlusion and joint limits <Cite id="chaumette-hutchinson-2007" />. Bateux and colleagues fine-tuned a CNN to estimate the pose between current and desired images and kept a pose-based control law <Cite id="bateux-2018" />.

Learned servoing can also aim the camera. EyeRobot 2.0, from Ken Goldberg (UC Berkeley) and colleagues (October 2026), swivels the two viewpoints of one stereo camera to fixate a 3D point, with gaze policies trained by RL on real data. It beat passive stereo by 40% in real trials, and when a grasped object hid the wrist views it more than doubled the success of policies that had them, 48% against 22% <Cite id="eyerobot-2-2026" />.

## Separate model error from geometric error

When the robot misses the mug, the detector may be fine and the camera mount may have moved. Hand-eye accuracy depends on noise and on how widely the arm moved during calibration, so Enebuse and colleagues advise accounting for both <Cite id="enebuse-2022" />. Check each stage on its own output first. The [calibration guide](/classical/calibration) lists the checks to rerun after a camera remount, and the [ROS 2 guide](/classical/ros2-for-ml-engineers) shows where images, transforms and timestamps meet at runtime.

## What the end-to-end models replaced

End-to-end vision-language-action (VLA) policies absorb the pipeline into their weights. Gemini Robotics controls robots directly, built on a reasoning model that can detect objects, point, and predict trajectories, grasps and 3D boxes <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2025" />. Google DeepMind says its April 2026 reasoning model, Gemini Robotics-ER 1.6, improves pointing, counting and success detection and acts as a robot's high-level planner, calling VLAs and other tools <Cite id="gemini-robotics-er-1-6-2026" />. Such a policy needs no detector class or CAD model for a new object, but when it misses a grasp by 8 mm there is no hand-eye estimate to re-solve. Some systems keep geometric steps visible, as [keypoint interfaces](/manipulation/hierarchical) do: MolmoAct encodes observations into depth-aware tokens and draws editable trajectory traces before predicting low-level actions <Cite id="molmoact-2025" />.
