Motion planning is the computation of a collision-free route from a robot's current configuration to a goal <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Sampling-based planners explore configurations and the connections between them; trajectory optimizers improve a complete candidate trajectory against an objective <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. [Kinematics](/classical/kinematics) maps joint angles to poses, and planning sequences them.

## Configuration space

Lozano-Pérez's 1983 reformulation moves planning from the workspace into <Term id="configuration-space">configuration space</Term>. A configuration $q$ is one complete posture, the vector of joint values. The set $\mathcal{C}$ of all of them is 2D for a point robot in the plane and 7-dimensional for a 7-DoF arm <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />.

The configuration-space obstacle $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$ holds every configuration where the robot intersects an obstacle; the rest is free space <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />:

$$
\mathcal{C} = \mathcal{C}_{obs} \cup \mathcal{C}_{free}, \qquad \mathcal{C}_{free} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{C}_{obs}
$$

Shrinking the robot to a point and growing each obstacle by its shape reduces collision checking to point membership. Planning becomes the search for a continuous curve in $\mathcal{C}_{free}$ from $q_{start}$ to $q_{goal}$ <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />. Explicit boundary or solid models of $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$ are difficult to build, though semi-algebraic models for chains and trees can be generated automatically. The basic semi-algebraic mover problem is PSPACE-hard when the number of <Term id="degrees-of-freedom">degrees of freedom</Term> is unbounded <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Hardness holds for a planar linkage with multiple links and for a multi-arm robot among 3D polyhedra; Canny's roadmap method is singly exponential <Cite id="solovey-complexity-2020" />.

Sampling-based methods skip building $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$ by querying a collision checker and validating whole local paths, though closed-chain constraints make sampling difficult <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. On humanoids, manifold-constrained planners can take tens of seconds; a 2026 SIMD projection method reports speed-ups of up to 100 to 1000 times <Cite id="mcvamp-2026" />.

## Sampling-based planning

The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) of Kavraki, Svestka, Latombe, and Overmars samples configurations uniformly, keeps the collision-free ones as nodes and adds extra nodes where a heuristic marks the space as difficult. A local planner links nearby nodes, and queries become graph searches, so one roadmap serves many start-goal pairs <Cite id="kavraki-1996" />.

LaValle introduced the rapidly-exploring random tree in Iowa State Computer Science Technical Report 98-11, October 1998 <Cite id="lavalle-1998" /><Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Each iteration samples a state, finds the nearest vertex under a chosen metric, picks a control input and integrates over a fixed time interval, keeping vertices and edge paths in free space. For dynamics $\dot{x}=f(x,u)$, the Euler step is <Cite id="lavalle-1998" />

$$
x_{new} \approx x + f(x,u)\Delta t
$$

with starting state $x$ and input $u$; the report recommends a higher-order integrator such as Runge-Kutta <Cite id="lavalle-1998" />. LaValle's 2006 book drops the step size: each sample connects to the nearest point on the tree's swath, the union of its edge paths, splitting an edge when needed <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />.

Nearest-neighbor selection gives RRT a Voronoi bias, because frontier vertices own larger Voronoi regions. The report leaves convergence rates open <Cite id="lavalle-1998" />. LaValle and Kuffner's kinodynamic version steers with controls instead of straight lines <Cite id="lavalle-kuffner-2001" />. Such planners must solve two-point boundary value problems or propagate dynamics; for differentially flat robots, FLASK obtains closed-form solutions and plans in microseconds to milliseconds <Cite id="flask-2026" />.

Kuffner and LaValle's RRT-Connect grows two trees, rooted at the start and the goal <Cite id="rrt-connect-2000" />. In the six manipulation problems of a 2023 comparison, it had the best overall success rate <Cite id="orthey-review-2023" />.

Probabilistic completeness means the chance of finding an existing solution approaches one as sampling continues, with no finite-budget guarantee <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Karaman and Frazzoli prove it for simplified PRM (sPRM) and geometric RRT on $X=(0,1)^d$, $d\geq2$. They assume a solution with clearance $\delta>0$, uniform independent samples, a fixed sPRM radius, capped RRT steering and straight-line collision tests. A 1-nearest-neighbor sPRM is not probabilistically complete <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />.

VAMP exploits fine-grained parallelism to accelerate forward kinematics and collision checking without specialized hardware. Its authors report gains of more than 500x on 7 to 14 DoF robots, bringing planning times into microseconds <Cite id="vamp-2023" />. The pRRTC planner runs all of RRT-Connect on a GPU and reports up to 10x speedups on constrained reaching with 5.4x lower standard deviation <Cite id="prrtc-2025" />. OMPL 2.0 targets real-time planning through hardware acceleration <Cite id="ompl-2-2026" />; release 2.0.0 of April 6, 2026 added VAMP as a SIMD backend, reaching planning speeds up to 25 kHz <Cite id="ompl-release-notes-2026" />. The library implements PRM and RRT, includes benchmarking tools and plugs into external collision checkers and visualizers <Cite id="ompl-2012" />.

## Optimality: RRT\*

Karaman and Frazzoli's 2011 analysis separates feasibility from cost. In an obstacle-free Euclidean example with steering distance at least the domain diameter, RRT's best cost converges almost surely to a suboptimal value. Their PRM result concerns a forest-building version that rejects connections inside a connected component. Fixed-radius sPRM, which allows them, is asymptotically optimal at greater computational cost <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />.

RRT\* adds least-cost parent selection and rewiring. A new vertex takes the cheapest collision-free parent among nearby vertices, and neighbors reattach to it only when that lowers their cost. With $n$ vertices, the connection radius is <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />

$$
r_n = \min\!\left\{\eta,\; \gamma\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right\},
$$

where $\eta>0$ caps the steering distance and $d\geq2$ is the dimension. Of the paper's two sufficient bounds on $\gamma$, the conservative one is $\gamma>4(\mu(X_{free})/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$, with free-space volume $\mu(X_{free})$ and unit-ball volume $\zeta_d$ <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />.

The result assumes a bounded Euclidean domain, uniform independent samples and straight-line connections, which excludes differential constraints. Cost must be positive, monotone under concatenation and at most a constant times path length. The optimum must have weak clearance, deforming continuously into paths with positive clearance, with cost continuous near it in a bounded-variation norm <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />:

$$
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}c_n=c^*\right)=1.
$$

Here $c_n$ is the best cost after $n$ iterations, so a finite budget can return a suboptimal path. The non-optimality analysis assumes that optimal paths traverse a set of states of measure zero. Expected processing work is of order $n\log n$, within a constant factor of RRT, though RRT\* makes $O(\log n)$ collision checks per iteration to RRT's one <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />.

After a first solution, Informed RRT\* skips routes irrelevant to the query by sampling only the domain's intersection with <Cite id="gammell-2014" />

$$
\hat{X}_f=\left\{x:\lVert x-x_{start}\rVert_2+\lVert x-x_{goal}\rVert_2\leq c_{best}\right\}.
$$

This prolate hyperspheroid has the start and goal as foci and the best path length $c_{best}$ as transverse diameter. It contains every state on a strictly improving path, but its states need not be collision-free, so collision checks remain. Over 100 runs per variation with 60 seconds of refinement, it refined faster than RRT\*, and it gains nothing once the set covers the domain <Cite id="gammell-2014" />.

FMT\* gave the first convergence-rate bounds in optimal sampling-based planning <Cite id="fmt-star-2013" />. BIT\* orders its search by potential solution quality, as in A\*, and keeps improving with more time, as in RRT\* <Cite id="bit-star-2017" />. AORRTC finds first solutions as fast as RRT-Connect, then converges. It solves difficult high-DoF problems in milliseconds where other almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners failed to find solutions consistently within seconds <Cite id="aorrtc-2025" />.

## Trajectory optimization

<Term id="trajectory-optimization">Trajectory optimization</Term> makes the trajectory the decision variable, with smoothness, clearance and task terms in the objective or constraints. Neither the objective nor an initial guess guarantees a collision-free result. CHOMP and TrajOpt both plan from infeasible guesses as well as refine existing trajectories <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. Graphs of Convex Sets (GCS) poses planning as a mixed-integer program with a very tight convex relaxation, so in most cases a cheap rounding suffices for globally optimal trajectories <Cite id="gcs-2022" />.

### CHOMP: covariant gradient descent

CHOMP combines an obstacle cost with an environment-independent prior on trajectory dynamics. In the discrete form, $\xi$ holds the interior waypoints, $K_d$ computes finite differences and $e_d$ accounts for the fixed endpoints <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />:

$$
\mathcal U(\xi)=f_{\mathrm{prior}}(\xi)+f_{\mathrm{obs}}(\xi), \qquad
f_{\mathrm{prior}}(\xi)=\frac12\sum_{d=1}^{D} w_d\lVert K_d\xi+e_d\rVert^2.
$$

The first-derivative term penalizes squared velocity; higher-order terms penalize other dynamics. The obstacle cost integrates a signed-distance penalty over the robot's body, weighted by arc length so that moving faster through a costly region cannot lower it. Covariant descent uses the inverse smoothness metric to spread each gradient step smoothly along the trajectory <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />.

On the first six joints of a seven-DoF Barrett WAM, CHOMP solved 99 of 105 problems from 15 endpoint configurations. With a $50\times50\times50$ voxel grid and a Matlab distance field, 400 iterations took about 12 seconds; the core optimization finished within the first 100, about 3 seconds, in most runs. On LittleDog it ran inside a footstep controller with a separate footstep planner and a ZMP preview controller seeding the trunk. Trajectories were computed before execution because worst-case optimization took about 1.5 times as long as execution <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />.

The paper also tests a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo variant and records finite-time local-minimum failures <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />. The 2013 journal CHOMP uses Hamiltonian Monte Carlo against high-cost local minima and for probabilistic completeness, and respects hard trajectory constraints <Cite id="chomp-ijrr-2013" />. STOMP's authors show that its stochastic updates overcome local minima that trap gradient methods like CHOMP <Cite id="stomp-2011" />.

### TrajOpt: penalties, trust regions, and collision limits

TrajOpt solves a sequence of convex subproblems, with a kinematic objective summing squared displacements between successive waypoints. Nonlinear constraints enter an $\ell_1$ penalty whose coefficients grow in an outer loop as needed; linear constraints are imposed directly. With signed distance $\operatorname{sd}$ positive for separated objects, the collision penalty is <Cite id="schulman-2013" />

$$
\max\!\left(d_{\mathrm{safe}}-\operatorname{sd},\,0\right).
$$

It is nonzero inside the safety margin. Pairs out to $d_{\mathrm{check}}>d_{\mathrm{safe}}$ enter the linearized subproblem, including some with zero current penalty. A box trust region bounds each step, expanding when actual improvement matches the local model and shrinking otherwise <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

Waypoint checks can miss collisions between waypoints, so TrajOpt penalizes a swept volume: the convex hull of endpoint shapes, inflated for rotation. The rotation bound adds $d_{\mathrm{arc}}=r\phi^2/8$ to the safety margin, a correction omitted in experiments because it was well under 1 cm. The swept-volume gradient is not guaranteed accurate in 3D. The implementation used Bullet with convex hulls of robot meshes and penalized self-collisions only at discrete times <Cite id="schulman-2013" />. The 2014 journal version covers 7-DoF arms, 18-DoF full-body robots, statically stable walking for the 34-DoF Atlas humanoid and physical experiments with the 18-DoF PR2 <Cite id="trajopt-ijrr-2014" />.

### What the TrajOpt benchmark measured

The 2013 benchmark used four simulated MoveIt scenes with fixed endpoints: 198 seven-DoF PR2 arm problems with 11 timesteps and 96 eighteen-DoF full-body problems with 41. Single initialization was a straight line. Multiple initialization used four hand-picked intermediate arm configurations, or up to five collision-free base configurations sampled with arms tucked. OMPL ran with default parameters and MoveIt's default smoother, and path length was normalized by the shortest path any planner found. ### Initial guesses and the two-stage pattern

CHOMP and TrajOpt both need an initial trajectory and can stall in bad local minima. In CHOMP's tests, straight-line seeds often beat RRT seeds on the objective where collision removal succeeded; in TrajOpt's, multiple initializations changed success rates <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. The GPU multi-robot planner MR. POP supplies diverse seeds that raise downstream optimizer success, for example from 4% to 72% <Cite id="mr-pop-2026" />.

Ratliff and colleagues describe PRM and RRT as commonly deployed in two phases: find a feasible path, then remove redundant or jerky motion. Schulman and colleagues give trajectory optimization two roles, refining another method's output or planning from scratch <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. Shortcutting, a cheap second stage, repeatedly picks two trajectory points and tries to replace the segment between them with a shorter collision-free one <Cite id="hauser-shortcut-2010" />.

As of January 2023, MoveIt 2 recommends Time-Optimal Trajectory Generation (TOTG) for time parameterization <Cite id="moveit-trajectory-processing-2026" />. Because TOTG sets no jerk limits, Ruckig jerk-limited smoothing can follow it as a further post-processing step <Cite id="moveit-time-parameterization-2026" />. PickNik's MoveIt Pro 10.1.0, released September 18, 2026, includes a ProRRT shortcut stage <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-1-2026" />.

## Where this meets the learned stack

Motion Policy Networks trained on over 3 million motion planning problems in over 500,000 [environments](/world-models/generative-sim) <Cite id="mpinets-2022" />. Neural MP distills motion-planner data into a reactive generalist policy and adds lightweight optimization for safety. Its authors report success gains of 23%, 17% and 79% over sampling, optimization and learning-based planners <Cite id="neural-mp-2024" />. A 2026 survey of neural motion planners finds that they often struggle to generalize to unseen, out-of-distribution planning settings <Cite id="neural-motion-planner-survey-2026" />.

Deep Reactive Policy replaced AIT\* with cuRobo as its expert planner because GPU acceleration let it scale to 10 million expert trajectories. Its authors report that AIT\* achieved 0% success on all dynamic tasks, and cuRobo fell to 3.00% when the goal was temporarily obstructed <Cite id="deep-reactive-policy-2025" />.

Receding-horizon model-predictive control keeps optimization inside the feedback loop <Cite id="mayne-2000" />; [reward design and the MPC debate](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc) weighs it against learned policies. Diffusion Policy represents a visuomotor policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process run with receding-horizon control <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. [Control](/classical/control) and state estimation track the resulting trajectories and locate the robot.

## The runtime handoff contract

A deployable planner needs the robot model, current state, collision geometry, allowed contacts, attached objects, joint and timing limits and a controller that can execute the result. A path checked against a stale scene is unsafe. TOTG time-parameterizes a path, computing the time-optimal trajectory that exactly follows a differentiable joint-space path within velocity and acceleration bounds <Cite id="totg-2012" />. Ruckig adds real-time jerk-limited generation; its authors tested it on over 1,000,000,000 random trajectories <Cite id="ruckig-2021" />. The cuRoboV2 authors note that fast planners output physically unexecutable trajectories, and report 99.7% success under a 3 kg payload against 72 to 77% for baselines <Cite id="curobo-v2-2026" />.

The [ROS 2 guide](/classical/ros2-for-ml-engineers) maps these artifacts onto the runtime graph. In hybrid systems a learned component proposes a goal, cost, waypoint or action chunk, and a planner or command gate checks what the model cannot certify. Validators fail too: MoveIt Pro 10.1.0 fixed a shortcut stage that let paths pass through obstacles between collision checks, and a ValidateTrajectory bug that checked the wrong robot configuration and risked passing a colliding trajectory as collision-free <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-1-2026" />.
