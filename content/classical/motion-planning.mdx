Motion planning is the problem of computing a collision-free motion that takes a robot from its current posture to a goal, a sequence of joint angles that [kinematics](/classical/kinematics) turns into poses <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Sampling planners construct a path by connecting random collision-free postures, and trajectory optimizers start with an initial trajectory and optimize the whole motion until it is smooth and collision-free <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /> <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

## In configuration space the robot is a point

A configuration $q$ specifies all of the robot's joint values as one point in <Term id="configuration-space">configuration space</Term>, so a seven-joint arm is a point in 7 dimensions. Configurations at which the robot collides with an obstacle are in $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$, and all other configurations are in the free space $\mathcal{C}_{free} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{C}_{obs}$. A collision-free path is then a path that only passes through free space <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />.

Computing an exact representation of that space is intractable: deciding whether a path exists, known as the generalized mover's problem, is PSPACE-hard <Cite id="reif-1979" />, and remains so even in the basic version with unbounded <Term id="degrees-of-freedom">degrees of freedom</Term> <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Sampling planners avoid representing the space, and instead ask a collision checker whether particular postures and short paths are collision-free <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />.

Constraints make sampling harder. For a robot holding a tray level, the configurations at which the tray is level form a very small subset of its space and are unlikely to be found by random sampling <Cite id="kingston-constraints-2018" />. MCVAMP uses vector instructions on CPUs to project many sampled configurations onto that subset at once <Cite id="mcvamp-2026" />, and ReVAMP performs inverse kinematics analytically to sample directly from it <Cite id="revamp-2026" />.

Also: Canny's roadmap <Cite id="solovey-complexity-2020" />.

## Sampling planners grow graphs through free space

The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) builds a graph of sampled collision-free postures once, which can then be used to answer many planning queries via graph search <Cite id="kavraki-1996" />. In the rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT), one tree is grown from the start by extending the node closest to a randomly sampled configuration toward that configuration and, if the extension is collision-free, adding the new node to the tree <Cite id="lavalle-1998" />. Because the frontier nodes are closest to unexplored space, the tree is pulled outward.

<ExplainerFigure id="path" />

RRT-Connect grows trees from both the start and goal configurations, stopping when the trees connect <Cite id="rrt-connect-2000" />. RRT-Connect led a 2023 comparison on six manipulation problems <Cite id="orthey-review-2023" />. These planners are probabilistically complete, meaning that if a solution exists, the probability of finding a solution approaches one as sampling continues <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Karaman and Frazzoli proved this for RRT and a simplified version of PRM <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />.

Also: kinodynamic RRT <Cite id="lavalle-kuffner-2001" />, FLASK <Cite id="flask-2026" />, OMPL <Cite id="ompl-2012" />, OMPL 2.0 <Cite id="ompl-2-2026" />.

## Parallel hardware keeps sampling ahead

Kinematics and collision checking take much of a planner's time, and both can be parallelized. On a GPU, sampling-based planning solved about 90% of feasible dual-arm planning problems with 14 joints in 2 seconds, while no search or optimization-based planner was able to solve a third of these problems <Cite id="prismplan-2026" />.

## RRT\* rewires its way to the optimal path

Even in empty space, RRT's best path almost surely converges to a suboptimal one <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />. RRT\* improves on this by rewiring: each new node takes the cheapest free parent among its neighbors, and neighbors switch to the new node if it is cheaper for them to do so, within a radius <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />

$$
r_n = \min\!\left\{\eta,\; \gamma\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right\},
$$

where $n$ is the number of nodes in the tree, $d$ is the dimension of the space being searched, $\eta$ is a maximum allowable step size, and $\gamma$ is a constant larger than a lower bound that depends on the volume of free space. The best cost then converges to the optimal cost with probability one, requiring order $n\log n$ work <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />. In 2020 Solovey and colleagues showed that the original proof has a gap, and the corrected proof suggests a larger rewiring radius, on the order of $(\log n/n)^{1/(d+1)}$ <Cite id="solovey-rrt-star-2020" />.

| Planner | How it improves paths faster |
| --- | --- |
| FMT\* (2013) | Grows a tree over a fixed batch of samples in cost order <Cite id="fmt-star-2013" /> |
| Informed RRT\* (2014) | After a first solution, samples only the ellipsoid that could hold a shorter path <Cite id="gammell-2014" /> |
| BIT\* (2017) | Orders batches of candidate connections by the cost they could give <Cite id="bit-star-2017" /> |
| AO-RRT-Connect (2025) | Adds asymptotic optimality to RRT-Connect, keeping its fast first answer <Cite id="aorrtc-2025" /> |

## Trajectory optimization pushes a whole motion clear

<Term id="trajectory-optimization">Trajectory optimization</Term> refers to approaches in which the unknown to be solved for is an entire trajectory and the objective function contains terms for jerkiness, obstacle proximity and the task. CHOMP and TrajOpt can move trajectories away from obstacles given initial guesses that pass through them, albeit with no guarantee of success <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /> <Cite id="schulman-2013" />. Graphs of Convex Sets casts planning as a mixed-integer program and can usually extract a globally optimal trajectory by rounding the solution of a tight relaxation <Cite id="gcs-2022" />.

In CHOMP, a trajectory is parameterized by a sequence of waypoints $\xi$, and the cost function is a smoothness term plus an obstacle term <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />:

$$
\mathcal U(\xi)=f_{\mathrm{prior}}(\xi)+f_{\mathrm{obs}}(\xi)
$$

Each gradient step makes changes smoothly over the entire trajectory. CHOMP was used to compute trajectories for six joints of a Barrett WAM arm and succeeded in 99 of 105 test cases <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />.

TrajOpt solves a sequence of convex optimization problems inside a trust region, augmenting the objective with increasing penalty terms until the hard constraints are satisfied. Its collision penalty $\max(d_{\mathrm{safe}}-\operatorname{sd},\,0)$ covers the swept volume between consecutive waypoints, so collisions between waypoints are caught <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

<Image id="atlas-vrc-simulation-2013" />

Its 2013 simulated benchmark consisted of 198 problems for the arm of the PR2 robot and 96 problems for its full body <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

Given only one initial guess, TrajOpt solved fewer arm problems than either OMPL planner; given multiple initial guesses, it solved the most <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

Deployments use a two-stage approach: a feasible path is first found using a sampling-based planner and then improved using shortcutting, the process of connecting path points with shorter collision-free segments, which is the cheapest way of improving a path <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /> <Cite id="hauser-shortcut-2010" />.

Also: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo CHOMP <Cite id="chomp-ijrr-2013" />, gradient-free STOMP <Cite id="stomp-2011" />, TrajOpt on Atlas <Cite id="trajopt-ijrr-2014" />, cuRobo on a gantry <Cite id="industrial-curobo-2025" />, MR-POP <Cite id="mr-pop-2026" />.

## Learned planners trade guarantees for speed

Motion Policy Networks were trained on more than 3 million planning problems in more than 500,000 [environments](/world-models/generative-sim) <Cite id="mpinets-2022" />, yet neural planners often fail in unseen settings <Cite id="neural-motion-planner-survey-2026" />. In Deep Reactive Policy, the authors replaced their AIT\* expert with the GPU-based planner cuRobo to scale to 10 million expert trajectories <Cite id="deep-reactive-policy-2025" />.

Ariadne learns from valid postures alone. Trained as one planner per scene, it averaged a 53.3% success rate against 44.7% for RRT on planning tasks for two seven-joint arms <Cite id="ariadne-2026" />.

Also: Neural Motion Planning <Cite id="neural-mp-2024" />, receding-horizon MPC <Cite id="mayne-2000" />, Diffusion Policy <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />, [MPC against learned policies](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc).

## A plan is only as safe as its execution

A deployable planner needs up-to-date information on the robot model, current state, collision geometry and limits, and a [controller](/classical/control) that can execute the result; the path must be checked against the latest collision geometry to be safe. The [ROS 2 guide](/classical/ros2-for-ml-engineers) maps these onto the runtime.

A planner's path has no clock. Time-Optimal Trajectory Generation can be used to generate the fastest possible timing given velocity and acceleration limits <Cite id="totg-2012" />, as recommended by MoveIt 2 <Cite id="moveit-trajectory-processing-2026" />, and the result can be smoothed with the jerk-limited trajectory generator Ruckig <Cite id="ruckig-2021" /> <Cite id="moveit-time-parameterization-2026" />.

Payloads change what is executable. cuRoboV2 reports a 99.7% execution success rate under a 3 kg payload, outperforming baseline planners at 72 to 77% <Cite id="curobo-v2-2026" />. Fast planning of such motions with cuRoboV2-Fast takes 4.4 ms on an RTX 5090 <Cite id="curobo-v2-fast-2026" />.

Validators fail too. MoveIt Pro 10.1.0 fixed a bug that allowed a colliding trajectory to be marked collision-free <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-1-2026" />. In MoveIt Pro 10.2.0, all trajectories are re-validated, resulting in 7 to 12 times less residual tunneling on PickNik's tunneling benchmark <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-2-2026" />.
