Motion planning finds a collision-free route from a robot's current posture to a goal, as a sequence of the joint angles that [kinematics](/classical/kinematics) turns into poses <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Sampling planners explore random postures and connect them; trajectory optimizers bend a whole candidate motion until it is smooth and clear <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. Any arm moving among obstacles uses one or both.

## Configuration space: the robot becomes a point

Picture an arm reaching past a shelf to grab a box. Checking every link against every shelf edge in 3D is messy. Lozano-Pérez's 1983 reformulation plans instead in <Term id="configuration-space">configuration space</Term>, where one point $q$, the list of all joint values, is one complete posture. The space $\mathcal{C}$ of all postures has as many dimensions as the robot has joints: 2 for a point moving in a plane, 7 for a seven-joint arm <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />.

Every posture in which the arm touches the shelf belongs to the obstacle region $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$; the rest is free space, $\mathcal{C}_{free} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{C}_{obs}$. The robot has shrunk to a point and the obstacles have grown to match its shape, so planning becomes finding a continuous curve through free space from $q_{start}$ to $q_{goal}$ <Cite id="lozano-perez-1983" />.

Mapping the obstacle region exactly is intractable in general. Reif showed in 1979 that the generalized mover's problem is PSPACE-hard <Cite id="reif-1979" />, a class believed harder than NP; the basic version stays PSPACE-hard when the number of <Term id="degrees-of-freedom">degrees of freedom</Term> is unbounded <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />, even for a planar linkage, while Canny's roadmap method is singly exponential <Cite id="solovey-complexity-2020" />. Sampling planners sidestep the map: they only ask a collision checker whether particular postures and short paths are free <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />.

Constraints make sampling harder. A robot holding a tray level, or two arms gripping one box, can only use a thin slice of its space that random samples almost never hit <Cite id="kingston-constraints-2018" /> <Cite id="revamp-2026" />. MCVAMP projects many samples onto that slice at once with CPU vector instructions, including for quasi-static humanoid motion <Cite id="mcvamp-2026" />, and ReVAMP (September 2026) samples the slice directly through analytic inverse kinematics <Cite id="revamp-2026" />.

## Sampling-based planning

A sampling planner throws random postures into the space, keeps the collision-free ones and links nearby pairs whose connecting path is also free. The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) of Kavraki, Svestka, Latombe and Overmars builds such a graph once, adding extra nodes where the space looks difficult, and then answers many start-goal queries as graph searches <Cite id="kavraki-1996" />.

The rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT), which LaValle introduced in an October 1998 technical report, grows a single tree from the start <Cite id="lavalle-1998" /><Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Each step picks a random point, finds the nearest node in the tree and extends a short way toward it, keeping the new branch only if it is collision-free. Because nodes on the frontier are nearest to more of the empty space, the tree is pulled outward into unexplored regions <Cite id="lavalle-1998" />. The original extends by applying a control input for a fixed time, so it also handles robots that cannot move in straight lines; LaValle and Kuffner's kinodynamic RRT developed that use <Cite id="lavalle-kuffner-2001" />, and FLASK plans in coordinates where such connections have closed-form solutions <Cite id="flask-2026" />.

<ExplainerFigure id="path" />

Kuffner and LaValle's RRT-Connect grows two trees, from start and goal, until they meet <Cite id="rrt-connect-2000" />. It had the best overall success rate on the six manipulation problems of a 2023 comparison <Cite id="orthey-review-2023" />.

What these planners promise is probabilistic completeness: if a solution exists, the chance of finding it approaches one as sampling continues, with no guarantee for a finite budget <Cite id="lavalle-2006" />. Karaman and Frazzoli proved it for RRT and a simplified PRM, assuming a solution with some clearance, uniform samples and straight-line collision tests; a PRM that links each node only to its single nearest neighbor is not complete <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />. OMPL, the Open Motion Planning Library, implements PRM, RRT and many variants with benchmarking tools <Cite id="ompl-2012" />; its 2.0 paper adds planning with geometric constraints and temporal goals <Cite id="ompl-2-2026" />.

## Planning on vector hardware and GPUs

A planner spends much of its time on forward kinematics and collision checks, and both parallelize. VAMP runs them in parallel with SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) CPU instructions <Cite id="vamp-2023" />, and since OMPL 2.0.0 (April 6, 2026) it is an OMPL backend for planning at up to 25 kHz <Cite id="ompl-release-notes-2026" />. On a GPU, sampling still holds up on hard arms. PrismPlan (September 2026) ran six GPU planners on one shared collision backend over 1,800 problems with a 2-second budget: on a 14-joint dual-arm system sampling solved about 90 percent of feasible problems while no search or optimization planner solved a third <Cite id="prismplan-2026" />.

## Optimality: RRT\*

Finding a path and finding a good path are separate problems. Karaman and Frazzoli showed in 2011 that RRT's best path converges, almost surely, to a suboptimal one even in a simple empty space <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />. Their RRT\* fixes this by rewiring: each new node takes the cheapest collision-free parent among its neighbors, and neighbors switch to it when that shortens their route. The neighborhood shrinks as the tree grows, with radius <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />

$$
r_n = \min\!\left\{\eta,\; \gamma\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right\},
$$

for $n$ nodes in $d$ dimensions, with $\eta$ a cap on the step and $\gamma$ a constant that must exceed a bound set by the free space's volume. Under stated assumptions (a bounded space, uniform samples, straight-line connections, a positive cost no more than a constant times path length, and an optimal path that can be nudged clear of obstacles) the best cost converges to the optimum with probability one, and the expected work is of order $n\log n$, within a constant factor of RRT <Cite id="karaman-frazzoli-2011" />. A finite budget can still return a poor path. In 2020 Solovey, Janson, Schmerling, Frazzoli and Pavone found a logical gap in the original proof; their corrected proof suggests a larger radius, scaling as $(\log n/n)^{1/(d+1)}$ <Cite id="solovey-rrt-star-2020" />.

Later planners improve paths faster. FMT\* grows a tree over a fixed batch of samples in order of cost <Cite id="fmt-star-2013" />. After a first solution, Informed RRT\* samples only an ellipsoid, with start and goal as its foci, that could contain a shorter path; over 100 runs per setting with 60 seconds of refinement it improved paths faster than RRT\*, and it gains nothing once that region covers the whole space <Cite id="gammell-2014" />. BIT\* orders batches of candidate connections by the solution cost they could give <Cite id="bit-star-2017" />, and AO-RRT-Connect adds asymptotic optimality to RRT-Connect while keeping its fast first answer <Cite id="aorrtc-2025" />.

## Trajectory optimization

<Term id="trajectory-optimization">Trajectory optimization</Term> treats the whole motion as the unknown and adjusts it to lower a cost for jerkiness, closeness to obstacles and the task. Nothing guarantees a collision-free result, but CHOMP and TrajOpt can start from a guess that passes through the shelf and push it out <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. Graphs of Convex Sets (GCS) poses planning as a mixed-integer program with a very tight convex relaxation, so in most cases a cheap rounding step gives globally optimal trajectories <Cite id="gcs-2022" />.

### CHOMP and STOMP

CHOMP's cost is a smoothness term plus an obstacle term <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />:

$$
\mathcal U(\xi)=f_{\mathrm{prior}}(\xi)+f_{\mathrm{obs}}(\xi)
$$

Here $\xi$ is the list of waypoints. The smoothness term penalizes squared velocity and ignores the scene. The obstacle term adds up a penalty from the signed distance to obstacles over the robot's body, weighted by arc length so that rushing through a bad region cannot lower it. Each gradient step is spread smoothly along the whole path instead of nudging single waypoints <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />. On six joints of a Barrett WAM arm CHOMP solved 99 of 105 problems, and on the LittleDog quadruped it ran inside a footstep controller, planned ahead because worst-case optimization took about 1.5 times as long as execution <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />. The 2013 journal version adds Hamiltonian Monte Carlo to escape high-cost local minima and respects hard constraints <Cite id="chomp-ijrr-2013" />. STOMP needs no gradient at all: it samples noisy variants of the trajectory and blends the cheaper ones <Cite id="stomp-2011" />.

### TrajOpt: penalties, trust regions, and collision limits

TrajOpt solves a sequence of convex subproblems, minimizing the summed squared distance between successive waypoints. Hard nonlinear constraints become penalties whose weight grows until they are met, and a box-shaped trust region limits each step, growing when the local model predicts well and shrinking when it does not. The collision penalty is $\max(d_{\mathrm{safe}}-\operatorname{sd},\,0)$: zero when the signed distance $\operatorname{sd}$ to an obstacle exceeds a safety margin $d_{\mathrm{safe}}$, rising as the robot gets closer <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

Checking only waypoints can miss a collision between them, so TrajOpt penalizes the swept volume, the convex hull of the robot's shape at consecutive waypoints. Its gradient is not guaranteed accurate in 3D, and self-collisions were checked only at discrete times <Cite id="schulman-2013" />.

### Initial guesses and the two-stage pattern

Optimizers can stall in poor local minima, so the starting guess matters. In CHOMP's tests straight-line seeds often beat RRT seeds where collision removal succeeded; in TrajOpt's, using several seeds changed success rates <Cite id="ratliff-2009" /><Cite id="schulman-2013" />. The common deployment runs in two stages: a sampling planner finds a feasible path, and a second stage removes redundant or jerky motion <Cite id="ratliff-2009" />. Shortcutting, the cheapest second stage, repeatedly picks two points on the path and tries to join them with a shorter collision-free segment <Cite id="hauser-shortcut-2010" />. The split scales to teams: MR-POP uses a parallel GPU sampling planner to seed an optimizer with feasible multi-robot trajectories <Cite id="mr-pop-2026" />.

## Learned planners

Learned planners trade guarantees for speed and reactivity, and still struggle outside their training data. Motion Policy Networks trained on over 3 million planning problems in over 500,000 [environments](/world-models/generative-sim) <Cite id="mpinets-2022" />, yet a 2026 survey finds that neural motion planners often fail to generalize to unseen settings <Cite id="neural-motion-planner-survey-2026" />. Neural Motion Planning distills expert plans from simulated clutter into a reactive policy and adds light optimization on a real arm <Cite id="neural-mp-2024" />. Deep Reactive Policy switched its expert from the AIT\* planner to cuRobo because the GPU let it scale to 10 million expert trajectories; its authors report that AIT\* had 0% success on all dynamic tasks, and cuRobo fell to 3.00% when the goal was temporarily blocked <Cite id="deep-reactive-policy-2025" />.

Ariadne, from Carnegie Mellon's Silong Yong, Guanya Shi and colleagues (October 2026, accepted at NeurIPS 2026), learns from valid postures alone, with no example trajectories, and builds a path by following the shape of the learned space. On DualKUKA, where two seven-joint arms must avoid obstacles and each other, it averaged 53.3% success across three environments, against 44.7% for RRT and 53.2% for PBDMP, which also receives the scene layout. It learns one planner per scene <Cite id="ariadne-2026" />.

Receding-horizon model-predictive control keeps optimization inside the feedback loop <Cite id="mayne-2000" />, and Diffusion Policy runs a learned visuomotor policy in the same receding-horizon way <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. [Reward design and the MPC debate](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc) weighs the two; [control](/classical/control) and state estimation then track the trajectory and locate the robot.

## The runtime handoff contract

A plan is only as safe as the system that executes it. A deployable planner needs the robot model, current state, collision geometry, allowed contacts, attached objects, joint and timing limits, and a controller that can follow the result; a path checked against a stale scene is unsafe.

A planner's path is a list of postures with no clock. Time-Optimal Trajectory Generation (TOTG) computes the fastest timing that follows a smooth joint path exactly within velocity and acceleration limits <Cite id="totg-2012" />, and MoveIt 2 has recommended it since January 2023 <Cite id="moveit-trajectory-processing-2026" />. Because TOTG sets no jerk limit, Ruckig, a real-time jerk-limited generator <Cite id="ruckig-2021" />, can smooth its output <Cite id="moveit-time-parameterization-2026" />.

Payloads and torque limits change what is executable. The cuRoboV2 authors note that fast planners output physically unexecutable trajectories, and report 99.7% success under a 3 kg payload against 72 to 77% for baselines <Cite id="curobo-v2-2026" />. cuRoboV2-Fast (November 2026) plans collision-free, torque-limited motions with that payload in 4.4 ms on average on an RTX 5090 GPU, 14.7 times faster than cuRoboV2 <Cite id="curobo-v2-fast-2026" />.

In hybrid systems a learned component proposes a goal, cost, waypoint or action chunk, and a planner or command gate checks what the model cannot certify; the [ROS 2 guide](/classical/ros2-for-ml-engineers) maps these pieces onto the runtime. Validators fail too. MoveIt Pro 10.1.0 fixed a shortcut stage that let paths pass through obstacles between collision checks, and a bug that could pass a colliding trajectory as collision-free <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-1-2026" />. Release 10.2.0 (October 2026) added an opt-in check that re-validates every planned trajectory and replans on collision, cutting residual tunneling through obstacles by roughly 7 to 12 times on PickNik's benchmark <Cite id="moveit-pro-10-2-2026" />.
