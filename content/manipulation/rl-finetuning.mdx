<Term id="imitation-learning">Imitation learning</Term> gets a policy that is good on the demonstration distribution and brittle off it, because small per-step errors compound into states the demonstrator never visited <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. Physical Intelligence states the operational version of the same problem: it is relatively easy to get a VLA to succeed at a task some of the time, and quite hard to make it succeed reliably <Cite id="pistar06-blog-2025" />. Reinforcement learning is the only mechanism in the toolbox that learns from failure as well as from success. The obstacle is cost: naive online RL on a multi-billion-parameter VLA, on real hardware, at control rates, is infeasible. Everything in this module is a way around that constraint, and by 2026 the field has converged on a small set of recipes that demonstrably work.

## The flow-matching log-likelihood problem

Modern policies emit actions through diffusion or <Term id="flow-matching">flow-matching</Term> heads, and that choice breaks the standard policy-gradient machinery. <Term id="ppo">PPO</Term>-style updates need the log-likelihood of the action the policy actually took; for a flow-matching head the "action" is the endpoint of an ODE integration, and its likelihood under the model is intractable. For two years this was the standard argument for why RL could not touch the frontier VLAs directly. Three methods dissolve it in different ways <Cite id="dppo-2024" /> <Cite id="conrft-2025" /> <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

**DPPO** (Ren et al., 2024) embeds the diffusion denoising process inside the environment MDP. Each policy action in this combined MDP is a denoising transition with an analytically evaluable Gaussian likelihood; the environment advances and supplies reward only after the final denoising step. PPO updates the denoising policy while the environment dynamics stay fixed. Environment-discounted advantages are multiplied by a separate denoising discount that downweights earlier, noisier steps. The value function depends on the environment state. The paper also fine-tunes only the last selected denoising steps, using a frozen original network for earlier steps and a trainable copy for the selected steps <Cite id="dppo-2024" />.

The comparisons are specific to their benchmarks and protocols. DPPO is the strongest performer among the compared diffusion-based RL methods on the paper's state-input Robomimic tasks; the PPO comparisons also cover Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture policies with different network architectures. Some Gym baselines are competitive, off-policy methods are more sample-efficient on HalfCheetah, and the paper reports slower wall-clock iterations than several baselines. The Gaussian baseline also slightly outperforms DPPO on the low-randomness Lamp task <Cite id="dppo-2024" />.

Its physical demonstration is zero-shot transfer of a simulation-trained One-leg assembly policy to a Franka Emika Panda; the hardware run involves no on-robot RL fine-tuning. At 10 Hz, DPPO succeeds in 16 of 20 hardware trials. The simulation comparisons, this small physical evaluation, and wall-clock time per training iteration measure different things <Cite id="dppo-2024" />.

**ConRFT** (Chen et al., 2025) fine-tunes a consistency-policy action head while keeping the visual encoders and transformer backbone frozen. The main eight-task study uses Octo-small on a 7-DoF Franka Emika arm at 10 Hz. Offline Cal-ConRFT combines a consistency-based <Term id="behavior-cloning">behavior cloning</Term> loss and a negative-Q actor loss with a Cal-QL critic, starting from 20-30 demonstrations per task. Online HIL-ConRFT keeps both actor losses, changes their weights, and uses a standard temporal-difference critic loss. Training batches sample equally from the demonstration and online replay buffers; human takeover transitions enter the demonstration buffer <Cite id="conrft-2025" />.

Keep training noise levels and inference steps separate. The consistency policy's training-noise grid specifies 40 sub-intervals; the separate five-step setting describes the diffusion-policy baseline, and the paper reports no measured ConRFT inference-call count <Cite id="conrft-2025" />.

The paper reports 96.3% mean <Term id="success-rate">success rate</Term> across eight tasks, with 20 trials per task. The listed online training times range from 15 to 90 minutes, with a reported mean of 48.8 minutes; the abstract and results prose instead state 45-90 minutes. Training time includes scripted motions, policy rollouts and onboard computation on an NVIDIA RTX A6000. The reported 144% is a rounded relative gain over the corresponding offline Cal-ConRFT mean of 39.4%. The separate SFT baseline happens to have the same reported mean <Cite id="conrft-2025" />.

Keep the intervention caveats with that comparison. The online learning curves can include successes produced by human takeovers, so they are not autonomous-policy success measurements. The paper describes every compared method as using human interventions, yet its PA-RL discussion presents PA-RL without them; the two accounts disagree. These are the authors' task-specific results, and the paper also warns about reward-classifier hacking and limited generalization <Cite id="conrft-2025" />.

**pi_RL** (Chen et al., 2026) fine-tunes flow-based policies with online PPO. **Flow-Noise** learns Gaussian noise for denoising transitions jointly with the velocity network, then uses the joint likelihood of the denoising path. This is not an exact marginal likelihood for the final executed action. The noise network is discarded for deterministic inference. **Flow-SDE** uses Gaussian transition likelihoods in a two-layer denoising/environment MDP; its hybrid sampler makes one randomly chosen denoising step stochastic and leaves the others as deterministic ODE updates. The PPO objective is distinct from the conditional flow-matching regression used in supervised training <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

The main experiments use pi0 and pi0.5. One configuration fine-tunes the full model with supervision, then freezes the VLM and trains a 300M-parameter action expert, on eight NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs; separate studies compare PPO with GRPO, add VLM LoRA, and evaluate PPO with Flow-SDE on GR00T N1.5. Each is its own evaluated configuration <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

The ID comparisons cover LIBERO, ManiSkill, MetaWorld and CALVIN. LIBERO reports 500 initial states per task suite; the CALVIN Scene D evaluation uses 1,000 five-subtask episodes. ManiSkill tests visual, semantic and execution shifts. Gains under distribution shifts do not consistently extend to the five unseen tasks in MetaWorld ML45, which trains on 45 tasks <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

Two setup descriptions disagree inside the paper itself. One puts pi0.5's proprioceptive state into the VLM; another omits state for its pi0.5 setting. A second conflict describes CALVIN ABC-to-D training while its results also report D-to-D. Neither conflict settles the input or OOD training protocol <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

RL training is simulated. A separate Real2Sim2Real case uses 20 motion-planner trajectories for supervised fine-tuning and 100 RL iterations, then transfers zero-shot to a physical Franka Panda with a RealSense D435 camera. The real-world case reports 40% real-world success without stating an evaluation-trial denominator. This is a separate transfer test; the on-robot RL training and any general reliability guarantee are absent from it <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />.

## Recap: advantage-conditioned policy learning

<Cite id="pistar06-2025" /> Recap, the method behind π*0.6, combines demonstrations, expert teleoperation corrections during rollouts, and autonomous experience. The training sequence is offline-RL pre-training, demonstration fine-tuning for a task, then batch collection and retraining; updates arrive in batch passes.

The language-conditioned distributional value function predicts negative remaining steps for successful episodes and a large negative value for failures, with per-task normalization. For post-training, the advantage estimate uses rewards over the next N = 50 steps plus the later value minus the current value. Pre-training uses a separate episode-return estimator. A task-dependent threshold turns the estimate into a binary indicator, and human corrections are explicitly assigned the positive indicator <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

The text input “Advantage: positive” or “Advantage: negative” appears after the predicted subtask and before the actions. Both good and bad data can train the policy. The default evaluation uses the positive-conditioned policy; indicator dropout during training also permits conditional/unconditional classifier-free guidance. A raw increase in value is only one input to the advantage estimator and its threshold rule <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

The relevant KL-regularized objective has the exponential solution:

$$
\hat{\pi}(a \mid o) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid o)\,\exp\!\left(A^{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(o,a)/\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\right).
$$

Recap's practical binary-conditioning method is motivated by a related improvement-indicator construction; the paper claims no exact implementation of that exponential weighting. Its practical form writes the improved policy as

$$
\hat{\pi}(a \mid o,\ell) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid o,\ell)
\left(\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid I,o,\ell)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid o,\ell)}\right)^{\beta_{\mathrm{CFG}}}.
$$

At $\beta_{\mathrm{CFG}}=1$, this reduces to the indicator-conditioned policy. The distinct subscripts separate the paper's two uses of β, and the two constructions are related without being identical <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

This avoids a PPO-style policy-extraction objective while still training the action expert through its flow-matching loss. The actual objective combines discrete next-token prediction with a continuous flow-matching loss. Knowledge Insulation stops the action expert's gradient at the VLM-backbone interface; the action expert itself is trained <Cite id="pistar06-2025" /> <Cite id="pi06-model-card-2025" />.

The scrubber is a deterministic teaching example. Its forty-second timeline, twenty-second credit-assignment arc, arbitrary positive values and stage-difference tags are chosen for illustration. Real Recap uses reward-inclusive estimates, task-dependent thresholds and a failure penalty. The execution view illustrates the requested conditioning and carries no guarantee that low-tag behaviors disappear at execution.

Recap's on-robot experience more than doubles successful completions per hour for double-shot espresso and the diverse-laundry evaluation, relative to offline RL plus task-specific demonstration fine-tuning. These are results on the report's static bimanual setup, with a button-up-shirt test in place of the full 11-item-type laundry training set <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. The [pi-line evaluation details](/manipulation/pi-line/) give the hardware, task protocols and time limits.

The paper qualifies its reliability claims: one passage describes failure reduction as “about a factor of two” and another says “more than 2×.” Its “90%+” success summary excludes diverse laundry, and the box chart reports separate stages. End-to-end success above 90% for every application is unestablished <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. See the [task-by-task qualifications](/manipulation/pi-line/).

The companion blog separately says that espresso throughput and success rate more than doubled after adding on-robot experience. Its demonstration-video caption gives 5:30am to 11:30pm for making various espresso drinks, while the report's introduction says “13 hours straight.” These are different duration accounts, and neither supplies a denominator for the quantitative double-espresso result; merging them into one continuous-run success statistic would invent data <Cite id="pistar06-blog-2025" /> <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

These are Physical Intelligence's own reported results; no independent replication exists yet. The report describes human reward labeling, interventions and episode resets, and batch offline updates. Intervention use varies by task: the T-shirt/shorts improvement experiment uses autonomous evaluation data without expert corrections <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

## HIL-SERL: a human in the loop, RL on the robot

HIL-SERL (Luo et al., 2024) combines vision-based off-policy RL built on RLPD with demonstrations and human corrections. The learner samples equally from demonstration and online replay buffers. Intervention transitions enter both buffers; autonomous policy transitions enter only the RL buffer. Training typically starts with 20 to 30 demonstrations. Jenga whipping is an exception to online correction: it starts with 30 demonstrations and does not use real-time human takeovers <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

In evaluation, HIL-SERL completes 100% of observed trials, at 100 evaluation trials per task, except IKEA whole assembly, which uses 10 trials and allows at most two attempts per sub-policy. Training takes 1 to 2.5 hours for nearly all tasks, but timing-belt assembly takes 6 hours. The reported time includes scripted motion, policy rollouts, intended stops and onboard computation on one RTX 4090. Against the imitation baseline the evaluation reports average success of 49.7% for the imitation policy and 100% for HIL-SERL, about twice the success rate, and average cycle times of 9.6 versus 5.4 seconds, described as 1.8× faster. These averages compare against HG-DAgger with matched episodes and interventions, except for flat BC on Jenga whipping and object flipping, trained on 50 and 200 demonstrations respectively. A separate comparison with 200 demonstrations covers DP, BC, IBRL, Residual RL and DAPG; HIL-SERL's own runs start from 20 to 30 <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

The online learning curves can include human-assisted successes, a different measurement from the 100-trial evaluation. A baseline inconsistency also remains: the paper gives DP 18% on dashboard assembly in one place and 28% in another; neither value is silently chosen as definitive <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

Luo and colleagues did not test generalization in unstructured environments or perform extensive randomization, and they leave substantially longer-horizon tasks uncertain. The paper reports code availability. The [RL for Robotics module](/rl-sim2real/rl-for-robotics) covers the off-policy context <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

## Residual RL and distillation back into the generalist

**PLD** (Probe, Learn, Distill; Xiao et al., 2025) freezes a VLA while training task-specific residual actors with off-policy RL. A hybrid collection stage first rolls out the base policy, then lets a residual specialist recover from the states it reaches. The resulting trajectories fine-tune the generalist through SFT. The base stays frozen only during specialist training: distillation fine-tunes it, and residual exploration carries no safety guarantee <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

The reported averages are 99.2% success for the model labelled OpenVLA, versus 91.8% for its baseline; pi0 reaches 97.2% versus 93.4%. These results cover LIBERO-Spatial, Object and Goal, ten tasks per suite, with 50 evaluation episodes per task; the rest of LIBERO sits outside them. On a separate four-task SimplerEnv suite, Octo-SFT improves from 71.8% to 96.6% on average. The WidowX carrot task rises from 43.3% to 93.9%, a 50.6-percentage-point gain. The printed average delta is +24.9, although its displayed means differ by 24.8 points; both readings stay far below a benchmark-wide relative gain above 50% <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

The Franka experiment starts with 200 teleoperated trajectories. After RL data generation and distillation, PLD records 30/30 randomized trials each for cube pick-up and peg insertion; peg insertion also reaches 30/30 with the comparison data sources. Under changed clutter and object conditions, PLD records 28/30 for a blue cube and 20/30 for a red cube <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

The abstract's 100% real-robot headline needs a further qualification for YAM. The detailed experiment describes four GPU insertion/removal stages, at most eight hours of training per subtask, and distillation into one BC policy. It reports at least one hour of continuous operation with recovery, while explicitly stating that per-stage one-shot success is not 100%. Human setup is present: the Franka peg experiment uses human hole repositioning. These results support a bounded data-generation recipe; universal self-improvement and safety guarantees remain unproven <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

**RLDG** (Xu et al., 2024) uses task-specific RL policies to generate data for fine-tuning generalists, so the generalist itself never receives RL updates. The paper implements its specialists with HIL-SERL and evaluates fine-tuned OpenVLA and Octo. Its comparisons match the task setup, training configuration, observation and action spaces, and number of successful training episodes, changing the data source. In the FMB single-object assembly experiment, RL supplies the insertion-stage data while human demonstrations still supply grasping and transport. OpenVLA succeeds in 20/20 trials with this mixed dataset versus 12/20 with human demonstrations: 100% versus 60%, a 40-percentage-point gain <Cite id="rldg-2024" />.

Xu and colleagues' FMB-insertion ablation relabels human-data actions with an RL policy. Its results suggest that both action quality and state distribution contribute, with action quality accounting for most of the benefit. The method still requires task rewards, and the authors report that speed-optimized RL data can produce premature object drops after distillation <Cite id="rldg-2024" />.

The paper's OpenVLA setup commands actions at 4 Hz; data collection, RL and Octo use 10 Hz. The separate low-level impedance controller runs at 1 kHz. These are setup-specific rates <Cite id="rldg-2024" />.

## EXPO-FT: value-guided edits on a frontier VLA

Perry Dong, Kuo-Han Hung, Tian Gao, Dorsa Sadigh and Chelsea Finn (Stanford) attack the problem from the value side. Their EXPO algorithm trains a large flow or diffusion base policy with a stable imitation objective, plus a lightweight Gaussian edit policy that nudges sampled actions toward higher value; an on-the-fly policy then executes and backs up the value-maximizing candidate among the base and edited actions, sidestepping gradient propagation through the denoising chain. The paper reports up to 2-3x sample-efficiency improvements on average over prior methods, both when fine-tuning a pretrained policy from offline data and when training online from it <Cite id="expo-2025" />.

EXPO-FT turns that algorithm into a full fine-tuning system for a frontier VLA <Cite id="expo-ft-2026" />. It extends EXPO to the action chunks VLAs emit natively, adds human teleoperation corrections during online training, and runs on π0.5 initialized from a task-specific supervised-finetuning checkpoint. Tasks the base policy cannot already do zero-shot receive imitation fine-tuning to around 40% success before online RL starts. Rewards are sparse and binary, from rule-based success detectors the paper reports at over 95% accuracy, and the robot takes end-effector velocity commands at 10 Hz.

The headline result: 30/30 successes on every evaluated task, within an average of 19.1 minutes of online robot data across the eight task variants <Cite id="expo-ft-2026" />. The chart shows the paper's own four-task comparison against supervised finetuning, HG-DAgger, DSRL and HIL-SERL.

<ExpoFtResults className="my-6" />

Read the HIL-SERL column with the paper's own caveat: HIL-SERL is highly reliable in its original evaluations, and this suite randomizes a substantially larger initial-state space; the standard training budget was too small for learning to even start on Cube Pick and Pool Shot. With extra samples, HIL-SERL reaches 27/30 and 13/30 on those two tasks. A 5.5/30 average under this protocol is evidence about initial-state breadth, and the original HIL-SERL results above stand on their own protocol <Cite id="expo-ft-2026" /> <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

DSRL represents a third position in the same comparison: keep the base policy frozen entirely and run RL over its latent-noise space, with black-box access and no weight updates <Cite id="dsrl-2025" />. Its authors demonstrate real-world autonomous improvement of diffusion policies and pretrained generalists this way. Dong and Finn argue the approach has a ceiling: “If the skill a robot needs isn't already somewhere in what the base model learned during pretraining, no amount of noise-searching will produce the action.” In their four-task comparison, DSRL averages 19/30 <Cite id="perry-dong-post-training-2026" /> <Cite id="expo-ft-2026" />.

Dong and Finn's September 2026 position, stated in a joint blog post, is that the field is at its GPT-2 moment: capable demos from pretrained VLAs, and no shared post-training recipe behind them. In their words, “RL shows incredible promise in training frontier robotics models, but it is not yet a recipe. It's a craft.” They argue robotics needs what language modeling has: a stable algorithm plus standard protocols for reward specification, resets, human input, hyperparameters and initialization. They attach their own limits to EXPO-FT: a human still defines success, resets the scene and intervenes; longer horizons strain the value function; and “Nineteen minutes of robot interaction is not nineteen minutes of post-training,” because gradient updates on a model this size dominate wall-clock time <Cite id="perry-dong-post-training-2026" />.

A September 2026 follow-up from the same group, Real-Time EXPO-FT, addresses the latency side: a slow VLA proposes action chunks while a lightweight edit policy reacts to the latest observation, and RL fine-tuning meets real-time control requirements. On the Kinetix benchmark a delayed policy trained this way beats every delayed and non-delayed competitor in 10 of 10 environments; on four dynamic real-world tasks with online data capped at 10 minutes, average performance rises from 42% to 97% with no human intervention. The [real-time execution module](/manipulation/realtime-execution/) covers the latency budgets this builds on <Cite id="realtime-expo-ft-2026" />.

## What RL actually buys, and the caveats

Liu et al.'s NeurIPS 2025 study reports that PPO beat the tested DPO/TPO and GRPO implementations in its OpenVLA pick-and-place setup, where preferences and returns came from sparse task rewards. The authors summarize the main OOD comparison with supervised fine-tuning as stronger semantic and execution generalization, with comparable visual robustness; arbitrary new-task generalization sits outside the test. The January 2026 revision adds a preliminary sim-to-real trial and an articulated-object task to a narrower main-text limitation statement. Its results are mixed: SFT wins on the articulated-task visual average and rephrased instructions, and on the action-chunking multi-receptacle test <Cite id="rl-vla-generalization-2025" />.

Peng et al.'s PAIR-VLA study addresses a related limitation in how RL treats visual changes. In simulated ManiSkill3 pick-and-place experiments with OpenVLA and π0.5, it adds paired-view invariance and sensitivity objectives to PPO: preserve action distributions for task-preserving changes and separate them for target-pose changes. The authors argue that task rewards alone give limited guidance about this distinction and report improved OOD success over standard PPO under the tested visual shifts. The sensitivity-only ablation loses to PPO on target-pose success; the separate camera-view experiment, using a different task-preserving view construction, also trails PPO slightly in-distribution despite its OOD gains. That comparison leaves reward design unisolated as the cause of a pre-to-post-RL loss of robustness; its task-preserving views also rely on simulator object masks, and real-world transfer remains untested <Cite id="pair-vla-2026" />.
