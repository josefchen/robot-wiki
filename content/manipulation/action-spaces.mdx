An action space is the interface between a policy and the rest of the robot. It decides what the network predicts, which errors the low-level controller must absorb, how frequently inference must run, and whether data from two embodiments can share labels. Calling both outputs “actions” hides differences large enough to reverse a model comparison.

The useful question is not which action space is best. It is which responsibilities should live in the learner and which should remain in a controller with explicit geometry, dynamics and safety limits.

## The contract has four parts

An action is more than a vector. A complete action contract specifies:

1. **Coordinates:** joint, Cartesian, object-relative or another frame.
2. **Semantics:** absolute target, delta, velocity, force, torque or controller parameter.
3. **Timing:** control rate, prediction horizon and how stale predictions are handled.
4. **Execution:** interpolation, clipping, inverse kinematics, feedback control and safety limits.

Two papers can both report a seven-dimensional action while meaning different things. One may emit a relative end-effector delta integrated by a task-space controller; another may emit absolute joint targets under a separate low-level loop. The output widths match; the learning problems do not.

## Joint-space actions

**Joint position targets** are one imitation-learning baseline. On matched leader-follower setups, demonstrated joint angles can serve as labels after checking calibration and gripper conventions. ACT uses absolute joint-and-gripper targets for its two ViperX arms, predicted in chunks <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />.

The advantage is repeatability on one mechanism. The controller can interpolate targets, enforce joint limits and close a high-rate loop below the policy. The cost is embodiment specificity. A shoulder angle on one arm does not describe the same end-effector motion on another.

**Joint velocity targets** encode increments instead of absolute configurations. Integrating them requires current joint state and can drift; equal velocities can produce different Cartesian motion on different robots.

Both representations require an accurate kinematic model. The [kinematics module](/classical/kinematics) derives the forward map and Jacobian that connect joint motion to the hand <Cite id="modern-robotics-2017" />.

## Cartesian actions

Cartesian actions describe what the tool should do: translate, rotate, open or close. Relative deltas encode motion from the current pose instead of an absolute target; their range and frame still need to be specified. They also provide a plausible shared label for arms whose joint counts and link lengths differ.

The simplicity is above the waterline. Underneath, each robot needs inverse kinematics or an operational-space controller, singularity handling, joint-limit avoidance and a named reference frame. Khatib's 1987 IEEE Journal of Robotics and Automation paper, "A Unified Approach for Motion and Force Control of Robot Manipulators: The Operational Space Formulation," frames motion and force control as one problem and gives the operational-space formulation its name <Cite id="khatib-1987" />.

Rotation needs special care. Euler-angle deltas have discontinuities and depend on axis order. Axis-angle and exponential coordinates are compact but must define whether the increment is expressed in the tool or world frame. A six-number action is not portable until that convention is written down.

NVIDIA says GR00T N1.7 adopts relative end-effector deltas shared across robot and human embodiments <Cite id="isaac-gr00t-repo-2026" />. That is a representation choice with consequences beyond preprocessing: it determines which non-robot data can become a useful target.

## Torque and impedance actions

**Torque actions** expose low-level dynamics and contact directly; position and impedance controllers can express related behaviors through their own feedback loops. They also demand a high control rate, accurate timing, actuator-specific scaling and a safety layer that remains effective when the learned output is wrong. Franka's control interface documentation states that libfranka can send real-time control values at 1 kHz using five different control modes, including gravity- and friction-compensated joint-level torque commands <Cite id="franka-fci-docs" />.

**Impedance actions** are a useful middle layer. The policy selects a pose target plus stiffness and damping, while a controller converts the error into forces. Variable end-effector impedance has been studied explicitly as an RL action space for contact-rich tasks <Cite id="martin-martin-2019" />. It gives learning control over compliance without asking it to synthesize every torque sample.

One design keeps compliance targets in the learned policy while a separately tested controller owns the fast feedback.

## Chunks change the time axis

An <Term id="action-chunking">action chunk</Term> predicts several future steps per inference. In ACT's ablations, chunking improved ACT and the two baselines the authors retrofitted with it, and the CVAE objective proved essential on human demonstrations: removing it cut success on the two simulated human-data tasks from 35.3% to 2% <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. Diffusion Policy also predicts a horizon and executes only the first portion before replanning <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />.

Chunks can represent temporally coordinated motion, but their inference cost depends on how often the policy replans. Longer open-loop execution can leave a stale plan when the scene changes. If contact makes the world diverge after the first few steps, the remaining chunk reflects an observation that is no longer current. Receding-horizon execution, temporal ensembling and real-time chunk replacement <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" /> are different answers to that problem.

Report the chunk horizon and the executed horizon separately. A predicted chunk length alone does not tell the reader whether all actions run open loop or when the policy replans.

## Tokens change the output geometry

RT-2 <Cite id="rt2-2023" /> and OpenVLA mapped continuous action dimensions into discrete vocabulary tokens so robot control could use language-model training machinery. OpenVLA discretizes seven action dimensions into 256 bins <Cite id="openvla-2024" />. Discretization permits token-level cross-entropy and imposes finite-bin resolution; autoregressive decoding must be budgeted in the latency measurement.

FAST takes the opposite route: transform an action chunk into frequency coefficients, quantize them, then apply byte-pair encoding <Cite id="pi0-fast-2025" />. Its universal FAST+ tokenizer was trained on 1M real robot action trajectories <Cite id="pi0-fast-2025" />. The paper reports that this DCT-plus-BPE scheme compresses action chunks significantly better than existing action tokenizers, but the decoded result is still meaningful only under the action convention used before tokenization.

Tokenization is therefore not an action space by itself. It is an encoding layered on joint, Cartesian or another continuous action space.

## A selection rule

Choose the lowest-level action that the task requires and the platform can support safely.

Hold the controller, rate, horizon and observation history fixed when comparing policy architectures. Otherwise the experiment measures a package of interface changes. The action space is the package's most consequential part because it decides which errors learning must solve and which errors classical control is still allowed to reject.
