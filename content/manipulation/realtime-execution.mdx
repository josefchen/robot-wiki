Real-time execution is delivering a policy's actions on the controller's clock while the next inference runs. At 50 Hz the control period is 20 ms, shared by cameras, network, preprocessing and model, and the hand-off between action chunks changes what the robot does <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. Slide the model-size control and the missed-deadline count follows the new inference time.

## Where the milliseconds go

A 60 ms inference cannot fit in a 20 ms tick, so the current chunk supplies the 50 Hz commands <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. Gemini Robotics pairs a cloud backbone with a local decoder: about 250 ms end to end, yet 50 Hz effective control through multi-action chunks <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2025" />.

The <Term id="real-time-chunking">Real-Time Chunking</Term> (RTC) paper measures π0.5 on an RTX 4090 in bfloat16, with five denoising steps and a wired LAN. Over 50 inference calls in a real episode, mobile latency totals 138.98 ± 6.71 ms (one standard deviation): model 96.89 ± 0.16, network 21.20 ± 3.12, image resizing 11.22 ± 5.00 and other work 9.67 ± 3.20 ms. Non-mobile total is 108.76 ± 2.34 ms. Resizing runs on an Intel i7-1260P NUC (mobile) or an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X workstation <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. GR00T N1 samples a 16-action chunk in 63.9 ms on an L40 in bf16 <Cite id="gr00t-n1-2025" />.

## The hardware wall

VLA-Perf (Jiang et al., 2026) is an analytical roofline model assuming optimistic software, adequate model accuracy and attainable peak compute and memory bandwidth. An optimized π0 Triton implementation on an RTX 4090 reaches 73.3 to 82.6% of its predicted throughput with one to three cameras, 10 flow-matching steps, chunk size 63 and an empty prompt <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />.

Its baseline π0 has 2.7B parameters: a 0.4B vision encoder, 2B VLM and 0.3B action expert. It models three 224×224 cameras, 32 language tokens (800 input tokens), 14 action dimensions, chunk size 50, 10 denoising steps, batch size one, and BF16 or FP16. Excluding network, sensor and execution latency, it predicts 162.5 inferences per second on H100 and 19.0 (52.57 ms) on Jetson Thor. A hypothetical 9.1B π0-L (1.1B SigLIP-Giant, 6.5B Llama2, 1.5B action expert) reaches 3.9 on Thor <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />.

On B100, raising denoising steps from 10 to 50 at chunk size 50 multiplies action-expert latency by 5 and total by 2.15. Growing the chunk from 50 to 250 at 10 steps adds 40% to expert and 11% to total latency. All three π0 components are memory-bound on Thor; on the other GPUs only the action expert is, and latency grows about linearly with parameter count <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />.

VLA-Perf calls 10 Hz inference "acceptable" and 100 Hz "high-performance" relative to common camera rates. Thor's predicted 19 Hz clears the first; the authors propose smaller models, fewer denoising steps or lower-precision quantization for the second <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />. NVIDIA's Jetson AI Lab measured π0.5 (action horizon 10) on Jetson AGX Thor: about 132 ms in PyTorch BF16, 54 ms with TensorRT FP8 (2.4x) and 49 ms with FP8 plus NVFP4 (about 2.7x). That model and setup differ from VLA-Perf's <Cite id="jetson-ai-lab-openpi-thor" />. NVIDIA rates Thor at up to 2,070 FP4 teraflops within 130 W, and 7.5x the AI compute of Jetson Orin <Cite id="nvidia-jetson-thor-available-2025" />.

RT-2's 55B model ran at 1 to 3 Hz and its 5B version at about 5 Hz <Cite id="rt2-2023" />. The 7B OpenVLA runs at about 6 Hz on an RTX 4090 but 1.2 Hz on its evaluation A5000 <Cite id="openvla-2024" />. OpenVLA-OFT's parallel decoding of chunked continuous actions raised throughput 26x and average success from 76.5% to 97.1% <Cite id="openvla-oft-2025" />. On one A100 with three camera images, OpenVLA-OFT's authors measured 1.8 actions per second for OpenVLA, 77.9 for OpenVLA-OFT+, 291.6 for π0 and 432.8 for ACT <Cite id="openvla-oft-2025" />. In October 2025, Yunchao Ma and colleagues at Dexmal and StepFun ran π0 with two camera views in 27.3 ms on one RTX 4090, a 30 Hz frame rate, with action trajectories up to 480 Hz <Cite id="realtime-vla-2025" />. Fast-in-Slow, which embeds a fast action module in its VLM, reaches 117.7 Hz control with eight-action chunks <Cite id="fast-in-slow-2025" />.

In VLA-Perf, server placement depends on the network: synchronous π0 on an RTX 4090 takes 38.7 ms over WiFi 6 against Thor's 52.6 ms, but 55.7 ms over 5G; B100 over 5G takes 27.8 ms. B100 over Ethernet 10G with a fast cloud link takes 23.4 ms, and over 4G with a slow cloud link 273.4 ms. Modeled links (upload/download, base latency): WiFi 6 560/800 Mbps, 3.5 ms; 5G 80/500 Mbps, 10 ms; 4G 19/75 Mbps, 25 ms; fast and slow cloud 10 and 1 Gbps, 10 and 100 ms <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />.

## Three ways to hand off a chunk

<Term id="action-chunking">Chunking</Term> captures temporal dependencies in demonstrations at the cost of reactivity to unexpected states <Cite id="bidirectional-decoding-2024" />. Let inference take $d$ milliseconds during the current chunk.

Synchronous execution runs part of a chunk, pauses for inference, then resumes, the default in many prior works <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. π0 predicts 50 actions but executes 16 on its 20 Hz UR5e and Franka setups and 25 on the other 50 Hz robots <Cite id="pi0-2024" />. Physical Intelligence ran π0, π0-FAST and π0.5 synchronously before RTC <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />. The RTC paper links stop-start motion to distribution shift, and RTC still progressed faster with inference pauses removed from elapsed time <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

Naive switching jumps to the new chunk on arrival. That removes dead time but adds a discontinuity: the new chunk comes from an observation $d$ milliseconds old, and the command steps by the two chunks' disagreement in one tick <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

<Term id="temporal-ensembling">Temporal ensembling</Term>, ACT's 2023 method, queries the policy every timestep and averages its predictions for each timestep with weights $w_i=\exp(-mi)$, where $i=0$ is the oldest retained prediction, so positive $m$ favours older ones <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. Averaging predictions from different modes need not give a valid action. In RTC's π0.5 real-robot comparison, both variants triggered the robot's protective stop at +100 and +200 ms of injected latency <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

Real-Time Chunking inpaints the next chunk while the current one executes. Guidance weights are 1 on the first $d$ actions, which execute during inference, 0 on the non-overlapping tail, and decay across the rest of the overlap. It works on diffusion and flow policies without retraining <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" /> <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />. On six π0.5 real-robot tasks with five denoising steps, RTC's aggregate throughput did not degrade at +0, +100 or +200 ms of injected delay, and it lit matches precisely at total delays above 300 ms <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

VLASH rolls the robot state forward with the previous chunk and cuts reaction latency up to 11.8x against synchronous inference <Cite id="vlash-2025" />. FutureRTC also predicts the execution-time observation, without modifying the policy <Cite id="futurertc-2026" />. A2C2 adds a small per-step correction head and gains 23 and 7 percentage points of success over RTC across increasing delays and execution horizons <Cite id="a2c2-2025" />. Event-triggered inference sets the inference gap by scene change, averaging 95% real-world success, 55 points above the strongest baseline <Cite id="event-triggered-vla-2026" />. SmolVLA's asynchronous stack decouples prediction from execution <Cite id="smolvla-2025" />; on a local network with an RTX 4090 its round trips stay under 100 ms, and tasks finish about 2x faster <Cite id="lerobot-async-inference-2025" />.

## Training the tolerance in

Black et al. (2025) simulate the delay in training, which avoids inference-time inpainting's added latency and works better at higher delays <Cite id="training-time-rtc-2025" />. π0.7 trains with simulated delays of 0 to 12 timesteps, a maximum of 240 ms on a 50 Hz robot. It predicts 50 actions and executes 15 or 25, with UR5e robots at 20 Hz and the others at 50 Hz <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. Soft RTC's medium overlap window cuts high-delay action delta and jerk by 9.1% and 9.6% against hard training-time RTC, at near-naive runtime <Cite id="soft-rtc-2026" />. [Real-Time EXPO-FT](/manipulation/rl-finetuning/) learns a lightweight edit policy that reacts to the latest observation while a slow VLA proposes chunks. On four dynamic real tasks, average performance rose from 42% to 97% with at most 10 minutes of online data <Cite id="realtime-expo-ft-2026" />. NVIDIA uses test-time and train-time RTC for GR00T N1.6 on the Unitree G1 and bimanual YAM <Cite id="gr00t-n1-6-2025" />. Figure's Helix offsets its two systems' training inputs to match their deployed latency gap, with S1 holding a 200 Hz loop <Cite id="helix-2025" />.

In September 2026, Fanding Huang, Cheng Chi, Zhi Wang and colleagues reported ChunkTrust, which infers how many actions to execute before replanning from the policy's own outputs while it runs. Its training-free horizon selector combines the stability of each generated chunk with its continuity with the actions already executed. Over all 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks it raised π0.5's task-averaged success by 6.80 percentage points, and on four real household tasks it raised the mean normalized process score from 50.4% to 57.5% <Cite id="chunktrust-2026" />.

## Spending the step budget

π0 integrated its flow with 10 Euler steps per chunk <Cite id="pi0-2024" />. π0.6 cut that to 5, at 63 ms per chunk on one H100 with three cameras <Cite id="pi06-model-card-2025" />. π0.7 keeps 5 steps; its minimal variant takes 38 ms on one H100 with three cameras and training-time RTC <Cite id="pi07-2026" />.

Distillation removes most steps: One-Step Diffusion Policy raises action prediction from 1.5 Hz to 62 Hz <Cite id="one-step-diffusion-2024" />, and Consistency Policy is an order of magnitude faster than the fastest alternative at competitive success <Cite id="consistency-policy-2024" />. FASTER finds reaction time set by the time to first action and the execution horizon, and compresses the immediate reaction to a single denoising step in π0.5 and X-VLA <Cite id="faster-flow-vla-2026" />. For token-based VLAs, chunking lengthens decoding linearly; PD-VLA's parallel decoding recovers 2.52 times the execution frequency on a 7-DoF arm <Cite id="pd-vla-2025" />.
