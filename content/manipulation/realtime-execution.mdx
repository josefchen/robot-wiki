Real-time execution keeps a robot moving on its controller's clock while the policy computes its next actions. At 50 commands a second the robot gets a new one every 20 ms, a budget shared by cameras, network and model. How the robot switches from one planned stretch of actions to the next changes how it moves <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

## Where the milliseconds go

A large policy usually needs longer than one tick to think, so it plans ahead: it outputs an <Term id="action-chunking">action chunk</Term>, a short sequence of future commands, which the robot plays while the next is computed; a 60 ms inference spans three 20 ms ticks <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. Gemini Robotics takes about 250 ms end to end through a cloud backbone, yet controls at 50 Hz through chunks from a local decoder <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2025" />. GR00T N1 also splits the work: language at 10 Hz, actions at 120 Hz <Cite id="gr00t-n1-2025" />.

Old plans cost most when the world moves. In the VLALagBench simulation benchmark, 100 ms of latency nearly halved success on moving targets, and overlapping inference with execution recovered part of the loss <Cite id="vla-lagbench-2026" />. The model is only part of the delay. In a real episode the <Term id="real-time-chunking">Real-Time Chunking</Term> (RTC) team's mobile π0.5 <Cite id="pi05-2025" /> setup spent 96.89 ms per inference in the model, 21.20 ms on the network, 11.22 ms resizing images and 9.67 ms on other work <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

## Onboard computers are the bottleneck

NVIDIA's Jetson Thor <Cite id="jetson-thor-2026" />, on sale since August 2025, has up to 7.5 times the AI compute of the Orin before it <Cite id="nvidia-jetson-thor-available-2025" />, yet the VLA-Perf model, which calls 10 Hz "acceptable" and 100 Hz "high-performance", predicts π0 on Thor reaches only the first. Its predictions are upper bounds; an optimized π0 on a gaming GPU reached 73.3 to 82.6% of them <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />.

How a model generates actions matters more than its size. Timing 18 open VLAs on Thor, VLA-HWBench found size explained at most 15% of latency differences; models emitting long chunks in a few flow-matching steps kept up with control, and token-by-token decoders did not <Cite id="vla-hwbench-2026" />. FlashRT-PAI (October 2026) measured the official π0.5 at 129.17 ms compiled, and its 4-bit version kept 94.04% success on LIBERO-10 on Thor <Cite id="flashrt-pai-2026" />.

Offloading to a server helps only on a fast network. In VLA-Perf, π0 on a remote RTX 4090 takes 38.7 ms over WiFi 6 against Thor's 52.6 ms, but 55.7 ms over 5G <Cite id="vla-perf-2026" />. Physical Intelligence's API beta serves π models from the cloud, and some early partner deployments met connectivity problems <Cite id="pi-api-beta-2026" />.

## Four ways to switch chunks

Chunks keep motion consistent with the demonstrations but react late to the unexpected <Cite id="bidirectional-decoding-2024" />.

Synchronous execution, long the default, runs part of a chunk, stops for inference, then resumes <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. π0 plays 16 or 25 of each 50-action chunk, depending on the robot <Cite id="pi0-2024" />, and Physical Intelligence ran π0, π0-FAST <Cite id="pi0-fast-2025" /> and π0.5 this way before RTC <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />. The pauses create stop-start motion unlike the training data <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

Naive switching jumps to the new chunk on arrival, removing the pause but adding a jerk: the new chunk was planned from an observation one inference old, and the command leaps by the chunks' disagreement in one tick <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. <Term id="temporal-ensembling">Temporal ensembling</Term>, from ACT (2023), averages the overlapping predictions for each timestep, favoring older ones <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />, but the average of two different plans need not be a valid action. In RTC's real-robot comparison, both ensembling variants triggered the robot's protective stop at 100 and 200 ms of added latency <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

Real-Time Chunking computes the next chunk while one plays. It fixes the actions that will run during inference and fills in the rest, a technique called inpainting, and works on diffusion and flow policies without retraining <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" /> <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />. LeRobot ships an implementation <Cite id="lerobot-rtc-docs" />. On six π0.5 real-robot tasks its throughput held with up to 200 ms of added delay, and it lit matches precisely at total delays above 300 ms <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

## Correcting for the stale view

Later methods correct for the old view. VLASH rolls the robot's state forward to when the chunk will run, cutting reaction latency up to 11.8 times against synchronous inference <Cite id="vlash-2025" />, and A2C2 adds a small correction at every step, gaining 23 percentage points over RTC <Cite id="a2c2-2025" />. FutureRTC predicts what the camera will see at execution time <Cite id="futurertc-2026" />, and event-triggered inference replans when the scene changes <Cite id="event-triggered-vla-2026" />. SmolVLA's asynchronous stack <Cite id="smolvla-2025" /> finishes tasks about twice as fast on a local network <Cite id="lerobot-async-inference-2025" />. On the LIBERO-Dynamic benchmark, asynchronous replanning helped only when the queue of pending actions never ran empty <Cite id="libero-dynamic-2026" />.

ChunkTrust uses the policy's own outputs to decide how many actions to play before replanning. It raised π0.5's average success over all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks <Cite id="robotwin-2-2025" /> by 6.80 percentage points, and its mean process score on four real household tasks from 50.4% to 57.5% <Cite id="chunktrust-2026" />.

## Training the policy to expect delay

Black et al. (2025) simulate delays in training, which avoids inpainting's extra computation and works better at long delays <Cite id="training-time-rtc-2025" />. π0.7 trains with delays of 0 to 12 ticks, up to 240 ms <Cite id="pi07-2026" />, and Soft RTC cuts jerk at high delay by 9.6% against the hard version <Cite id="soft-rtc-2026" />. NVIDIA uses both test-time and train-time RTC for GR00T N1.6 <Cite id="gr00t-n1-6-2025" />. Figure's Helix offsets its two systems' training inputs to match their deployed latency gap <Cite id="helix-2025" />.

Stanford's Chronos Policy tells the model when each observation was captured and each action will run, and randomizes both in training. On simulated conveyor pick-and-place with delays up to 200 ms, it kept 76.4% success where Diffusion Policy <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" /> fell to 1.8%, over 450 episodes per condition, and on three real tasks with 10 or 20 trials per condition it scored highest under every timing <Cite id="chronos-policy-2026" />. [Real-Time EXPO-FT](/manipulation/rl-finetuning/) pairs a slow VLA with a quick edit policy that reacts to the newest view, raising four dynamic real tasks from 42% to 97% <Cite id="realtime-expo-ft-2026" />.

## Fewer denoising steps, faster chunks

Diffusion and flow policies refine each chunk over several steps. π0 used 10 steps per chunk <Cite id="pi0-2024" />, π0.6 cut that to 5 <Cite id="pi06-model-card-2025" />, and π0.7's minimal variant takes 38 ms on one H100 with three cameras <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. Distillation removes most of them: One-Step Diffusion Policy raises action prediction from 1.5 Hz to 62 Hz <Cite id="one-step-diffusion-2024" />, and Consistency Policy is an order of magnitude faster than the fastest alternative at competitive success <Cite id="consistency-policy-2024" />. FASTER compresses the immediate reaction into one denoising step <Cite id="faster-flow-vla-2026" />, and for token-based VLAs, PD-VLA's parallel decoding raises execution frequency 2.52 times <Cite id="pd-vla-2025" />.
