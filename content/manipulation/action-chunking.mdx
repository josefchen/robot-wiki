<Term id="behavior-cloning">Behavior cloning</Term> fails in closed loop for a precise reason: a policy with per-step error ε on the expert's state distribution can accumulate cost that grows quadratically with episode length, because each mistake carries the robot into states the expert never visited <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. Over a T-step episode the worst-case cost scales as

$$
\underbrace{O(\varepsilon T^2)}_{\text{behavior cloning}} \qquad \underbrace{O(\varepsilon T)}_{\text{DAgger}}
$$

where the second bound is what <Term id="dagger">DAgger</Term> buys by repeatedly relabeling the states the policy actually visits <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. <Term id="action-chunking">Action chunking</Term> attacks the same bound from a different side: it shrinks the effective horizon. Instead of predicting one action per inference, the policy predicts a sequence of k actions at once, so the number of sequential decisions in an episode drops by a factor of k.

Drag the chunk size slider to k=1 and then back to 100. That curve is ACT's own ablation: 1% success one action at a time, 44% at a hundred. Everything else in this module explains why those two numbers are so far apart.

## ACT: chunking with a CVAE

Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), introduced together with the ALOHA bimanual teleoperation hardware, wraps chunking in a <Term id="cvae">conditional variational autoencoder</Term> <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. During training, an encoder reads the demonstration's action sequence and produces a latent style variable. At test time the policy decodes a chunk conditioned on camera images, joint positions, and the latent clamped to zero.

The 14 action coordinates are six arm joints and one gripper coordinate per arm. The ACT hardware description names two six-DoF ViperX arms; the paper's “7+7=14 DoF” shorthand counts the gripper coordinates too, so it never means seven arm joints <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. Mobile ALOHA explicitly describes its 14 arm-action coordinates as including two continuous gripper actions <Cite id="mobile-aloha-2024" />.

The objective in the card follows ACT's implementation description: the experiments specify L1 reconstruction, while the printed algorithm uses MSE, with a weighted KL regularizer in both accounts <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. The paper is internally inconsistent here; the card pins the L1 choice for this implementation and claims nothing about a universal ACT objective.

### The chunk-size dial

Chunk size is a real bias/variance dial. The curve above comes from the paper's two simulated MuJoCo tasks (Cube Transfer and Bimanual Insertion), averaged over four settings (each task trained on scripted and on human demonstrations, temporal ensembling disabled): ACT measures 1% success at k=1, 44% at k=100, and a decline at k=200 and k=400 as the policy becomes effectively open-loop <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. Too small and <Term id="compounding-error">compounding error</Term> returns; too large and the policy stops reacting to the world mid-chunk.

<Callout variant="info">
  Chunking does not reduce the per-decision error ε. It reduces the number of decisions, T divided by k, over which that error compounds. The bias you pay is commitment: the world can change inside a chunk, and the policy will not notice until the next inference.
</Callout>

## Temporal ensembling and its limits

The pinned ACT reference implementation uses <Term id="temporal-ensembling">temporal ensembling</Term>: it queries the policy at every timestep and averages the retained predictions for the current action in oldest-to-newest order <Cite id="act-reference-2023" />. Its weights are $w_i = \exp(-m \cdot i)$, where $i = 0$ is the earliest retained prediction and $m = 0.01$. Thus the oldest retained prediction has the largest weight; increasing $m$ reduces the relative weight of newer predictions. The example below starts with already-selected predictions and illustrates the weighted average; the reference code's nonzero-buffer selection rule stays outside it.

<Image id="temporal-ensembling" />

```python title="temporal_ensembling.py"
# ACT reference convention: preds contains current-step predictions already
# selected and ordered oldest-to-newest. Index 0 is the oldest retained
# prediction, not "zero chunks ago". Reference m=0.01. This helper does not
# reproduce ACT's raw-buffer nonzero occupancy filter.
def temporal_ensemble(preds: list[np.ndarray], m: float = 0.01) -> np.ndarray:
    weights = np.exp(-m * np.arange(len(preds)))   # w_i = exp(-m * i)
    weights /= weights.sum()
    return np.tensordot(weights, np.stack(preds), axes=1)
```

For an illustrative scalar example **after selection**, oldest-to-newest predictions `[0, 10, 20]` at $m = 0.01$ receive normalized weights approximately `[0.33667217, 0.33332222, 0.33000561]` and yield `9.93333444`. This does not exercise the reference occupancy filter, which would discard a literal zero scalar in the raw buffer.

<Callout variant="warn">
  The RTC experiment used π0.5 with five denoising steps: model latency was 76 ms for the baselines and 97 ms for RTC, plus 10 to 20 ms over LAN. The tested temporal-ensembling variants triggered protective stops at **an additional 100 or 200 ms** of injected delay, measured on top of the existing model and network latency <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. The accompanying blog reports averages over six tasks, ten episodes per task; this is an experimental result and carries no universal failure threshold <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="error" title="Failure mode: neither left nor right">
  When two overlapping chunks commit to different modes, say left versus right around an obstacle, the weighted average can be an action that belongs to neither mode. The robot drifts between strategies instead of committing to one.
</Callout>

<Aside title="Why idle segments freeze single-step policies">
  Demonstrations contain pauses that are unpredictable from a Markovian single-step state. Both ACT and [Diffusion Policy](/manipulation/diffusion-policy) document robots freezing when the policy cannot represent the pause <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. Temporal context is the fix; the backbone's size is beside the point.
</Aside>

<Term id="real-time-chunking">Real-Time Chunking</Term> (RTC) reframes the hand-off between chunks as an inpainting problem on the flow or diffusion trajectory: actions that will already have executed are frozen, the overlapping middle is partially attended to, and the remainder is generated fresh <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. In the reported π0.5 experiment, RTC showed no degradation in average task throughput through +200 ms of injected delay, without retraining <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. This evaluates RTC on a <Term id="flow-matching">flow-matching</Term> policy; it does not establish adoption by later generations of [the pi line](/manipulation/pi-line).

The dial below is a deterministic toy of a cross-mode hand-off; it does not reproduce the RTC benchmark. The paper reports average task throughput, and these percentages are the toy's own; the slider also extends beyond the experiment’s +200 ms endpoint <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.

Octo's ALOHA finetuning example predicts 64 actions and executes 12 before replanning. The table's 20 Hz entry names the UR5e/Franka reference, while the paper describes operation at up to 50 Hz. Its continuous action outputs are trained with conditional flow matching.


<Cite id="rt1-2022" /> RT-1 predicts one robot action per timestep and commands the Everyday Robots mobile manipulator at 3 Hz. Each action dimension is mapped to one of 256 uniformly distributed bins within that variable’s bounds. Six input frames are observation history; the action horizon is a single step. The paper announces open code; pretrained-weight availability is not disclosed in that statement.

<Cite id="pi05-2025" /> The π0.5 paper predicts 50 action entries (inclusive indexing, H=49) and sends targets at 50 Hz in its two-mobile-platform setup. That does not establish how many actions execute before replanning. The paper combines FAST-token prediction with flow matching; the cited openpi README supports only the flow-matching head for π0.5 training and inference.

<Cite id="pi06-model-card-2025" /> The November 17, 2025 π0.6 model card describes a continuous-action flow-matching expert; FAST tokens supervise the VLM backbone during training. The card does not specify predicted or executed chunk length, robot-control rate, or model-specific weight-release and licensing terms. Its 63 ms action-chunk latency uses five denoising steps and three camera inputs on one H100; it is an inference latency, and it carries no control-frequency claim. The card’s openpi release statement concerns the π0.5 comparator; π0.6 sits outside it.

<Cite id="pi07-2026" /> In the π0.7 paper’s experiments, the continuous-action flow-matching expert predicts 50-step chunks with five denoising steps and executes either 15 or 25 steps. UR5e robots run at 20 Hz; the other tested robots run at 50 Hz. The paper does not assign either execution choice to a particular robot. FAST tokens supervise the backbone during training; the runtime action output is continuous. The model distills experience from RL-trained π*0.6, so evaluation without task-specific post-training still reflects RL-derived data from that distillation. Model-specific weight-release and licensing terms are not disclosed in this paper.

<Cite id="isaac-gr00t-repo-2026" /> The NVIDIA Isaac GR00T README describes the N1.7 GA release: a predicted horizon of 40, a flow-matching DiT head, relative-end-effector support, and downloadable weights. Relative EEF is not its only embodiment interface; it also describes SONIC latent-action output. No single robot-control frequency is disclosed there; inference throughput is a different quantity. Its overview says Apache 2.0, but its License section distinguishes Apache 2.0 code from weights under the NVIDIA Open Model License. Download availability does not resolve that licensing discrepancy.

<Cite id="helix-02-2026" /> Figure’s January 27, 2026 Helix 02 announcement reports S1 full-body joint targets at 200 Hz, tracked by S0 actuator commands at 1 kHz. These are separate layers, so the two rates answer different questions. Chunk length and weight-release/licensing terms are not disclosed in this announcement; neither absence establishes inapplicability or closed licensing.
