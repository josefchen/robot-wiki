<Term id="behavior-cloning">Behavior cloning</Term> fails in closed loop for a precise reason: a policy with per-step error ε on the expert's state distribution can accumulate cost that grows quadratically with episode length, because each mistake carries the robot into states the expert never visited <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. Over a T-step episode the worst-case cost scales as

$$
\underbrace{O(\varepsilon T^2)}_{\text{behavior cloning}} \qquad \underbrace{O(\varepsilon T)}_{\text{DAgger}}
$$

where the second bound is what <Term id="dagger">DAgger</Term> buys by repeatedly relabeling the states the policy actually visits <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. <Term id="action-chunking">Action chunking</Term> attacks the same bound from a different side: it shrinks the effective horizon. Instead of predicting one action per inference, the policy predicts a sequence of k actions at once, so the number of sequential decisions in an episode drops by a factor of k.

## ACT: chunking with a CVAE

Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), introduced together with the ALOHA bimanual teleoperation hardware, wraps chunking in a <Term id="cvae">conditional variational autoencoder</Term> <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. During training, an encoder reads the demonstration's action sequence and produces a latent style variable. At test time the policy decodes a chunk conditioned on camera images, joint positions, and the latent clamped to zero.

### The chunk-size dial

Chunk size is a real bias/variance dial, not a hyperparameter footnote. On the paper's two simulated MuJoCo tasks (Cube Transfer and Bimanual Insertion), averaged over four settings (each task trained on scripted and on human demonstrations, temporal ensembling disabled), ACT measures 1% success at k=1, 44% at k=100, and a decline at k=200 and k=400 as the policy becomes effectively open-loop <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" />. Too small and <Term id="compounding-error">compounding error</Term> returns; too large and the policy stops reacting to the world mid-chunk.

<Callout variant="info">
  Chunking does not reduce the per-decision error ε. It reduces the number of decisions, T divided by k, over which that error compounds. The bias you pay is commitment: the world can change inside a chunk, and the policy will not notice until the next inference.
</Callout>

## Temporal ensembling and its limits

ACT executes chunks with <Term id="temporal-ensembling">temporal ensembling</Term>: at each timestep, the actions predicted for that step by overlapping chunks are averaged with exponential weights $w_i = \exp(-m \cdot i)$, where $i = 0$ indexes the oldest prediction and $m$ (0.01 in the reference implementation) sets how quickly older predictions are discounted. This smooths chunk boundaries at the cost of one inference per step instead of one per chunk.

<Image id="temporal-ensembling" />

```python title="temporal_ensembling.py"
# Temporal ensembling (ACT, arXiv:2304.13705). preds[i] is the prediction
# for the current action made by the chunk issued i chunks ago, so index 0
# is the oldest prediction (the largest weight, per the paper's w_0).
def temporal_ensemble(preds: list[np.ndarray], m: float = 0.01) -> np.ndarray:
    weights = np.exp(-m * np.arange(len(preds)))   # w_i = exp(-m * i)
    weights /= weights.sum()
    return np.tensordot(weights, np.stack(preds), axes=1)
```

<Callout variant="warn">
  Temporal ensembling assumes inference is nearly free in wall-clock time. Once inference latency reaches 100 to 200 ms, the newest chunk in the average is always stale by a full round trip, and Physical Intelligence documents the scheme failing outright <Cite id="pi-real-time-chunking-blog-2025" />.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="error" title="Failure mode: neither left nor right">
  When two overlapping chunks commit to different modes, say left versus right around an obstacle, the weighted average can be an action that belongs to neither mode. The robot drifts between strategies instead of committing to one.
</Callout>

<Aside title="Why idle segments freeze single-step policies">
  Demonstrations contain pauses that are unpredictable from a Markovian single-step state. Both ACT and [Diffusion Policy](/manipulation/diffusion-policy) document robots freezing when the policy cannot represent the pause <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. Temporal context, not a bigger backbone, is what fixes it.
</Aside>

<Term id="real-time-chunking">Real-Time Chunking</Term> (RTC) reframes the hand-off between chunks as an inpainting problem on the flow or diffusion trajectory: actions that will already have executed are frozen, the overlapping middle is partially attended to, and the remainder is generated fresh <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />. RTC holds throughput flat out to +200 ms of injected inference delay with no training-time change, which is why the later <Term id="flow-matching">flow-matching</Term> policies of [the pi line](/manipulation/pi-line) adopt it over ensembling.

The dial below injects inference delay into both execution strategies. Temporal ensembling holds while inference stays fast, then the averaged action drifts off the committed mode and the strategy fails inside the documented 100 to 200 ms window. RTC freezes the actions that will already have executed and stays on the in-flight mode, so its throughput line does not move <Cite id="real-time-chunking-2025" />.
