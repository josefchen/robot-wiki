<Term id="behavior-cloning">Behavior cloning</Term> is the oldest way to make a robot imitate a person: record what an expert did, treat each observation as an input and the expert's action as the label, and fit a policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ by supervised learning. The idea predates deep learning. ALVINN (1988) used a three-layer back-propagation network with camera and laser-range inputs. It was trained on simulated road images and tested on NAVLAB, a modified Chevy van; training on real images while a human drives was proposed future work, not the reported training procedure <Cite id="alvinn-1988" />.

The appeal is obvious: no reward function to design, no exploration to manage, just regression onto demonstrations. The failure mode is equally specific, and nearly every architectural innovation in learned manipulation since 2023 is a partial answer to it. Supervised learning assumes that test inputs come from the same distribution as training inputs. A robot running in closed loop violates that assumption with its own actions.

## Covariate shift, stated precisely

Training data is drawn from the expert's state distribution $d_{\pi^*}$. At deployment the robot visits its own distribution $d_\pi$, and the two diverge the moment the policy makes its first mistake. A small error moves the robot into a state slightly outside the demonstrations; from that unfamiliar state the policy is more likely to err again; each error pushes it further out. Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell made this precise: a policy with per-step error $\varepsilon$ on the expert distribution can incur total cost growing quadratically in the episode length $T$, and they gave a matching lower bound showing the quadratic term is unavoidable under i.i.d. training <Cite id="dagger-2011" />.

For a task cost bounded in $[0,1]$ and a surrogate loss that upper-bounds imitation 0-1 loss, the two relevant statements are:

$$
J(\pi) \leq J(\pi^*) + T^2\varepsilon
$$

$$
J(\hat{\pi}) \leq J(\pi^*) + uT\varepsilon_N + O(1).
$$

The first uses error $\varepsilon$ on the expert distribution. The second is DAgger’s conditional, infinite-sample Theorem 3.2: after $N=\tilde{O}(uT)$ iterations there exists a policy $\hat{\pi}$ in the learned sequence satisfying the bound. Here $\varepsilon_N$ is the true loss of the best policy in hindsight across the collected mixed-policy state distributions, not the expert-distribution error above. The factor $u$ bounds the increase in expert cost-to-go after one action. Linear-in-$T$ excess cost requires $u$ independent of $T$; in the worst case $u$ can be $O(T)$. The loss and mixing assumptions are stated in the <Term id="dagger">DAgger</Term> section below <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. Physical Intelligence restated the same point in operational terms in 2025: it is relatively easy to get a learned policy to succeed at a task some of the time, and much harder to make it succeed reliably <Cite id="pistar06-blog-2025" />.

## Fitting demonstrations is hard before deployment even starts

<Term id="compounding-error">Compounding error</Term> is the deployment problem. Three training-side problems compound it:

**Multimodality.** Human demonstrations can contain several valid action strategies. <Term id="diffusion-policy">Diffusion Policy</Term> models multimodal action distributions: in its Push-T example it learns both modes, left-going and right-going, and commits to one mode within a rollout <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />.

**Temporally correlated confounders.** Demonstrations contain pauses and idle segments that are unpredictable from a single Markovian state. A single-step policy cannot tell "waiting" from "acting," and both ACT and Diffusion Policy document robots freezing in place because of it <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" /> <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. The fix is temporal context, not a larger backbone.

**The demonstration ceiling.** Behavior cloning can at best match the demonstrator. If the teleoperator is slow, cautious, or inconsistent, the policy inherits all of it, and the <Term id="covariate-shift">covariate-shift</Term> analysis above means it will usually do worse.

## DAgger: relabel the states you actually visit

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) attacks the quadratic bound directly <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. The loop:

      { key: 'query', value: 'the expert for the correct action at every visited state' },
      { key: 'aggregate', value: 'those state-action pairs into the training set' },

    ]}
  />
</Card>

DAgger reduces <Term id="imitation-learning">imitation learning</Term> to no-regret online learning. Its guarantee is existential over the learned sequence, not a promise that every iterate improves. Theorem 3.2 assumes a bounded, strongly convex surrogate loss that upper-bounds imitation 0-1 loss, with expert mixing $\beta_i \leq (1-\alpha)^{i-1}$ for a constant $\alpha$ independent of $T$. It uses the true best-policy-in-hindsight loss

$$
\begin{gathered}
\varepsilon_N = \min_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_i}}[\ell(s,\pi)]\\
\pi_i = \beta_i\pi^*+(1-\beta_i)\hat{\pi}_i.
\end{gathered}
$$

The analysis allows selection of the sequence policy with the lowest true loss under its own state distribution, or uniformly choosing a sequence policy once for an entire episode. Algorithm 3.1 returns the best policy on validation; it does not guarantee that the last iterate is best. Finite-sample Theorem 3.4 instead uses empirical training loss $\hat{\varepsilon}_N$: with $m=O(1)$ trajectories per iteration and $N=O(u^2T^2\log(1/\delta))$, the analogous bound holds for some sequence policy with probability at least $\left(1-\delta\right)$. On-policy coverage alone neither makes $u$ constant nor guarantees linear task cost <Cite id="dagger-2011" />.

The catch is the expert. Classic DAgger needs a supervisor who can look at an arbitrary mid-failure robot state and name the correct action, which is unnatural and slow for a human teleoperator. HG-DAgger (the retained arXiv v2 text) gives the human expert uninterrupted control when they judge intervention necessary. The expert guides the system back to a safe, stable region; action labels are collected during these recovery trajectories, and the expert manually returns control to the novice <Cite id="hg-dagger-2019" />. Fourteen years later, the same pattern shows up as the "coaching" stage of Physical Intelligence's Recap, where human corrections on visited states fine-tune a vision-language-action policy <Cite id="pistar06-blog-2025" />.

<Aside title="Why not just collect more demonstrations?">
  More i.i.d. data can reduce imitation error, but the worst-case guarantee still contains $T^2$. That does not predict quadratic realized cost for every task. DAgger changes where labels are collected; whether its excess-cost bound is linear also depends on the expert’s recovery cost $u$ and the learning assumptions.
</Aside>

## Where this leads

These results separate three questions: can a policy fit demonstrations, will it visit states covered by training, and can the expert recover after a mistake? [Action chunking](/manipulation/action-chunking) addresses temporal action structure. [Diffusion Policy](/manipulation/diffusion-policy) represents multiple action modes. DAgger collects expert labels on visited states. None of those mechanisms alone establishes an unconditional long-horizon task-cost guarantee.
