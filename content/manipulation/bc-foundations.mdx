<Term id="behavior-cloning">Behavior cloning</Term> teaches a robot by example: a person demonstrates a task, usually by remote-controlling the robot, and a neural network learns to map what the robot sensed to [what the person did](/how-robots-work/#puppeteer). It is the simplest form of <Term id="imitation-learning">imitation learning</Term>; its central weakness is that small mistakes <Term id="compounding-error">compound</Term> <Cite id="ross-bagnell-2010" />.

## Learning to drive by watching

ALVINN (1988) steered Carnegie Mellon's Navlab van with a neural network trained on simulated road images <Cite id="alvinn-1988" />, and by 1991 it learned to drive in under five minutes by watching a person <Cite id="pomerleau-1991" />. But a good driver stays centered, so the network never saw how to recover from drifting. Dean Pomerleau shifted each camera image in software into 14 off-center copies labeled with corrected steering <Cite id="pomerleau-1991" />. Later driving systems added rotated views <Cite id="bojarski-e2e-driving-2016" /> and, when 30 million examples proved too few, synthetic perturbations <Cite id="chauffeurnet-2018" />.

<Image id="navlab-1-5-cmu-2017" />

Robot arms use the same recipe: ACT, Diffusion Policy and large multitask behavior models all learn from teleoperated demonstrations <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" /> <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" /> <Cite id="tri-lbm-2025" />.

## Watch the error compound

Each slip moves the robot into situations, or states, that its training data did not cover, where the next slip is more likely, so small errors grow into large drift; labeling those states, as DAgger does, shrinks it <Cite id="dagger-2011" />.

## Covariate shift, stated precisely

Training data come from states the expert visits, but the robot acts in states it reaches itself, which drift further with each mistake <Cite id="dagger-2011" />. This mismatch is <Term id="covariate-shift">covariate shift</Term>, and Ross and Bagnell (2010) bounded its cost <Cite id="ross-bagnell-2010" />. If the cloned <Term id="policy">policy</Term> picks a different action from the expert's with probability $\varepsilon$ on the expert's own states, and each step costs between 0 and 1, its total cost over a $T$-step task can exceed the expert's by $T^2\varepsilon$, and the bound is tight <Cite id="dagger-2011" />:

$$
J(\pi) \leq J(\pi^*) + T^2\varepsilon
$$

Later theory cuts both ways. With log-loss training, the data needed can be independent of task length when payoffs and model complexity are controlled <Cite id="foster-bc-horizon-2024" />, and with labels counted per state a DAgger variant provably wins when recovery is cheap <Cite id="stagger-hybrid-il-2024" />. Yet in worst-case discrete problems a $T^2$ penalty survives even with expert queries <Cite id="rajaraman-il-limits-2020" />, and smooth deterministic imitators of continuous systems can do exponentially worse than their training error as tasks lengthen <Cite id="simchowitz-continuous-il-2025" />.

## What demonstrations get wrong

Some tasks have several right answers. In Push-T the gripper can go left or right around a block; Diffusion Policy learns both routes and commits to one per attempt <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />, and energy-based implicit policies, built for multi-valued functions, often beat mean-squared-error ones <Cite id="implicit-bc-2021" />, though Pan et al. (2025) credit generative policies' gains to iterative computation <Cite id="much-ado-noising-2025" />. Operators also pause, and a policy that sees only the current frame can learn to stall <Cite id="act-aloha-2023" /> <Cite id="diffusion-policy-2023" />. History helps but can backfire: more input can make a cloned policy worse under shift <Cite id="causal-confusion-2019" />, or let it copy the expert's previous action <Cite id="copycat-bc-2020" />.

Quality and variety matter more than volume. Behavior cloning at best matches its demonstrator, and results depend on demonstration quality and the training snapshot kept <Cite id="robomimic-2021" />. Past a threshold, more demonstrations per environment or object barely help, while more environments and objects do <Cite id="lin-data-scaling-laws-2024" />. Weighting the states where small errors cause failure helps <Cite id="scrim-state-criticality-2026" />, as does averaging recent weights to damp the swings that training noise causes <Cite id="butterfly-sgd-noise-2023" />. OCBench (Park and Levine, October 2026) reproduces two puzzles: success that keeps rising as a policy overfits, and step-by-step policies that fail almost completely on two tasks where action chunking works <Cite id="ocbench-2026" />.

## DAgger: relabel the states you actually visit

<Term id="dagger">DAgger</Term> (Dataset Aggregation, 2011) removes the mismatch at its source: the expert labels the states the learner itself reaches, which turns imitation into a standard online learning problem <Cite id="dagger-2011" />.

Under idealized assumptions (unlimited samples per round, a well-behaved loss, a shrinking expert share of control), one of its policies meets the bound below, where $\varepsilon_N$ is the best achievable error on visited states and $u$ bounds how much one wrong action can add to the cost of finishing. The penalty is linear in $T$ only while $u$ stays bounded <Cite id="dagger-2011" />.

$$
J(\hat{\pi}) \leq J(\pi^*) + uT\varepsilon_N + O(1)
$$

Recovery data can also be made without an expert: add noise to the demonstrator's commands <Cite id="dart-2017" />, generate off-course images <Cite id="diffusion-meets-dagger-2024" /> or nudge the operator toward the learner's likely mistakes <Cite id="data-inoculation-2026" />. DART gained 62% on average over behavior cloning at grasping in clutter <Cite id="dart-2017" />.

## Human corrections beat more demonstrations

Labeling odd states is unnatural for an operator, so in HG-DAgger the person takes over, steers the robot back to safety and hands back control; the recovery becomes training data <Cite id="hg-dagger-2019" />. BC-Z gathered such interventions and performed 24 unseen tasks at 44% average success <Cite id="bc-z-2022" />, and Compliant Residual DAgger takes small corrections without pausing the robot, raising base success by 64% on four tasks <Cite id="cr-dagger-2025" />.

Corrections beat equal effort on new demonstrations. I3L (Stanford, October 2026) beat demonstration-only training by 35.6 points on whole-body mobile manipulation <Cite id="i3l-2026" />. RaC found that imitation plateaus far below perfect despite thousands of demonstrations, and its correction data beat the prior best with 10 times less collection <Cite id="rac-2025" />. PHIL (November 2026) shows operators the planned motion so they step in early, and with five correction episodes lifted a diffusion policy's mean success from 44.0% under HG-DAgger to 70.3% <Cite id="phil-proactive-correction-2026" />.

The loop now feeds human corrections to vision-language-action models <Cite id="pistar06-blog-2025" /> <Cite id="pistar06-2025" /> <Cite id="sop-2026" />, and NVIDIA's Recova routes real rollouts and human fixes into DAgger training <Cite id="recova-2026" />. DAgger also distills simulation experts into camera-based students; in RebarSim, plain cloning in its place cut simulated success from 96.1% to 68.9% <Cite id="rebarsim-2026" />.

## Beyond DAgger

Other fixes change the policy or add a reward. [Action chunking](/manipulation/action-chunking/), predicting several moves at once, and exploratory data collection each provably avoid exponential compounding, thanks to the stability of the controlled system <Cite id="zhang-chunking-exploration-2025" />; a 2026 study credits chunking's benefit largely to past context and implicit ensembling <Cite id="lazzati-why-chunking-2026" />. A generative imitator such as a [diffusion policy](/manipulation/diffusion-policy/), run over a stabilizing controller, can match the expert's per-step distribution <Cite id="block-generative-bc-2023" />. GAIL's adversarial imitation <Cite id="gail-2016" /> and DARP's nearest-neighbour retrieval, 15 to 46% better than behavior cloning <Cite id="darp-2026" />, need no expert queries. HIL-SERL adds real-world reinforcement learning, reaching near-perfect success in 1 to 2.5 hours <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.
