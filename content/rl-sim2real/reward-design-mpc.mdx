Reward design is writing the objective that a reinforcement learning policy learns to maximize. Legged-locomotion rewards are weighted sums of terms whose weights are tuned by hand <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, and language models now write reward code <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. Model-predictive control instead plans actions online with an explicit dynamics model <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

This local teaching model uses twelve authored terms, weights and fixed per-term magnitudes. Its signed weighted total is dimensionless per step, not a measured reward. Freeze, prance and chatter are chosen classification rules and drawn poses, not trained policies, measured control frequencies or actuator-damage predictions.

## The reward is the product

Rudin and colleagues define a weighted sum of nine reward terms, including feet air time; the reward is gait-independent, prescribing no gait schedule <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-32"><Cite id="rudin-2021" /></span>. The initial `legged_gym` commit (October 2021) declares fifteen entries in `LeggedRobotCfg.rewards.scales`. Nine are nonzero: linear- and angular-velocity tracking, vertical linear velocity, roll/pitch angular velocity, joint torque, joint acceleration, feet air time, collisions and action rate. `_prepare_reward_function` drops zero scales and multiplies the rest by the time step. The joint-limit and contact-force functions among its nineteen reward functions are inactive in this config, and the zero-valued `feet_stumble` entry does not match the `_reward_stumble` function, which penalizes feet hitting vertical surfaces. The air-time term rewards long steps on first contact when the planar command norm exceeds 0.1 <Cite id="legged-gym-repo-2021" />.

Rudin and colleagues report artifacts such as a dragging leg and unreasonably high or low base heights, and tuned the reward weights before hardware transfer <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Margolis and Agrawal note that a policy failing in a new environment needs "a slow and iterative cycle of reward and environment redesign" <Cite id="walk-these-ways-2022" />. Rudin's <Term id="ppo">PPO</Term> adapts its learning rate from the KL divergence between new and old policies: with a target of 0.01, it lowers the rate when KL exceeds twice the target and raises it under half, within stated limits <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

## Three answers to weight tuning

The first answer adds constraints. Yunho Kim and colleagues use three rewards (command tracking, joint torque, action smoothness) and 11 constraints, six probabilistic and five average. They trained separate controllers in simulation for Raibo, Mini-cheetah, Hound, ANYmal B, ANYmal C, Unitree Go1 and Atlas, deploying on Raibo and Mini-cheetah. Across robots they adjusted only the torque coefficient among the rewards, found for the reference robot by trial and error, though constraint limits and PD gains still changed. Their IPO-based optimizer uses TRPO with adaptive constraint thresholds, and its probabilistic and average limits are not per-step hard bounds <Cite id="rewards-constraints-2024" />.

Dohyeong Kim and colleagues split acrobatic tasks into manual stages with their own rewards and costs. A back-flip has five stages, Stand-Sit-Jump-Air-Land, with five reward and five cost functions. CoMOPPO combines normalized reward and cost advantages, while stage-transition rules, cost thresholds, preferences and hyperparameters stay hand-set. Simulation covers Unitree Go1 and H1, and a real Go1 performs back-flips, side-rolls and two-hand walking <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-16"><Cite id="stagewise-cmorl-2024" /></span>.

The second answer adapts weights during training. Srisuchinnawong and Manoonpong's ROGER lowers the primary-reward gain and raises penalty gains near selected constraint thresholds, reversing the balance farther away, with penalties subtracted from the primary reward. The revised paper reports an exploration-induced violation in simulation and limits its safety claims to assumptions of zero-penalty optimality and gentle system and learning dynamics <Cite id="gain-adaptation-2025" />.

The third answer removes per-skill tuning. BeyondMimic covers many agile humanoid skills with one tracking setup and shared hyperparameters <Cite id="beyondmimic-2025" />, and language models can write the reward code itself.

## Eureka: the reward as code under evolution

Eureka searches over reward code while PPO learns the policies. A language model receives a task description and environment observation code, with the existing reward excluded, and proposes Python reward functions that train PPO policies in Isaac Gym. A separate task-fitness function selects candidates, and scalar snapshots of fitness and each reward component feed the next mutation <Cite id="eureka-2024" />.

The evaluation covers 29 simulated tasks, nine Isaac Gym environments and 20 Dexterity tasks across 10 morphologies. The headline is 83% of tasks and 52% average normalized improvement over each benchmark's original shaped reward; Eureka matches or beats the human reward on all nine Isaac tasks and 15 of 20 Dexterity tasks. Search uses five runs of five iterations with 16 candidates each and reports the best reward across restarts. Final rewards get five PPO runs, scored by the mean of maximum task fitness over ten checkpoints. Human-normalized scores are bounded to `[0, 3]` for the headline, so 52% measures normalized reward improvement <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-translate-x-[16px]"><Cite id="eureka-2024" /></span>.

Eureka needs a task-fitness function, which its authors say can diverge from human intent; they show an extension with human-written reward reflection <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. Specification gaming satisfies the literal objective without achieving the intended outcome, and DeepMind researchers have collected around 60 examples <Cite id="specification-gaming-2020" />. Task-only rewards for athletic hardware skills invite reward hacking, which an actuator model fitted to real data reduced <Cite id="fey-athletic-2025" />. MAPL learns quadruped rewards from objective-wise LLM preferences and matches or beats expert rewards in four environments <Cite id="mapl-2026" />. No published study compares how often LLM-written and human-written rewards exploit the contact solver.

DrEureka writes both reward functions and domain-randomization distributions, getting a quadruped to walk atop a yoga ball <Cite id="dreureka-2024" />.

Lee and colleagues' Reward Design Agent (RDA) compares against a matched Eureka implementation with the same language-model backbone, task instructions and training budget. RDA also analyzes rendered trajectories with reward logs, scores subtasks, summarizes failures and revises subtask descriptions and reward code. On 12 ManiSkill tabletop tasks and four HumanoidBench tasks with SAC and SimbaV2, it reports higher rated alignment on HumanoidBench (0.70 against Eureka's 0.47) with success of 0.42, slightly lower than Eureka's. A vision-language model rates five rollout videos, each queried four times. The authors list costly RL training, imperfect fine-grained visual reasoning and conflicting catch and throw rewards in basketball as limitations <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-translate-x-[128px]"><Cite id="rda-2026" /></span>.

## Curriculum: staying on the difficulty frontier

<Term id="reward-shaping">Reward shaping</Term> decides what to optimize; <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> decides in what order difficulty appears. Ng and colleagues showed that a shaping reward expressible as a difference of potentials leaves the optimal policy unchanged, that this form is necessary for invariance, and that well-known shaping bugs come from non-potential-based rewards <Cite id="ng-reward-shaping-1999" />.

Terrain curriculum promotes robots that succeed on a tile to harder terrain and demotes those that fail, with thousands of parallel robots tracking the difficulty frontier <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Manipulation lacks such cheap evaluation, since its [MDP is not cheaply simulatable](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion). Automatic Domain Randomization widens the randomization distribution as success crosses a threshold, an idea from OpenAI's dexterous hand work that Isaac Lab ships as a configurable curriculum <Cite id="openai-rubiks-cube-2019" /> <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. A velocity-command curriculum is one of two key components of Margolis and colleagues' Mini Cheetah controller, which sustains up to 3.9 m/s <Cite id="margolis-rapid-2022" />.

Isaac Lab's DexPBT workers run independent RL processes with their own hyperparameters, and strong workers replace weak workers' weights and hyperparameters. Its RL-Games implementation reproduces a 6D reposing task with eight workers on one to two GPUs each, converging in about 16 hours on NVIDIA OVX L40 hardware <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. Eurekaverse has a language model write progressively harder parkour environments as code, and its curriculum transfers to a real quadruped, outperforming courses designed by humans <Cite id="eurekaverse-2024" />.

BVER, from Marco Hutter (ETH Zurich) and colleagues (October 2026), borrows from [bidirectional RRT](/classical/motion-planning/): it grows start states outward from the goal and goals outward from the start, steering them toward each other. On quadruped box climbing it reached 95% success on a 0.4 m box in about 65% fewer iterations than the best reference-free curriculum it was compared with. It was also the only one of them to learn a 0.7 m box <Cite id="bver-2026" />. Model-based control can also drive the curriculum: ZEST adds a model-based assistive wrench as an automatic curriculum during RL training <Cite id="zest-2026" />.

## The classical baseline got stronger

Classical legged control simplifies the model in its <Term id="mpc">MPC</Term> layer. MIT Cheetah 3 cast its dynamics as a convex problem that still captures the full 3D system, planning ground reaction forces over horizons up to 0.5 s and solving in under 1 ms at 20 to 30 Hz <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Zhang and colleagues call this tradition the "model hierarchies commonly seen in traditional model-based MPC", built on "reduced-order models and hierarchical control approaches" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

Whole-body MPC itself ran on legged hardware earlier. Sleiman and colleagues' 2021 framework plans locomotion and manipulation as one multi-contact optimal control problem, solved in real time on the robot's onboard computer, and pushed and pulled a heavy resistive door <Cite id="sleiman-wbmpc-2021" />. Whole-body sampling-based MPC reached legged hardware in 2024. Alvarez-Padilla and colleagues report what they call the first whole-body sampling-based MPC deployment on real legged robots, including climbing a box about the robot's height <Cite id="mppi-whole-body-2024" />. DIAL-MPC optimizes full-order quadruped dynamics in real time without training and outperforms RL policies by 50% on climbing tasks <Cite id="dial-mpc-2024" />. An IROS 2026 paper from the RAI Institute uses continuous-time whole-body MPC on a Spot with an arm to return table-tennis balls of varying spin and rally with human players <Cite id="rai-iros-2026" />.

Zhang and colleagues' MuJoCo-based iLQR controller was first posted in 2025 and revised in 2026 to appear at ICRA 2026. It runs on Unitree Go1 and Go2, a Go1 walking on its hind legs into a handstand, and an H1 humanoid tracking a trotting gait. It uses finite-difference derivatives and one warm-started iLQR update per solve, sending joint-angle targets to PD control that the planning model includes. On a 20-core 13th-generation Intel i9, iLQR and TV-LQR feedback run at about 50 Hz and 300 Hz, with a default 0.35 s horizon discretized at 100 Hz. The experiments fuse 100 Hz OptiTrack measurements with 500 Hz joint data, and onboard-only deployment is untested. Raising MuJoCo's impratio from 1 to 100 to reduce slipping doubled a quadruped iteration from 10 to 20 ms <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

MPC re-solves against the measured state at every step, so feedback corrects part of the model mismatch online. The gap remains: Zhang and colleagues call for better system identification "to overcome the significant sim-to-real gap" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />, and Boston Dynamics says MPC works well when "the controller's model behaves similarly to the physical system". Its 2024 Spot account keeps the model-based locomotion controller while a learned policy removes the need to run multiple MPC instances in parallel <Cite id="bd-spot-rl-2024" />. In its August 2025 Atlas report with TRI, language-conditioned policies drive the robot through the control interface of an MPC-based teleoperation system <Cite id="bd-atlas-lbm-2025" />. Its 2026 Atlas workflow trains RL policies in simulation to stay as close as possible to a reference animation <Cite id="bd-atlas-hard-work-2026" />.

## The case for each side

<MpcComparison className="my-6" />

The case for learning: sim-trained controllers took two generations of ANYmal through natural environments "beyond the reach of prior published work in legged locomotion", in Lee and colleagues' words, at the cost of one forward pass at deploy time <Cite id="lee-2020" />. Miki and colleagues later report traversal of harsh natural terrain at "unprecedented speeds" <Cite id="miki-2022" />. Miller and colleagues report an end-to-end RL policy on Spot at over 5.2 m/s, more than triple the default controller's maximum speed <Cite id="miller-spot-2025" />. In drone racing, RL beat optimal control because it "optimizes a better objective" and copes with model uncertainty through <Term id="domain-randomization">domain randomization</Term> <Cite id="racing-rl-vs-oc-2023" />. Semantic, language-conditioned control comes from the learned side, as Gemini Robotics 2 shows on [humanoid whole-body control](/rl-sim2real/humanoid-wbc/) <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2-2026" />.

The case for model-based control: a deliberately simple whole-body iLQR baseline on an off-the-shelf simulator runs in real time on quadruped and humanoid hardware, with no task data and hand-tuned cost weights <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />. Grandia and colleagues optimize all of ANYmal's degrees of freedom in real time against foothold constraints from elevation maps <Cite id="grandia-2022" />. Sampling MPC now discovers gaits and contact sequences without handcrafted patterns, in real time on a CPU <Cite id="schramm-2025" />. Convex MPC can enforce friction-cone limits as hard constraints, as MIT Cheetah 3's ground-reaction-force planner does with a pyramid approximation, where a reward penalty only discourages violations <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Newton's hydroelastic contact modeling is inspired by Drake's, Drake developer TRI partners with Newton on solvers and contact, and Newton is now a physics backend for Isaac Lab <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" /> <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. NVIDIA presents higher-fidelity contact modeling as a route to better sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />, and massively parallel sampling MPC on a real Franka reports contact sensitivity as a key challenge <Cite id="dierking-2026" />.

## Where the debate landed

Hybrids now combine the two. MPC-guided policy search used MPC with full state to generate training data for a simulated quadrotor's network, which runs at a fraction of MPC's cost <Cite id="mpc-gps-2015" />. DTC trains an RL policy to track footholds from a model-based planner and reports more robustness than model-based counterparts on slippery or deformable ground <Cite id="dtc-2023" />. A policy trained entirely in a differentiable simulator transferred zero-shot to a real quadruped <Cite id="schwarke-2024" />.
