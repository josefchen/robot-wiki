Reward design is writing the objective a reinforcement learning policy learns to maximize: in legged locomotion, a weighted sum of terms whose weights are tuned by hand <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, and now also reward code written by language models <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. Model-predictive control instead plans actions online with an explicit dynamics model <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

## The reward is the product

Rudin and colleagues define a weighted sum of nine reward terms, including feet air time, and prescribe no gait schedule <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. The initial `legged_gym` commit (October 2021) declares fifteen entries in `LeggedRobotCfg.rewards.scales`. Nine are nonzero, from velocity tracking and joint torque to feet air time and action rate, and the joint-limit and contact-force functions among its nineteen reward functions are inactive in this config <Cite id="legged-gym-repo-2021" />.

Rudin and colleagues report artifacts such as a dragging leg and unreasonably high or low base heights, and tuned the weights before hardware transfer <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Margolis and Agrawal note that a policy failing in a new environment needs "a slow and iterative cycle of reward and environment redesign" <Cite id="walk-these-ways-2022" />. Rudin's <Term id="ppo">PPO</Term> adapts its learning rate from the KL divergence between new and old policies: with a target of 0.01, it lowers the rate when KL exceeds twice the target and raises it under half <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

## Three answers to weight tuning

The first answer adds constraints. Yunho Kim and colleagues use three rewards (command tracking, joint torque, action smoothness) and 11 constraints, six probabilistic and five average, training separate controllers in simulation for Raibo, Mini-cheetah, Hound, ANYmal B, ANYmal C, Unitree Go1 and Atlas and deploying on Raibo and Mini-cheetah. Across robots they adjusted only the torque coefficient among the rewards, found by trial and error, though constraint limits and PD gains still changed, and the probabilistic and average limits are not per-step hard bounds <Cite id="rewards-constraints-2024" />.

Dohyeong Kim and colleagues split acrobatic tasks into manual stages with their own rewards and costs, five reward and five cost functions for a back-flip; stage-transition rules, cost thresholds, preferences and hyperparameters stay hand-set, and a real Go1 performs back-flips, side-rolls and two-hand walking <Cite id="stagewise-cmorl-2024" />.

The second answer adapts weights during training. Srisuchinnawong and Manoonpong's ROGER shifts gains between the primary reward and penalties near selected constraint thresholds, with penalties subtracted from the primary reward; the revised paper reports an exploration-induced violation in simulation and limits its safety claims to assumptions of zero-penalty optimality and gentle system and learning dynamics <Cite id="gain-adaptation-2025" />. The third answer removes per-skill tuning: BeyondMimic covers many agile humanoid skills with one tracking setup and shared hyperparameters <Cite id="beyondmimic-2025" />, and language models can write the reward code itself.

## Eureka: the reward as code under evolution

Eureka searches over reward code while PPO learns the policies. A language model receives a task description and environment observation code, with the existing reward excluded, and proposes Python reward functions; a separate task-fitness function selects candidates, and fitness and reward-component snapshots feed the next mutation <Cite id="eureka-2024" />.

The evaluation covers 29 simulated tasks, and the headline is 83% of tasks and 52% average normalized improvement over each benchmark's original shaped reward; Eureka matches or beats the human reward on all nine Isaac tasks and 15 of 20 Dexterity tasks. Search reports the best reward across restarts, and final rewards are scored by the mean of maximum task fitness over ten checkpoints. Human-normalized scores are bounded to `[0, 3]` for the headline, so 52% measures normalized reward improvement <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-translate-x-[48px]"><Cite id="eureka-2024" /></span>.

Eureka needs a task-fitness function, which its authors say can diverge from human intent; they show an extension with human-written reward reflection <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. Specification gaming satisfies the literal objective without achieving the intended outcome, and DeepMind researchers have collected around 60 examples <Cite id="specification-gaming-2020" />. DrEureka writes both reward functions and domain-randomization distributions, getting a quadruped to walk atop a yoga ball <Cite id="dreureka-2024" />.

## Curriculum: staying on the difficulty frontier

<Term id="reward-shaping">Reward shaping</Term> decides what to optimize; <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> decides in what order difficulty appears. Ng and colleagues showed that a shaping reward expressible as a difference of potentials leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that this form is necessary for invariance <Cite id="ng-reward-shaping-1999" />.

A terrain curriculum promotes robots that succeed on a tile to harder terrain and demotes those that fail <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Manipulation lacks such cheap evaluation, since its [MDP is not cheaply simulatable](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion). Automatic Domain Randomization widens the randomization distribution as success crosses a threshold, an idea from OpenAI's dexterous hand work that Isaac Lab ships as a configurable curriculum <Cite id="openai-rubiks-cube-2019" /> <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />, and a velocity-command curriculum is one of the two main components of Margolis and colleagues' Mini Cheetah controller <Cite id="margolis-rapid-2022" />.

<Image id="isaac-gym-anymal-terrain-2021" />

BVER, from Marco Hutter (ETH Zurich) and colleagues (October 2026), borrows from [bidirectional RRT](/classical/motion-planning/), growing start states outward from the goal and goals outward from the start; on quadruped box climbing it reached 95% success on a 0.4 m box in about 65% fewer iterations than the best reference-free curriculum it was compared with <Cite id="bver-2026" />.

Success Guided Sampling (SGS), from Octi Zhang, Byron Boots and colleagues at the University of Washington and NVIDIA (CoRL 2026), applies the same idea to task configurations in general. It samples the configurations near the edge of what the policy can do, because uniform sampling spends a growing share of each batch on configurations already mastered or still out of reach. With up to about a million parallel environments, it solved multi-terrain quadruped locomotion and contact-rich assembly tasks that the prior methods it was compared with failed to solve, and the distilled RGB assembly policies transferred zero-shot to real hardware <Cite id="sgs-2026" />.

## The classical baseline got stronger

Classical legged control simplifies the model in its <Term id="mpc">MPC</Term> layer. MIT Cheetah 3 cast its dynamics as a convex problem that still captures the full 3D system, planning ground reaction forces over horizons up to 0.5 s and solving in under 1 ms <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Zhang and colleagues call this tradition the "model hierarchies commonly seen in traditional model-based MPC", built on "reduced-order models and hierarchical control approaches" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

Whole-body MPC itself ran on legged hardware earlier: Sleiman and colleagues' 2021 framework plans locomotion and manipulation as one multi-contact optimal control problem, solved in real time onboard, and pushed and pulled a heavy resistive door <Cite id="sleiman-wbmpc-2021" />. DIAL-MPC optimizes full-order quadruped dynamics in real time without training and outperforms RL policies by 50% on climbing tasks <Cite id="dial-mpc-2024" />.

Zhang and colleagues' MuJoCo-based iLQR controller, revised in 2026 to appear at ICRA 2026, runs on Unitree quadrupeds and an H1 humanoid with one warm-started iLQR update per solve. On a 20-core 13th-generation Intel i9, iLQR and TV-LQR feedback run at about 50 Hz and 300 Hz; the experiments fuse 100 Hz OptiTrack measurements with 500 Hz joint data, and onboard-only deployment is untested. Raising MuJoCo's impratio from 1 to 100 to reduce slipping doubled a quadruped iteration from 10 to 20 ms <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

MPC re-solves against the measured state at every step, so feedback corrects part of the model mismatch online, though not all of it: Zhang and colleagues call for better system identification "to overcome the significant sim-to-real gap" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />, and Boston Dynamics says MPC works well when "the controller's model behaves similarly to the physical system". Its 2024 Spot account keeps the model-based locomotion controller while a learned policy removes the need to run multiple MPC instances in parallel <Cite id="bd-spot-rl-2024" />, and in its August 2025 Atlas report with TRI, language-conditioned policies drive the robot through the control interface of an MPC-based teleoperation system <Cite id="bd-atlas-lbm-2025" />.

## The case for each side

<MpcComparison className="my-6" />

The case for learning: sim-trained controllers took two generations of ANYmal through natural environments "beyond the reach of prior published work in legged locomotion", in Lee and colleagues' words, at the cost of one forward pass at deploy time <Cite id="lee-2020" />. Miller and colleagues report an end-to-end RL policy on Spot at over 5.2 m/s, more than triple the default controller's maximum speed <Cite id="miller-spot-2025" />. In drone racing, RL beat optimal control because it "optimizes a better objective" and copes with model uncertainty through <Term id="domain-randomization">domain randomization</Term> <Cite id="racing-rl-vs-oc-2023" />, and semantic, language-conditioned control comes from the learned side, as Gemini Robotics 2 shows on [humanoid whole-body control](/rl-sim2real/humanoid-wbc/) <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2-2026" />.

The case for model-based control: a deliberately simple whole-body iLQR baseline runs in real time on quadruped and humanoid hardware with no task data <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />. Grandia and colleagues optimize all of ANYmal's degrees of freedom in real time against foothold constraints from elevation maps <Cite id="grandia-2022" />, and sampling MPC now discovers gaits and contact sequences without handcrafted patterns, in real time on a CPU <Cite id="schramm-2025" />. Convex MPC can enforce friction-cone limits as hard constraints, as MIT Cheetah 3's planner does, where a reward penalty only discourages violations <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />.

Newton's hydroelastic contact modeling is inspired by Drake's, Drake developer TRI partners with Newton on solvers and contact, and Newton is now a physics backend for Isaac Lab <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" /> <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. Massively parallel sampling MPC on a real Franka still reports contact sensitivity as a main challenge <Cite id="dierking-2026" />.

## Where the debate landed

Hybrids now combine the two: MPC-guided policy search used MPC with full state to generate training data for a simulated quadrotor's network, which runs at a fraction of MPC's cost <Cite id="mpc-gps-2015" />. DTC trains an RL policy to track footholds from a model-based planner and reports more robustness than model-based counterparts on slippery or deformable ground <Cite id="dtc-2023" />, and a policy trained entirely in a differentiable simulator transferred zero-shot to a real quadruped <Cite id="schwarke-2024" />.
