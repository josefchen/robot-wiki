In <Term id="reinforcement-learning">reinforcement learning</Term> a robot learns whatever its reward pays for, so reward design, writing that score, is where much of the engineering goes. In legged robots it is a weighted sum of terms tuned by hand <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, and now also code written by language models <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. Model-predictive control (MPC) skips learning and plans each move online with an explicit physics model <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

## The reward is the product

A walking policy's behavior is largely set by its reward terms and their weights. Rudin and colleagues summed nine terms, from tracking the commanded velocity to rewarding time with feet in the air, and prescribed no gait <Cite id="rudin-2021" />; the `legged_gym` code of October 2021 sets nine of its fifteen reward weights to nonzero values <Cite id="legged-gym-repo-2021" />. Early policies dragged a leg or held the body unreasonably high or low until the weights were retuned before hardware transfer <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. When a policy fails in a new environment, Margolis and Agrawal write, the fix is "a slow and iterative cycle of reward and environment redesign" <Cite id="walk-these-ways-2022" />.

## Three answers to weight tuning

The first answer adds constraints. Yunho Kim and colleagues used three rewards and 11 constraints to train controllers for seven robots, from Raibo to Atlas, and deployed them on Raibo and Mini Cheetah. Among the rewards only the torque weight changed between robots, though constraint limits and motor gains still did, and its limits bound averages or probabilities, so a single step may exceed them <Cite id="rewards-constraints-2024" />. Dohyeong Kim and colleagues split acrobatics into hand-set stages with their own rewards and costs, and a real Go1 performed back-flips, side-rolls and two-hand walking <Cite id="stagewise-cmorl-2024" />.

The second answer adapts or restructures the weights. ROGER shifts weight between the main reward and its penalties as the robot nears selected constraint limits, with safety claims limited to stated assumptions <Cite id="gain-adaptation-2025" />. MAPL scores competing objectives from a language model's trajectory comparisons and combines them <Cite id="mapl-2026" />, and MPBRS (October 2026) reused one reward structure and set of weights across nine humanoid robots, with lower tuning sensitivity than standard shaping <Cite id="mpbrs-2026" />.

The third answer removes per-skill tuning. BeyondMimic covers many agile humanoid skills with one tracking setup and shared settings <Cite id="beyondmimic-2025" />, ZEST moves skills onto Atlas without extensive reward shaping <Cite id="zest-2026" />, and FastSAC trains humanoid walking with deliberately minimal rewards <Cite id="seo-2025" />. A planner can also stand in for the shaped reward. In August 2026, Schuck, Hutter and colleagues used sample-based MPC in simulation to generate demonstrations, then trained RL on sparse task rewards alone; the learned policies surpassed their MPC teacher and ran on an arm-equipped Spot and a Unitree G1 <Cite id="smpc-sparse-rl-2026" />.

## Language models write the reward

Eureka treats reward design as a search over code. A language model reads the task description and the environment's observation code, proposes Python reward functions, and <Term id="ppo">PPO</Term> trains a policy on each. A separate fitness function scores the results, and the scores guide the next round <Cite id="eureka-2024" />.

Across 29 simulated tasks, Eureka's rewards beat each benchmark's original shaped reward on 83%, with 52% average normalized improvement, and matched or beat the human reward on all nine Isaac tasks and 15 of 20 Dexterity tasks. The headline takes the best reward across restarts and caps human-normalized scores at 3 <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-translate-x-[48px]"><Cite id="eureka-2024" /></span>.

<EurekaLoop className="my-6" />

The fitness function is still written by people and can diverge from what they intend; the authors show an extension in which humans comment on the reward <Cite id="eureka-2024" />. RDA uses a vision-language model to watch trajectories and summarize failures before revising reward code <Cite id="rda-2026" />. The underlying risk is specification gaming, satisfying the literal objective without the intended outcome, of which DeepMind researchers collected around 60 examples <Cite id="specification-gaming-2020" />; rewards for the task alone are prone to it <Cite id="fey-athletic-2025" />. DrEureka writes both rewards and randomization ranges, getting a quadruped to walk on a yoga ball <Cite id="dreureka-2024" />, and ARPG (November 2026) has language models write the whole problem definition, finding that runnable code can still produce unlearnable tasks when state, action, reward and termination do not fit together <Cite id="arpg-2026" />.

## Curriculum: staying on the difficulty frontier

<Term id="reward-shaping">Reward shaping</Term> decides what to optimize; a <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> decides in what order difficulty appears. Shaping has a classic safe form: Ng and colleagues showed that a bonus written as a difference of "potentials" between states leaves the best policy unchanged, and that only this form guarantees it <Cite id="ng-reward-shaping-1999" />.

A terrain curriculum promotes robots that succeed on a tile to harder terrain and demotes those that fail <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Manipulation lacks such cheap evaluation, since it is [harder to simulate](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion). Automatic Domain Randomization widens the range of simulated conditions as success crosses a threshold, an idea from OpenAI's robot hand that Isaac Lab ships as a configurable curriculum <Cite id="openai-rubiks-cube-2019" /> <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. A curriculum over commanded speed is one of the two main parts of Margolis and colleagues' Mini Cheetah controller <Cite id="margolis-rapid-2022" />, Eurekaverse writes progressively harder parkour courses <Cite id="eurekaverse-2024" />, and Skild's September 2026 soccer policy trained against recent versions of itself with scoring as its objective <Cite id="skild-self-play-2026" />.

<Image id="isaac-gym-anymal-terrain-2021" />

BVER, from Marco Hutter's group at ETH Zurich (October 2026), borrows from [bidirectional RRT](/classical/motion-planning/), growing start states outward from the goal and goals outward from the start; on quadruped box climbing it reached 95% success on a 0.4 m box in about 65% fewer iterations than the best compared curriculum <Cite id="bver-2026" />. Success Guided Sampling, from the University of Washington and NVIDIA (CoRL 2026), samples task setups near the edge of the policy's ability, since uniform sampling wastes a growing share of each batch on setups already mastered or still out of reach. With up to about a million parallel environments it solved multi-terrain walking and contact-rich assembly tasks the compared methods failed on, and camera-based assembly policies distilled from it transferred zero-shot to real hardware <Cite id="sgs-2026" />.

## The classical baseline got stronger

Classical legged control simplifies the model inside its <Term id="mpc">MPC</Term> layer. MIT Cheetah 3 cast its dynamics as a convex problem, one a solver can finish quickly and reliably, planning ground forces up to 0.5 s ahead and solving in under 1 ms <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />. Zhang and colleagues describe this tradition as built on "reduced-order models and hierarchical control approaches" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />.

Whole-body MPC, which plans with the full robot model, has since run on hardware. Sleiman and colleagues' 2021 framework planned walking and manipulation as one problem, solved in real time onboard, and pushed and pulled a heavy resistive door <Cite id="sleiman-wbmpc-2021" />. DIAL-MPC optimizes full quadruped dynamics in real time without training and outperformed RL policies by 50% on climbing tasks <Cite id="dial-mpc-2024" />. Whole-body MPPI uses parallel MuJoCo rollouts for real walking, box climbing and pushing <Cite id="mppi-whole-body-2024" />, and the RAI Institute's whole-body MPC lets an arm-equipped Spot rally at table tennis with people <Cite id="rai-iros-2026" />.

Zhang and colleagues' MuJoCo-based iLQR controller (to appear at ICRA 2026) runs on Unitree quadrupeds and an H1 humanoid at about 50 Hz, with a faster feedback layer at 300 Hz; the experiments use external motion capture, so onboard-only deployment is untested <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />. A November 2026 humanoid iLQR-MPC replans footsteps and contact forces together 100 times a second in simulation, and a real G1 running it recovered from 245 N forward and 175 N sideways pushes with no policy training <Cite id="ilqr-mpc-humanoid-2026" />.

MPC re-solves against the measured state at every step, so feedback corrects part of any model error, though not all of it. Zhang and colleagues call for better system identification "to overcome the significant sim-to-real gap" <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />, and Boston Dynamics says MPC works well when "the controller's model behaves similarly to the physical system". Its 2024 Spot account keeps the model-based controller while a learned policy removes the need to run several MPC instances in parallel <Cite id="bd-spot-rl-2024" />. In its August 2025 Atlas report with TRI, language-conditioned policies drive the robot through the interface of an MPC-based teleoperation system <Cite id="bd-atlas-lbm-2025" />.

## The case for each side

<MpcComparison className="my-6" />

The case for learning: simulation-trained controllers took two generations of ANYmal through natural environments "beyond the reach of prior published work in legged locomotion", in Lee and colleagues' words, at the cost of one network pass per step <Cite id="lee-2020" />, and later through a 2.2 km alpine hike <Cite id="miki-2022" />. Miller and colleagues report an RL policy on Spot at over 5.2 m/s, more than triple the default controller's top speed <Cite id="miller-spot-2025" />, and Boston Dynamics uses RL to have Atlas lift a fridge, sensing the load through its own body <Cite id="bd-atlas-hard-work-2026" />. In drone racing, RL beat optimal control because it "optimizes a better objective" and copes with model uncertainty through <Term id="domain-randomization">domain randomization</Term> <Cite id="racing-rl-vs-oc-2023" />. Language-conditioned control also comes from the learned side, as Gemini Robotics 2 shows in [humanoid whole-body control](/rl-sim2real/humanoid-wbc/) <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2-2026" />.

The case for model-based control: a simple whole-body iLQR baseline runs in real time on quadruped and humanoid hardware with no task data <Cite id="mujoco-ilqr-2026" />. Grandia and colleagues optimize all of ANYmal's joints in real time against foothold limits from elevation maps <Cite id="grandia-2022" />, and sampling MPC now discovers gaits from trotting to galloping without handcrafted patterns, in real time on a CPU <Cite id="schramm-2025" />. Convex MPC can enforce friction limits as hard constraints, as MIT Cheetah 3's planner does, where a reward penalty only discourages violations <Cite id="di-carlo-2018" />.

The two sides increasingly share tools. Newton's hydroelastic contact is inspired by Drake's, TRI partners with Newton on solvers and contact, and Newton is a physics backend for Isaac Lab <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" /> <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. Massively parallel sampling MPC on a real Franka arm still reports contact sensitivity as a main challenge <Cite id="dierking-2026" />.

## Where the debate landed

Hybrids combine the two. MPC-guided policy search used MPC with full state to generate training data for a simulated quadrotor's network, which runs at a fraction of MPC's cost <Cite id="mpc-gps-2015" />. DTC trains an RL policy to follow footholds from a model-based planner and reports more robustness than model-based counterparts on slippery or soft ground <Cite id="dtc-2023" />. A policy trained entirely in a differentiable simulator, where the physics provides gradients, transferred zero-shot to a real quadruped <Cite id="schwarke-2024" />. Sumo runs the hybrid the other way: a sampling planner steers a pretrained whole-body RL policy, and a real Spot uprighted a tire heavier than its nominal lifting capacity <Cite id="sumo-2026" />. In a simulated quadruped, letting RL adjust a model-based controller's references raised survival under disturbances from 50.35% to 77.29% <Cite id="structured-references-2026" />.
