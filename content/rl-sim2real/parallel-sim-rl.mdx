Massively parallel simulation is training a robot policy across thousands of simulated copies of its environment at once, with the physics on a GPU. It produced the four-minute walking run in the [previous module](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion), trained by Rudin and colleagues <Cite id="rudin-2021" /> with the 2017 <Term id="ppo">PPO</Term> algorithm <Cite id="ppo-2017" />.

## The 2021 break: physics moved onto the GPU

Isaac Gym kept physics and policy training on one GPU, passing PhysX buffers straight to PyTorch tensors without CPU round trips. Its authors report 2 to 3 orders of magnitude improvement over a CPU simulator feeding a GPU network <Cite id="isaac-gym-2021" />.

Rudin and colleagues scaled from 128 robots, typical of CPU implementations, to 16,384 on one workstation GPU. Flat terrain took under four minutes and uneven terrain twenty, "a speedup of multiple orders of magnitude compared to previous work". Their deployment policy used 4,096 robots, 98,304-transition batches (24 steps per robot) and 1,500 updates in under 20 minutes on an i9-11900k CPU and RTX A6000 GPU <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

Their game-inspired <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> promotes robots that cross their tile, demotes those covering under half the commanded distance, and loops hardest-level solvers back to a random level <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. The paper's released `legged_gym` repository (October 2021) is the Isaac Gym environment used to train ANYmal on rough terrain <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-32"><Cite id="legged-gym-repo-2021" /></span>.

In the <Term id="parallel-simulation">massively parallel</Term> regime, Rudin's PPO also bootstraps the critic at time-outs and adapts the learning rate to a KL target of 0.01 <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

## The JAX line: Brax, MJX, MuJoCo Playground

MuJoCo, a physics engine for model-based control <Cite id="mujoco-2012" />, became free in October 2021 and open source in 2022 under Google DeepMind <Cite id="mujoco-overview-docs-2026" />. Brax (Google, 2021) compiled physics and learning algorithms in JAX on one accelerator, training performant policies on MuJoCo-like tasks in minutes <Cite id="brax-2021" />.

MuJoCo XLA (MJX) ran it on the GPU. MuJoCo Playground (2025) built on it an open framework for quadrupeds, humanoids, hands and arms, with sim-to-real transfer as an express goal <Cite id="mujoco-playground-2025" />. MJX-Warp, built for NVIDIA GPUs, fixes contact and constraint bottlenecks but lacks automatic differentiation <Cite id="mjx-docs-2026" />. mjlab pairs MuJoCo Warp with Isaac Lab's manager-based API <Cite id="mjlab-2026" />.

## What the environment count actually buys

The interactive scene is an unbenchmarked toy with a fixed 220-million-transition budget, an assumed CPU-cost curve that raises per-iteration costs, and a calibrated near-four-minute default at 4,096 environments. Only Rudin's separate deployment run ties 4,096 robots to a reported bound <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

That curve illustrates a bottleneck the Isaac Lab paper reports: on a state-based Franka drawer task, an RTX 5090 workstation with an AMD 9800X3D nearly matches a server with two RTX PRO 6000 GPUs and two 64-core EPYC 9554 CPUs. The authors attribute this task-dependent result to CPU bottlenecks in PhysX and the training loop, and the workstation's fast single core <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

Environment count also changes learning. At fixed batch size, more robots cut Rudin's training time but hurt the policy past a point <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. SAPG reports PPO saturating beyond a certain count <Cite id="sapg-2024" />, while Beukman and colleagues avoid plateaus with over 1M environments, improving monotonically to one trillion transitions <Cite id="beukman-2026" />. Parallel Q-Learning scales off-policy learning to tens of thousands of environments <Cite id="pql-2023" />, and FastTD3 solves a range of HumanoidBench tasks in under 3 hours on one A100 <Cite id="fasttd3-2025" />.

## Isaac Lab and the framework era

Isaac Lab grew out of Orbit <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />, a 2023 framework on Isaac Sim <Cite id="orbit-2023" />. Its 2025 paper describes OpenUSD scene graphs, PhysX, RTX rendering, tiled camera rendering in one pass, and Warp ray-cast height scanners and LiDAR-like sensors without photoreal effects. It adds implicit and explicit actuator models, from nonlinear effort limits to learned networks, a manager-based API, and built-in RSL-RL, RL-Games, SKRL, Stable-Baselines3 and Ray <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

Headless state-based training on RTX PRO 6000 GPUs scales "almost perfectly linearly". With eight GPUs and 16,384 environments, the DextrAH <Cite id="dextrah-g-2024" /> teacher grasp-and-lift task exceeds 900,000 FPS and Franka cabinet 1.6 million, counting environment steps over simulation plus learning time <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

NVIDIA's 2026 overview says Isaac Lab 3.0 splits backend code from the core API. Users choose Isaac Sim's PhysX and RTX for sensor-rich work or headless Newton for throughput, adding photoreal sensors through the standalone OVRTX renderer, or using Newton's renderer for many-environment vision RL <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" />. Isaac Lab 3.0 Early Access shipped on 16 September 2026 for Isaac Sim 6.1 and Newton 1.5.2, with general availability targeted for late October 2026 <Cite id="isaac-lab-3-ea-2026" />.

## Newton: physics as a multi-solver library

The Linux Foundation welcomed Newton, an open-source GPU physics engine co-developed by Disney Research, Google DeepMind and NVIDIA, on 29 September 2025 <Cite id="newton-lf-2025" />. Newton generalizes the deprecated `warp.sim` module of NVIDIA Warp, a Python GPU framework <Cite id="nvidia-warp-repo-2026" />, and uses MuJoCo Warp as its primary backend <Cite id="newton-repo-2026" />.

NVIDIA's 16 March 2026 post announced Newton 1.0 GA at GTC 2026, built on Warp and OpenUSD, and called Isaac Sim 6.0 and Isaac Lab 3.0 early access releases <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-24"><Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" /></span>. Isaac Sim 6.1.0 reached general availability on 10 September 2026 <Cite id="isaac-sim-v6-1-0-2026" />. Newton v1.6.0 shipped the same day with faster CUDA collision detection, experimental graph-friendly robot controllers and an opt-in compliant mode for the VBD solver <Cite id="newton-v1-6-0-2026" />.

Behind one API, NVIDIA's overview lists MuJoCo and Featherstone solvers in generalized coordinates, and SemiImplicit, XPBD and Kamino in maximal coordinates. VBD (vertex block descent <Cite id="vbd-2024" />) handles rigid bodies, particles, cloth and soft bodies with limited joints, implicit MPM particle-based continuum materials, and Style3D cloth <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" />. The release post credits Disney Research's Kamino with closed-chain mechanisms and describes coupling VBD and MPM with MuJoCo Warp for deformable manipulation and locomotion <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />.

The post describes SDF collision from CAD meshes and Drake-inspired hydroelastic contact over finite-area patches, and its assembly example sets a plus or minus 10 mm sparse-SDF band, `narrow_band_range=(-0.01, 0.01)` <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-4"><Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" /></span>. Isaac Sim 6.1 added hydroelastic contact, a pressure-field model <Cite id="elandt-2019" />, to its experimental Newton integration <Cite id="isaac-sim-6-1-release-notes-2026" />.

NVIDIA reports that Skild AI trains GPU-rack-assembly RL policies, including connector insertion, with Isaac Lab's Newton backend, SDF and hydroelastic contact. It says Samsung "will use Newton" for synthetic VLA training data and that Lightwheel tunes SimReady assets on real measurements. Its refrigerator example couples MuJoCo Warp two-way with a VBD cable solver for RB-Y1 cable insertion, and mentions a water-hose connector insertion <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />.

NVIDIA also reports MuJoCo 3.5 (MJWarp) speedups over MJX of 252x for locomotion and 475x for manipulation on RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, without stating task variants, environment counts, precision or timing definition <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. MJWarp optimizes throughput, where MuJoCo optimizes single-step latency <Cite id="mjwarp-docs-2026" />.

## Limits

GPUSimBench finds GPU batching in Isaac Lab and Genesis adds significant run-to-run and inter-environment variability under identical initial conditions <Cite id="gpusimbench-2026" />.

Isaac Lab targets its CPU bottlenecks in future Isaac Lab and Newton releases. For <Term id="domain-randomization">domain randomization</Term>, simulation state sits on the GPU, but mass, friction, contact offsets and joint armature go through PhysX CPU APIs. Mesh scale and collider type change only before simulation starts, most other parameters at runtime <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

Newton's README lists differentiability <Cite id="newton-repo-2026" />, and support differs between solvers <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-5"><Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" /></span>. Its primary backend states: "Differentiability via Warp is not yet available" <Cite id="mujoco-warp-repo-2026" />. SHAC cut training time over 17x against the best established RL algorithm on muscle-actuated locomotion <Cite id="shac-2022" />. Schwarke and colleagues report the first legged sim-to-real transfer learned entirely in differentiable simulation <Cite id="schwarke-2024" />.

Isaac Sim 6.1 adds beta <Term id="system-identification">system identification</Term> tools for headless and interactive optimization of robot simulation parameters <Cite id="isaac-sim-6-1-release-notes-2026" />. The overview's authors call Drake the "gold standard" when contact-implicit trajectory optimisation and rigorous numerics matter more than throughput <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" />.
