## The 2021 break: physics moved onto the GPU

Robot physics usually ran on CPUs while the neural network trained on a GPU, with data shuttling between them. Isaac Gym (2021) kept both on one GPU and reported 2 to 3 orders of magnitude improvement over CPU simulators feeding GPU networks <Cite id="isaac-gym-2021" />. Rudin and colleagues scaled from 128 robots, typical for CPUs, to 16,384 on one workstation GPU; their deployed policy trained 4,096 robots on uneven terrain in under 20 minutes <Cite id="rudin-2021" />.

Their game-inspired <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> promotes robots that cross their tile to harder terrain and demotes those covering under half the commanded distance; robots that solve the hardest level loop back to a random one <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. The `legged_gym` repository released this environment in October 2021 <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-32"><Cite id="legged-gym-repo-2021" /></span>.

## What more environments buy

More environments shorten training only until something else becomes the bottleneck. On one Franka drawer task, an RTX 5090 workstation nearly matched a server with two data-center GPUs and two 64-core CPUs, which Isaac Lab's authors attribute to CPU bottlenecks and the workstation's fast single core <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. Learning changes too: at a fixed batch size, more robots cut Rudin's training time but hurt the policy past a point <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, and SAPG reports PPO saturating beyond a threshold <Cite id="sapg-2024" />.

Others push past that limit. Beukman and colleagues avoid plateaus with over a million environments, improving steadily up to one trillion transitions <Cite id="beukman-2026" />, and Success Guided Sampling scales past a million by focusing practice on tasks the policy solves about half the time <Cite id="sgs-2026" /> <Cite id="nvidia-corl-gr00t-2-2026" />. Off-policy methods, which reuse old data, now scale too: Parallel Q-Learning to tens of thousands of environments <Cite id="pql-2023" />, and FastTD3 with large batches for humanoid learning <Cite id="fasttd3-2025" />.

## The software: two lines converging

Two families of tools grew up. NVIDIA's runs from Isaac Gym to Orbit and Isaac Lab; Google DeepMind's builds on MuJoCo, a physics engine for model-based control <Cite id="mujoco-2012" /> open-sourced in 2022 <Cite id="mujoco-overview-docs-2026" />, compiled for accelerators with JAX and, as MuJoCo Warp, tuned for NVIDIA GPUs, favoring throughput but lacking automatic differentiation <Cite id="mjx-docs-2026" /> <Cite id="mjwarp-docs-2026" />. The lines now meet in Newton, an open GPU physics engine from Disney Research, Google DeepMind and NVIDIA whose main backend is MuJoCo Warp <Cite id="newton-lf-2025" /> <Cite id="newton-repo-2026" />.

Throughput is the selling point, and most figures come from the vendors. Isaac Lab scales "almost perfectly linearly" across GPUs: with eight GPUs and 16,384 environments, the DextrAH <Cite id="dextrah-g-2024" /> grasp-and-lift teacher exceeds 900,000 steps per second and a Franka cabinet task 1.6 million <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. NVIDIA reports Isaac Lab 3.0 with Newton at up to 2.5 times the throughput of Isaac Lab 2.3 with PhysX on a camera-based dexterous benchmark <Cite id="nvidia-corl-gr00t-2-2026" />, and MuJoCo Warp 252 times faster than MJX on locomotion and 475 times on manipulation, without stating environment counts, precision or timing method <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. Skild AI's Steel solver, experimental in Newton 1.8.0 <Cite id="newton-v1-8-0-2026" />, reports 3.8 times PhysX's throughput over 20 benchmarks in Skild's own tests <Cite id="skild-steel-2026" />, and NVIDIA reports Skild training GPU-rack-assembly policies on Newton <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />.

Contact models are improving with it. Newton handles collisions against detailed CAD meshes and hydroelastic contact, which spreads force over a patch instead of a point <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" /> <Cite id="elandt-2019" />. Isaac Sim 6.1 <Cite id="isaac-sim-v6-1-0-2026" /> added that model to its experimental Newton integration <Cite id="isaac-sim-6-1-release-notes-2026" />, alongside Newton 1.6.0 on the same day <Cite id="newton-v1-6-0-2026" />. Newton generalizes NVIDIA Warp's deprecated simulation module <Cite id="nvidia-warp-repo-2026" />.

## Limits

Parallel runs are not perfectly repeatable: GPUSimBench found that batched execution in Isaac Lab and Genesis adds significant variability between runs and between environments, even from identical starting conditions <Cite id="gpusimbench-2026" />, while Steel lists run-to-run determinism as a feature <Cite id="newton-v1-8-0-2026" />. Isaac Lab keeps simulation state on the GPU, but changes to mass, friction and similar parameters for <Term id="domain-randomization">domain randomization</Term> go through the CPU, and mesh scale can change only before simulation starts <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

Gradients through the simulator are the next frontier. SHAC cut training time more than 17 times against the best established RL algorithm on muscle-driven locomotion <Cite id="shac-2022" />, and Schwarke and colleagues report the first legged sim-to-real transfer learned entirely in a differentiable simulator <Cite id="schwarke-2024" />. Newton lists differentiability, though support differs by solver <Cite id="newton-repo-2026" /> <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" /> and its main backend says "Differentiability via Warp is not yet available" <Cite id="mujoco-warp-repo-2026" />. Isaac Sim 6.1 adds beta <Term id="system-identification">system identification</Term> tools <Cite id="isaac-sim-6-1-release-notes-2026" />, and NVIDIA's overview calls Drake the "gold standard" when rigorous numerics matter more than throughput <Cite id="state-of-simulation-2026" />.
