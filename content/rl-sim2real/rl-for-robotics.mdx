Reinforcement learning (RL) trains a robot by trial and error: it acts, receives a numerical reward for how well that went, and adjusts its <Term id="policy">policy</Term> to earn more <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />. In robotics the method is shaped by the price of each try, nearly free in GPU simulation <Cite id="isaac-gym-2021" /> <Cite id="rudin-2021" /> and costly on a real machine.

<Image id="daydreamer-a1-push-recovery-2022" />

## What each try costs

An RL method is limited by how many environment steps, one action and its result, it can afford. In simulation thousands of robots step at once and reset themselves; on hardware each step takes real time and wear, and each episode needs a reset <Cite id="rudin-2021" /> <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />.

<SampleEfficiencyLedger className="my-6" />

Reviews of robot RL list real-world problems that games and simulated benchmarks rarely face <Cite id="ibarz-2021" />, and call stable, sample-efficient real-world RL a priority <Cite id="tang-drl-survey-2025" />.

## Two ways to spend experience

An <Term id="on-policy">on-policy</Term> method learns only from data its current policy just produced, then discards it; PPO takes several update passes over each fresh batch where plain policy gradient takes one <Cite id="ppo-2017" />. An <Term id="off-policy">off-policy</Term> method keeps every try in a <Term id="replay-buffer">replay buffer</Term> and reuses it. Most off-policy methods build on <Term id="q-learning">Q-learning</Term>, which estimates how good each action is and learns from the best next action, even one never taken <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />.

| Algorithm | What it changed |
| --- | --- |
| DDPG (2016) | Off-policy actor-critic learning for continuous actions <Cite id="ddpg-2016" /> |
| TD3 (2018) | Curbed DDPG's overestimated action values <Cite id="td3-2018" /> |
| SAC (2018) | Rewards acting as randomly as the task allows, for steadier learning <Cite id="sac-2018" /> |

### Why simulation made PPO the default

With thousands of simulated robots, tries are cheap and stability is scarce. SAC's authors credit on-policy training with stability at the price of "poor sample complexity" <Cite id="sac-2018" />. Rudin and colleagues trained the four-legged ANYmal with 4,096 simulated robots in under twenty minutes on one GPU, over 122,880 steps per second <Cite id="rudin-2021" /> ([parallel simulation](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl)).

<Image id="isaac-gym-allegro-envs-2021" />

Off-policy methods used data better but ran slower by the clock, so most GPU-simulation work used PPO <Cite id="pql-2023" />. That gap has closed. Parallel Q-Learning beat PPO in wall-clock time <Cite id="pql-2023" />; FastTD3 <Cite id="fasttd3-2025" /> and FastSAC trained humanoid walking that worked on real robots in 15 minutes on one gaming GPU <Cite id="seo-2025" />. In 2026 SAC matched PPO across seven legged robots using PPO's own rewards <Cite id="rsl-rl-sac-2026" />, and FlashSAC beat PPO on more than 60 tasks in 10 simulators <Cite id="flashsac-2026" />.

<Term id="sample-efficiency">Sample efficiency</Term>, the experience a method needs, decides whether real-robot learning takes an afternoon or a season. A small Minitaur quadruped learned to walk from 160,000 steps, about two hours including resets <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />. DayDreamer, which practices inside a learned world model, taught a Unitree A1 to roll over, stand and walk in one hour without a simulator, with people stepping in at the edge of the area <Cite id="daydreamer-2022" />. A Walk in the Park needed 20 minutes and a carefully tuned controller <Cite id="walk-in-the-park-2022" />, and an 11.5-ton excavator learned sub-centimeter path tracking in 40 minutes <Cite id="excavator-mbrl-2026" />. Grasping took far longer: QT-Opt used over 580,000 attempts on seven robots in about 800 robot hours <Cite id="qt-opt-2018" />, and a 2016 study that trained a supervised grasp predictor used about 800,000 over two months <Cite id="levine-hand-eye-2016" />.

## Offline reinforcement learning

<Term id="offline-reinforcement-learning">Offline reinforcement learning</Term> learns from a fixed log of past experience <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />, so it can reuse robot [datasets](/data-hardware/datasets). The danger is optimism: the policy favors actions the log never shows, their estimated values are wrongly high, and no fresh data corrects them <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />. BCQ showed this stops standard off-policy algorithms from learning on fixed data <Cite id="bcq-2018" />. The fixes keep the policy near the data: CQL pushes down values of unseen actions <Cite id="cql-2020" />, IQL never evaluates them <Cite id="iql-2022" />, and TD3+BC adds an imitation term <Cite id="td3-bc-2021" />. Q-Transformer beat earlier offline and imitation methods on a large real-robot suite <Cite id="q-transformer-2023" />, and offline data can speed up later learning <Cite id="ptr-2022" /> <Cite id="cal-ql-2023" /> <Cite id="rlpd-2023" />.

### Does any of this beat behavior cloning on robot data?

On human demonstrations, often not. The robomimic study found that neither BCQ nor CQL did well, while behavior cloning with memory of recent steps was strong <Cite id="robomimic-2021" />. Kumar and colleagues showed that offline RL can beat imitation when the data covers the optimal policy, while on purely expert data the two match in the worst case <Cite id="offline-rl-vs-bc-2022" />. So [behavior cloning](/manipulation/bc-foundations) is the simpler start for teleoperation data, though newer real-robot results favor RL: policies fine-tuned on RL-generated data beat the same policies trained on human demonstrations by up to 40% <Cite id="rldg-2024" />, and Physical Intelligence's RECAP, offline RL for a VLA model, more than doubled throughput on some of the hardest tasks <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

## Sparse reward, and learning from failure

If reward comes only on success, a robot that never succeeds learns nothing. Shaping the reward is one answer ([reward design](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc)). <Term id="hindsight-experience-replay">Hindsight experience replay</Term> (HER) is another: it stores each attempt again as if the goal had been a state the robot reached later, so a missed push still teaches how to push <Cite id="her-2017" />.

<HindsightRelabel className="my-6" />

With HER, DDPG learned pushing, sliding and pick-and-place on a simulated Fetch arm where DDPG alone failed, and the pick-and-place policy worked on a real Fetch without fine-tuning. The tasks had help, such as half the pick-and-place episodes starting with the box grasped, and HER needs a reward it can recompute for any goal <Cite id="her-2017" />.

## The reset problem

A simulated episode restarts itself <Cite id="autonomous-rl-2022" />; a real one ends wherever the robot is, and something must put things back <Cite id="leave-no-trace-2017" />. Zhu and colleagues asked for real-world RL that learns from raw sensors, needs no instrumented rewards and no human resets; their D'Claw hand trained unattended, though evaluation included manual resets <Cite id="real-world-rl-ingredients-2020" />. Leave No Trace learns a reset policy and aborts before states it cannot undo <Cite id="leave-no-trace-2017" />, and VaPRL cut interventions in simulated tasks by practicing from chosen starting points <Cite id="vaprl-2021" />. <Term id="reset-free-learning">Reset-free</Term> MTRF chained a valve and a pipe task on a robot hand with no manual resets, though it relied on a designer's task graph, supplied rewards, motion capture, scripted arm motion and a person placing objects <Cite id="reset-free-rl-2021" />.

FIND (September 2026) lets a vision-language agent look at the scene each attempt leaves and pick the next task, favoring those with low recent success. With a frozen π0.5 policy and residual off-policy RL, success on eight real tasks rose from 55% to 71.9%; one run logged 456 episodes in six hours with 30 recovery interventions <Cite id="find-2026" />.

## Real-robot RL reaches near-perfect reliability

The usual recipe starts from a few demonstrations, learns off-policy on the robot and lets a person step in when it goes wrong <Cite id="serl-2024" /> <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />. HIL-SERL reports 100% success in 100 trials per task, 10 of 10 for whole IKEA assembly, after one to two and a half hours of training for nearly all tasks, counting scripted motion and stops <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" /> <Cite id="hil-serl-scirobotics-2025" />. RL-100 (Science Robotics, July 2026) reports 1,000 successes in 1,000 episodes across eight tasks, from pouring to box folding, under its authors' own protocol <Cite id="rl-100-2025" /> <Cite id="rl-100-scirobotics-2026" />; Johannes A. Stork argued in the same issue that real-world learning can take manipulation beyond imitation <Cite id="stork-beyond-imitation-2026" />.

| System | Reported result |
| --- | --- |
| SERL (2024) | Circuit-board assembly and cable routing in 25 to 50 minutes per policy <Cite id="serl-2024" /> |
| ConRFT (2025) | 96.3% on eight tasks after 45 to 90 minutes of online VLA fine-tuning <Cite id="conrft-2025" /> |
| Q2RL (2026) | Pipe assembly and kitting in 1 to 2 hours online <Cite id="q2rl-2026" /> |
| LWD (2026) | One policy on 16 two-armed robots, 95% over eight tasks <Cite id="lwd-2026" /> |
| ReF-HIL (2026) | 90% success after 18 to 63 minutes of active training <Cite id="ref-hil-2026" /> |
| ACCRUE (2026) | 31.0% to 68.5% less training time on assembly sequences <Cite id="accrue-2026" /> |

## Where the field is heading

Korniak and colleagues improved one generalist policy online across many tasks at once, with gains on tasks left out of online training; value-based RL was critical to that transfer <Cite id="generalist-rl-improvement-2026" />. [RL fine-tuning](/manipulation/rl-finetuning) covers RL on vision-language-action policies, such as online PPO for π0 and π0.5 <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" /> and residual specialists <Cite id="pld-2026" />. DROID <Cite id="droid-2024" /> and Open X-Embodiment <Cite id="open-x-embodiment-2023" /> offer large demonstration sets but no rewards, so offline RL on them needs labels added. [Why RL won locomotion](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion) and [sim-to-real transfer](/rl-sim2real/sim2real-transfer) cover the simulation route.
