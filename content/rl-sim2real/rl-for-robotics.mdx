Reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics is the training of robot policies from their own experience of acting, formalized as a Markov decision process of states, actions, transitions and rewards <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />. A simulated step is nearly free when thousands of GPU robots act at once <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, while a real step costs machine time and a reset.

## What a budget buys, and what it forbids

<SampleEfficiencyLedger className="my-6" />

A review of robotic deep RL case studies lists real-world challenges that mainstream RL research, focused on games and simulated control, seldom addresses <Cite id="ibarz-2021" />. A 2025 survey of real-world successes names stable, sample-efficient real-world RL as a priority for future work <Cite id="tang-drl-survey-2025" />.

## Two ways to spend a transition

An <Term id="on-policy">on-policy</Term> method's update is valid only for data the current policy generated, so each batch is collected, consumed and discarded. PPO alternates sampling with optimizing a surrogate objective that allows several minibatch epochs per batch, where plain policy gradient takes one step <Cite id="ppo-2017" />. An <Term id="off-policy">off-policy</Term> method improves one policy from data another produced, so experience accumulates in a <Term id="replay-buffer">replay buffer</Term>. Underneath is <Term id="q-learning">Q-learning</Term>: fit the value of a state-action pair and bootstrap from the best next action, even one never taken <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />.

DDPG adapted deterministic policy gradients to deep networks and learned pixel-to-torque policies off-policy <Cite id="ddpg-2016" />. TD3 reduced DDPG's overestimation bias with clipped double critics, delayed policy updates and target policy smoothing <Cite id="td3-2018" />. Soft actor-critic added a maximum-entropy objective; its authors named sample complexity and brittle convergence as the two challenges keeping model-free deep RL out of complex real-world domains and designed SAC to address both <Cite id="sac-2018" />.

### Why robotics defaulted to PPO anyway

With thousands of GPU environments, steps are nearly free and training stability is the scarce resource. SAC's authors credit on-policy training with stability at the price of "poor sample complexity" and call off-policy learning with neural function approximation "a major challenge for stability and convergence" <Cite id="sac-2018" />. Rudin and colleagues trained ANYmal with 4,096 parallel robots, 98,304 steps per update and 1,500 updates in under twenty minutes on one workstation GPU, averaging more than 122,880 environment steps per second. The hardware was an i9-11900k CPU and RTX A6000 GPU <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Minitaur's 160,000 control steps over about two hours average about 22 per second, under its 50 Hz rollout rate because resets and overhead count <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />. The [parallel-simulation module](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl) covers the first regime.

The default is now contested. Most GPU-simulation work used PPO for its simplicity and ease of scaling, while off-policy methods were more data-efficient but slower in wall-clock time; Parallel Q-Learning scales to tens of thousands of environments and beats PPO in wall-clock time <Cite id="pql-2023" />. FastTD3 solves a range of HumanoidBench tasks in under 3 hours on one A100 GPU <Cite id="fasttd3-2025" />, and FastSAC and FastTD3 train sim-to-real humanoid locomotion in 15 minutes on one RTX 4090 <Cite id="seo-2025" />.

<Term id="sample-efficiency">Sample efficiency</Term> is the axis these results turn on. Haarnoja and colleagues learned Minitaur locomotion from 160,000 control steps, about 400 rollouts, in about two hours of real-world time that includes resets and the operator <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />. Smith and colleagues learned quadruped walking with model-free RL in 20 minutes in the real world <Cite id="walk-in-the-park-2022" />. DayDreamer trained a Unitree A1 to roll over, stand and walk in one hour without a simulator, and ten more minutes of online learning under pushes produced resistance to light pushes or recovery from harder ones. The authors intervened manually at the edge of the training area without changing the robot's joint configuration or orientation <Cite id="daydreamer-2022" />. In 2026, an online model-based RL controller learned directly on an 11.5-ton hydraulic excavator, without demonstrations or simulation pretraining. After 20 minutes of interaction its tracking accuracy was comparable to that of earlier learned controllers trained on 100 to 150 minutes of data <Cite id="excavator-mbrl-2026" />.

Scale changes the shape of the cost. QT-Opt collected over 580,000 grasp attempts across seven robots in about 800 robot hours <Cite id="qt-opt-2018" />. In 2026, LWD ran offline-to-online RL across a fleet of 16 dual-arm robots on eight manipulation tasks and reached an average success rate of 95% <Cite id="lwd-2026" />. The 2016 hand-eye preprint, which trained a supervised grasp-success predictor, reports about 800,000 grasp attempts over two months with 6 to 14 robots at a time, though its abstract says "over 800,000" and its introduction "several months" <Cite id="levine-hand-eye-2016" />.

## Offline reinforcement learning

<Term id="offline-reinforcement-learning">Offline reinforcement learning</Term> learns a policy from a fixed dataset of transitions without further environment interaction during training, then deploys it <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />. It can reuse corpora like those in the [datasets module](/data-hardware/datasets). Levine, Kumar, Tucker and Fu identify distributional shift as a central challenge. A learned policy can choose actions outside the behavior distribution, Bellman targets then use erroneously high Q-values for them, and later backups propagate the errors without fresh feedback. The tutorial assumes the data covers enough high-reward transitions for learning to be feasible <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />. BCQ showed that extrapolation error leaves standard off-policy algorithms such as DQN and DDPG unable to learn from fixed batches uncorrelated with the current policy <Cite id="bcq-2018" />.

Conservative value estimation pushes estimates down where data is thin: CQL learns a lower bound on policy value by penalizing Q-values on out-of-distribution actions and raising them under the data distribution <Cite id="cql-2020" />. IQL avoids the query entirely, fitting an expectile over dataset actions so the state value acts as an implicit maximum, then extracting the policy by advantage-weighted regression <Cite id="iql-2022" />. TD3+BC adds a behavior-cloning term and state normalization to an off-policy actor-critic and matches far more elaborate offline methods <Cite id="td3-bc-2021" />.

Q-Transformer discretizes each action dimension and backs up Q-values autoregressively with a Transformer conditioned on observation history and earlier action dimensions. It adds conservative regularization and Monte Carlo and n-step returns, and its real-robot experiments mix successful human demonstrations with failed autonomous trials under sparse success rewards, and online fine-tuning is left for future work <Cite id="q-transformer-2023" />.

### Does any of this beat behavior cloning on robot data?

Mandlekar and colleagues found batch RL weak on human demonstration data. BCQ did well on suboptimal, multimodal agent-generated data, but neither BCQ nor CQL did well on human datasets, a result the authors say "puts the ability of such algorithms to learn from more natural dataset distributions into question". History-dependent BC-RNN was a strong baseline, and the authors note that human demonstrators can act on more than the current observation <Cite id="robomimic-2021" />.

Kumar, Hong, Singh and Levine give sufficient conditions under which particular offline RL algorithms beat BC. Their theory assumes coverage of the optimal policy and horizon-independent trajectory returns, and they test tabular algorithms on gridworlds and CQL against BC on simulated manipulation, navigation and Atari. With noisy data that improves coverage, conservative offline RL has a better horizon-dependent bound than BC on equal expert data, while on expert data similar performance is a worst-case result. Their drawer-manipulation comparison, in simulation, pits BC on expert successes against CQL on equally sized noisy scripted data. Naive CQL can perform no better than BC <Cite id="offline-rl-vs-bc-2022" />. For teleoperation data, a supervised [behavior-cloning baseline](/manipulation/bc-foundations) is the simpler starting point.

Newer real-robot results favor RL. Offline RL pretrained on diverse robot data learned new tasks in a new domain on a real WidowX from as few as 10 demonstrations <Cite id="ptr-2022" />. Generalist policies fine-tuned on RL-generated data beat the same policies trained on human demonstrations by up to 40% in success <Cite id="rldg-2024" />. Physical Intelligence's RECAP pretrains a VLA with offline RL and more than doubles task throughput on some of the hardest tasks <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. RL-100 reports 1000 of 1000 successful real-robot episodes <Cite id="rl-100-2025" />.

Offline data can also seed online learning. RLPD starts online off-policy training from a buffer already holding offline data, with no pretraining phase or explicit conservatism <Cite id="rlpd-2023" />, and HIL-SERL builds on RLPD with human demonstrations and online corrections <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />. Cal-QL changes one line of CQL so that offline initializations fine-tune well online, beating prior methods on 9 of 11 benchmarks <Cite id="cal-ql-2023" />. Q2RL extracts a Q-function from a behavior-cloning policy and switches between cloned and RL actions by their Q-values; on a real robot it learned pipe assembly and kitting in one to two hours of online interaction <Cite id="q2rl-2026" />.

## Sparse reward, and learning from failure

A robot that has never completed a task has no successful trajectory, and a completion-only reward gives its value function nothing to fit. Shaping is one answer ([reward design](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc)). <Term id="hindsight-experience-replay">Hindsight experience replay</Term> targets sparse binary rewards directly, storing transitions under their original goal and alternative goals and recomputing the reward for each. Its simplest strategy uses the goal achieved in the final state, and its future strategy samples goals achieved later in the same episode <Cite id="her-2017" />.

Andrychowicz and colleagues trained DDPG with HER on a simulated seven-DoF Fetch arm for pushing, sliding and pick-and-place, and the future variant learned all three where DDPG alone did not. Pushing added a contact-penetration penalty, and pick-and-place started half its training episodes with the box already grasped. A simulation-trained pick-and-place policy transferred to a physical Fetch without real-robot fine-tuning, using a separately trained camera estimator. HER needs an achieved-goal mapping and a reward recomputable for any replacement goal, and training on multiple goals can help even when only one matters at deployment <Cite id="her-2017" />.

## The reset problem

A simulated episode restarts from a fresh state for free. A physical episode ends wherever the robot is, and a person or a learned behavior has to put it back. Zhu and colleagues name three requirements for real-world RL: learning from raw sensory inputs, assigning rewards without hand-designed perception or instrumentation, and learning without human episode resets. A D'Claw hand learned valve rotation and bead manipulation from RGB images after people supplied goal examples; training ran unattended, but evaluation included manual resets for bead manipulation. The authors leave sample complexity, exploration on harder tasks and safe operation to future work <Cite id="real-world-rl-ingredients-2020" />.

Leave No Trace learns a reset policy alongside the forward policy and uses its value function to abort before non-reversible states, reducing manual resets <Cite id="leave-no-trace-2017" />. Sharma and colleagues' EARL benchmark separates deployed-policy evaluation, which sums gaps to optimal deployment return over training, from continuing-policy evaluation, the limiting average reward of the agent's own actions, and treats interventions as constrained costs. Its experiments still schedule resets during training and stop after a fixed step budget, and the authors note that the two objectives can favor different behavior <Cite id="autonomous-rl-2022" />. VaPRL, from the same group, cuts required interventions by three orders of magnitude against episodic RL on simulated robotics tasks <Cite id="vaprl-2021" />.

<Term id="reset-free-learning">Reset-free learning</Term> replaces manual resets with learned recovery behaviors. Gupta and colleagues' MTRF sequences task-specific policies with a designer-provided task graph, so re-centering, pickup and flip-up restore the conditions for in-hand reorientation after a drop. A D'Hand on a Sawyer arm learned valve reorientation and pipe insertion without manual episode resets. The setup still used supplied rewards, motion capture for the in-hand object, scripted arm motion for pickup, and a user who positioned the objects and started the robot. Pipe insertion used frozen fingers and separate approach and insertion tasks to reduce collisions, and the method assumes that successful upstream tasks leave valid starting states for downstream ones <Cite id="reset-free-rl-2021" />. Without such a task graph a person handles the reset, a cost the human-in-the-loop line pays deliberately <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

## Where this goes from here

The [parallel simulation](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl) module covers the sampler behind on-policy training, [why RL won locomotion](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion) covers which problems it represents faithfully, and [sim-to-real transfer](/rl-sim2real/sim2real-transfer) covers deployment. [RL fine-tuning](/manipulation/rl-finetuning) covers RL on pretrained vision-language-action policies. pi_RL applies online PPO to supervised-fine-tuned pi0 and pi0.5 flow-based policies, replacing flow-matching regression with a policy-gradient update <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />. PLD trains off-policy residual specialists against a frozen VLA, collects hybrid recovery data and fine-tunes the generalist by SFT <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

SERL, an open off-policy real-robot library, trained PCB assembly, cable routing and object relocation in 25 to 50 minutes per policy on average <Cite id="serl-2024" />. HIL-SERL reports 100% success in 100 evaluation trials per task, except IKEA whole assembly at 10 of 10. Nearly all tasks took one to two and a half hours of real-world training and timing-belt assembly six, counting scripted motion, rollouts, intended stops and onboard computation on one RTX 4090 <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />. ConRFT reports 96.3% average success after 45 to 90 minutes of online VLA fine-tuning <Cite id="conrft-2025" />, and ReF-HIL reaches 90% autonomous success in 18 to 63 minutes of active training <Cite id="ref-hil-2026" />. DROID <Cite id="droid-2024" /> and Open X-Embodiment <Cite id="open-x-embodiment-2023" /> supply large real-robot demonstration corpora; both papers evaluate imitation policies, so offline RL on them needs reward labels the datasets do not define.
