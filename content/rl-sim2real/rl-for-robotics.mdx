Reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics trains robot policies from their own experience of acting, formalized as a Markov decision process of states, actions, transitions and rewards <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />. A simulated step is nearly free when thousands of GPU robots act at once <Cite id="rudin-2021" />, while a real step costs machine time and a reset.

## What a budget buys, and what it forbids

<SampleEfficiencyLedger className="my-6" />

A review of robotic deep RL case studies lists real-world challenges that research on games and simulated control seldom addresses <Cite id="ibarz-2021" />, and a 2025 survey of real-world successes calls stable, sample-efficient real-world RL a priority <Cite id="tang-drl-survey-2025" />.

## Two ways to spend a transition

An <Term id="on-policy">on-policy</Term> method can only update on data the current policy generated, so each batch is collected, consumed and discarded; PPO allows several minibatch epochs per batch, where plain policy gradient takes one step <Cite id="ppo-2017" />. An <Term id="off-policy">off-policy</Term> method improves one policy from data another produced, so experience accumulates in a <Term id="replay-buffer">replay buffer</Term>. Underneath is <Term id="q-learning">Q-learning</Term>: fit the value of a state-action pair and bootstrap from the best next action, even one never taken <Cite id="sutton-barto-2018" />.

<ReplayReuse className="my-6" />

TD3 reduced DDPG's overestimation bias with clipped double critics, delayed policy updates and target policy smoothing <Cite id="td3-2018" />. Soft actor-critic added a maximum-entropy objective aimed at sample complexity and brittle convergence <Cite id="sac-2018" />.

### Why robotics defaulted to PPO anyway

With thousands of GPU environments, steps are nearly free and training stability is the scarce resource. SAC's authors credit on-policy training with stability at the price of "poor sample complexity", and call off-policy learning with neural function approximation "a major challenge for stability and convergence" <Cite id="sac-2018" />.

<Image id="isaac-gym-allegro-envs-2021" />

Rudin and colleagues trained ANYmal with 4,096 parallel robots, 98,304 steps per update and 1,500 updates in under twenty minutes on one workstation GPU, more than 122,880 environment steps per second <Cite id="rudin-2021" />. Minitaur's 160,000 control steps over about two hours average about 22 per second, below its 50 Hz rollout rate because resets and overhead count <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />. [Parallel simulation](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl) covers the first regime.

Most GPU-simulation work used PPO for its simplicity and ease of scaling, while off-policy methods were more data-efficient but slower in wall-clock time; Parallel Q-Learning scales to tens of thousands of environments and beats PPO in wall-clock time <Cite id="pql-2023" />, and FastSAC and FastTD3 train sim-to-real humanoid locomotion in 15 minutes on one RTX 4090 <Cite id="seo-2025" />.

<Term id="sample-efficiency">Sample efficiency</Term> is how much experience a method needs to learn. Haarnoja and colleagues learned Minitaur locomotion from 160,000 control steps in about two hours of real-world time that includes resets and the operator <Cite id="haarnoja-walk-2019" />. DayDreamer trained a Unitree A1 to roll over, stand and walk in one hour without a simulator, and ten more minutes of online learning taught it to withstand pushes; the authors intervened manually at the edge of the training area <Cite id="daydreamer-2022" />.

QT-Opt collected over 580,000 grasp attempts across seven robots in about 800 robot hours <Cite id="qt-opt-2018" />. The 2016 hand-eye preprint, which trained a supervised grasp-success predictor, reports about 800,000 grasp attempts over two months with 6 to 14 robots at a time <Cite id="levine-hand-eye-2016" />.

## Offline reinforcement learning

<Term id="offline-reinforcement-learning">Offline reinforcement learning</Term> learns a policy from a fixed dataset of transitions without further environment interaction during training, then deploys it <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />. It can reuse corpora like those in the [datasets module](/data-hardware/datasets), but a learned policy can choose actions outside the behavior distribution, Bellman targets then use erroneously high Q-values for them, and later backups propagate the errors without fresh feedback <Cite id="offline-rl-tutorial-2020" />. BCQ showed that this extrapolation error leaves standard off-policy algorithms unable to learn from fixed batches <Cite id="bcq-2018" />, and CQL answers it by learning a lower bound on policy value, penalizing Q-values on out-of-distribution actions <Cite id="cql-2020" />.

### Does any of this beat behavior cloning on robot data?

Mandlekar and colleagues found batch RL weak on human demonstrations: neither BCQ nor CQL did well on human datasets, while history-dependent BC-RNN was a strong baseline <Cite id="robomimic-2021" />. Kumar, Hong, Singh and Levine give sufficient conditions under which offline RL beats BC, assuming coverage of the optimal policy; with noisy data that improves coverage, conservative offline RL has a better bound than BC on equal expert data, while on expert data similar performance is a worst-case result. Their drawer-manipulation comparison, in simulation, pits BC on expert successes against CQL on noisy scripted data. Naive CQL can perform no better than BC <Cite id="offline-rl-vs-bc-2022" />.

For teleoperation data, a supervised [behavior-cloning baseline](/manipulation/bc-foundations) is the simpler starting point, though newer real-robot results favor RL. Generalist policies fine-tuned on RL-generated data beat the same policies trained on human demonstrations by up to 40% in success <Cite id="rldg-2024" />, and Physical Intelligence's RECAP pretrains a VLA with offline RL and more than doubles task throughput on some of the hardest tasks <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />.

## Sparse reward, and learning from failure

A robot that has never completed a task has no successful trajectory, so a completion-only reward gives its value function nothing to fit. Shaping is one answer ([reward design](/rl-sim2real/reward-design-mpc)). <Term id="hindsight-experience-replay">Hindsight experience replay</Term> targets sparse binary rewards directly, storing transitions under their original goal and alternative goals and recomputing the reward for each, for example with goals achieved later in the same episode <Cite id="her-2017" />.

<HindsightRelabel className="my-6" />

Andrychowicz and colleagues trained DDPG with HER on a simulated seven-DoF Fetch arm for pushing, sliding and pick-and-place, where DDPG alone failed. Pushing added a contact-penetration penalty, and pick-and-place started half its training episodes with the box already grasped. A simulation-trained pick-and-place policy transferred to a physical Fetch without real-robot fine-tuning, and HER needs an achieved-goal mapping and a reward recomputable for any replacement goal <Cite id="her-2017" />.

## The reset problem

A simulated episode restarts from a fresh state for free; a physical one ends wherever the robot is, and a person or a learned behavior has to put it back.

Zhu and colleagues name learning from raw sensory inputs, assigning rewards without hand-designed instrumentation, and learning without human episode resets as the requirements for real-world RL; their D'Claw hand trained unattended, but evaluation included manual resets for bead manipulation <Cite id="real-world-rl-ingredients-2020" />. Leave No Trace learns a reset policy alongside the forward policy and aborts before non-reversible states <Cite id="leave-no-trace-2017" />. Sharma and colleagues' EARL benchmark sums gaps to optimal deployment return over training and treats interventions as constrained costs, though its experiments still schedule resets during training <Cite id="autonomous-rl-2022" />.

<Term id="reset-free-learning">Reset-free learning</Term> replaces manual resets with learned recovery behaviors. Gupta and colleagues' MTRF sequences task-specific policies with a designer-provided task graph, and a D'Hand on a Sawyer arm learned valve reorientation and pipe insertion without manual episode resets. The setup still used supplied rewards, motion capture for the in-hand object, scripted arm motion for pickup and a user who positioned the objects, pipe insertion used frozen fingers, and the method assumes that successful upstream tasks leave valid starting states for downstream ones <Cite id="reset-free-rl-2021" />. Without such a task graph a person handles the reset, a cost the human-in-the-loop line pays deliberately <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />.

## Where this goes from here

[Parallel simulation](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl) explains the sampler behind on-policy training, [why RL won locomotion](/rl-sim2real/why-rl-locomotion) which problems simulation represents faithfully, and [sim-to-real transfer](/rl-sim2real/sim2real-transfer) deployment. [RL fine-tuning](/manipulation/rl-finetuning) covers RL on pretrained vision-language-action policies: pi_RL applies online PPO to flow-based pi0 and pi0.5 policies, replacing flow-matching regression with a policy-gradient update <Cite id="pi-rl-2026" />, and PLD fine-tunes a generalist on hybrid recovery data from residual specialists <Cite id="pld-2026" />.

HIL-SERL reports 100% success in 100 evaluation trials per task, except IKEA whole assembly at 10 of 10. Nearly all tasks took one to two and a half hours of real-world training and timing-belt assembly six, counting scripted motion, rollouts and intended stops <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />. DROID <Cite id="droid-2024" /> and Open X-Embodiment <Cite id="open-x-embodiment-2023" /> supply large real-robot demonstration corpora, but both papers evaluate imitation policies, so offline RL on them needs reward labels the datasets do not define.
