Sim-to-real transfer is the work of making a policy trained in simulation behave the same way on a physical robot. Simulators are cheap and fast but never exact, so behavior learned there can fail on hardware. Four method families close or work around that gap <Cite id="reality-gap-survey-2026" />.

## Domain randomization: train on a distribution of simulators

<Term id="domain-randomization">Domain randomization</Term> trains on many perturbed simulators so that the real world looks like one more variation. The idea is old: in 1995 Jakobi and colleagues found that how well simulated behavior carried over depended on the simulation's noise level <Cite id="jakobi-1995" />. Tobin and colleagues randomized textures, lighting and camera pose, and a network trained only on those images located eight objects to about 1.5 cm in 480 real webcam images; the real world became "just another variation" <Cite id="tobin-2017" />.

Peng and colleagues randomized the physics instead: link mass, joint damping, friction, controller gains, action timing and sensor noise, over [that range of worlds](/how-robots-work/#worlds). A recurrent policy trained only in simulation then pushed a puck to targets with a real Fetch arm <Cite id="peng-2018" />, and later theory showed why policies with memory of recent history matter <Cite id="chen-dr-theory-2021" />. OpenAI's 2018 robot hand randomized friction and appearance and transferred to the physical hand <Cite id="openai-dactyl-2018" />; its Rubik's-cube successor widened the ranges automatically as the policy succeeded, a form of <Term id="curriculum-learning">curriculum</Term> <Cite id="openai-rubiks-cube-2019" />.

<Image id="mujoco-playground-franka-block-2025" />

Isaac Lab, the [GPU-parallel training framework](/rl-sim2real/parallel-sim-rl), ships randomization as a configurable curriculum, with mass and friction changeable during training and mesh scale only before it starts <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. Too much randomization has a cost: poorly tuned ranges "can lead to overly conservative policies" <Cite id="spi-active-2025" />.

## Teacher-student distillation: train with privileges, deploy without

<Term id="teacher-student-distillation">Teacher-student distillation</Term> splits learning in two. A teacher policy learns with privileged simulator information, such as exact terrain and contact forces; a student that sees only what the real robot can sense learns to imitate it. Learning by Cheating used this form for driving <Cite id="learning-by-cheating-2019" />, after asymmetric actor-critic had given full state only to the critic <Cite id="asymmetric-ac-2017" />.

Lee and colleagues' student read two seconds of <Term id="proprioception">proprioceptive</Term> history and took ANYmal quadrupeds through mud, snow, rubble and vegetation without tuning for each environment <Cite id="lee-2020" />. The same pattern distills a depth-camera walking policy for stairs and stepping stones <Cite id="agarwal-vision-2022" />, a stereo-camera grasping network for a KUKA arm and Allegro hand <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />, a camera-only rebar-insertion policy that seated factory rebars at 1.4 mm clearance in 91.3% of real rollouts <Cite id="rebarsim-2026" />, and a camera-based humanoid that ran up to 54 loco-manipulation cycles, though only with up to 64 GPUs <Cite id="viral-2025" />.

RMA adds a module that estimates the current conditions. On the A1 it reads half a second of recent states and actions and updates an estimate of the surroundings about 10 times a second, while the walking policy runs at 100 times a second on the latest estimate. It adapts in fractions of a second without real-world fine-tuning, but fails after large perturbations or several blocked legs, and its estimate captures what matters for behavior without guaranteeing each physical parameter <Cite id="rma-2021" />. UP-OSI had earlier fed dynamics parameters predicted from recent history into one universal policy <Cite id="up-osi-2017" />. The limit is an information gap: a student cannot rebuild what its sensors never see, which is hardest under heavy camera occlusion <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />.

## System identification: measure the gap instead

Randomization averages over model error; <Term id="system-identification">system identification</Term> measures it. Hwangbo and colleagues fitted a neural network to real data from ANYmal's elastic actuators. Policies trained with ideal or analytical actuator models could not take a single step without falling <Cite id="hwangbo-2019" />. Others pair an identified actuator model with randomization <Cite id="tan-quadruped-2018" />, choose test motions that reveal parameters fastest, beating baselines by 42 to 63% on locomotion tasks <Cite id="spi-active-2025" />, tune Spot's simulator against hardware data <Cite id="miller-spot-2025" />, learn complex actuation without torque sensors <Cite id="fey-athletic-2025" /> or identify an unknown payload's mass <Cite id="halo-payload-2026" />.

Friction and bounce show up only on contact. CUE, from Marco Pavone's group (November 2026), probes where its simulation is least accurate; it cut held-out billiard-ball position error by 72% against the strongest baseline in simulation and ran on a real pool table <Cite id="cue-dynamics-2026" />.

ASAP corrects the simulator with what the robot did. It runs motion-tracking policies trained on <Term id="retargeting">retargeted</Term> human motion on a Unitree G1, records the result with motion capture, and trains a second network to output a correction added before each simulator step. The tracking policy is fine-tuned against the corrected simulator and only it is deployed. On hardware, against the uncorrected policy, tracking error fell from 61.2 to 50.2 mm on a kick and from 159 to 112 mm on LeBron James's "Silencer" celebration. It did not win everywhere: in one simulated test plain system identification slightly beat it, 105 against 106 mm, and its limits include motor overheating, dependence on motion capture and the data a full-body correction would need <Cite id="asap-2025" />. HumanoidNDM (October 2026) fine-tunes G1 tracking online without motion capture, through a dynamics model learned from the robot's own sensing <Cite id="humanoid-ndm-2026" />. Swift's champion-level drone racing also combined simulated RL with real data <Cite id="swift-drone-racing-2023" />.

## Real-to-sim: rebuild the scene, keep the physics

Real-to-sim methods rebuild the deployment scene in simulation, usually with photo-realistic 3D reconstructions called Gaussian splats, and keep a standard physics engine. The results can come close to real data. Diffusion policies trained only on SplatSim's simulated demonstrations reached 86.25% real success across four UR5 tasks of 40 trials each, against 97.5% for policies trained on real data, though preparing the splats took manual segmentation and alignment <Cite id="splatsim-2024" />.

| --- | --- | --- |
| RialTo (2024) | Digital twins from small amounts of real data, for RL | Over 67% more robust imitation policies <Cite id="rialto-2024" /> |
| PolaRiS (2025) | Test environments from short video scans | Tracks real generalist-policy performance better than benchmarks <Cite id="polaris-2025" /> |
| Soft-body splats (2025) | Deformable twins from real videos | Simulated rollouts correlate strongly with real ones <Cite id="softbody-splat-eval-2025" /> |
| SimFoundry (2026) | Automated scene generation | 0.911 mean correlation with real results, 7 tasks, 5 policy types <Cite id="simfoundry-2026" /> |
| ZeroBot (2026) | Meshes from a single image | 87% real success after 119 seconds of training on average <Cite id="zerobot-2026" /> |
| AutoRSR (2026) | Assets from scanned workspaces | 73.3 to 86.7% real success, comparable to π0.5 on 150 demonstrations <Cite id="autorsr-2026" /> |
| RoboRender (2026) | Photorealistic video from simulated trajectories | 71% average, about 3.6 times plain visual randomization <Cite id="roborender-2026" /> |
| ForceTwin (2026) | Friction and springs of doors and other articulated objects, probed by a person with a force-sensing gripper | 87% goal completion on nine object and robot pairs <Cite id="forcetwin-2026" /> |
| GRAIL (2026) | Generated scenes for humanoids; a CoRL 2026 best-paper finalist <Cite id="nvidia-corl-gr00t-2-2026" /> | 84% real pick-up success on a Unitree G1 <Cite id="grail-2026" /> |

NVIDIA's Newton 1.0 renders both meshes and splats in one camera <Cite id="newton-manipulation-blog-2026" />. A rebuilt twin can still mislead. On a UR5 ring toss, a model trained on real data placed 90% of rings on the real robot but 30% in RoboGSim, so its scores cannot replace real tests <Cite id="robogsim-2024" />. In a 3D-scanned lab, navigation agents' simulated and real success correlated at only 0.18 because they exploited collision dynamics; tuning simulation parameters raised it to 0.844 <Cite id="kadian-2019" />.

RoboSimStudio, from Ken Goldberg's group at UC Berkeley and NVIDIA (October 2026), lets people and coding agents edit a simulation's solver, contact model and physical parameters. Agents built a two-armed sponge-wiping simulation from a smartphone video in about an hour, and a policy improved there completed the wipe on the real robot. One real rollout displaced a bar support the simulation held fixed, and the authors judged agreement on this single task mainly qualitatively <Cite id="robosimstudio-2026" />.

## Levers behind successful transfer

Choices outside the four families also decide whether transfer works. The survey says the action space "plays a crucial role in reducing the sim-to-real gap", and that co-training with real data benefits robot foundation models <Cite id="reality-gap-survey-2026" />. Mixing simulated with real data raised real-world performance by 38% on average <Cite id="sim-real-cotraining-2025" />, mainly by aligning how the policy represents both <Cite id="lei-cotraining-2026" />, and RL in simulation anchored on real data raised real success by 24% for OpenVLA and 20% for π0.5 on four tabletop tasks <Cite id="rl-co-2026" />. A more strongly regularized actor-critic transferred more robustly than PPO, the usual default <Cite id="smm-ac-2026" />. IndustReal transferred assembly with simulation-aware policy updates and an action integrator at deployment <Cite id="industreal-2023" />, and a motor-energy model with identified parameters supported transfer on three main and ten further legged robots without randomizing dynamics <Cite id="bjelonic-sim2real-2025" />.

Hardware can also meet the simulator halfway. DeepMind's soccer humanoid transferred through high-frequency control, targeted randomization and training perturbations <Cite id="haarnoja-soccer-2023" />. Actuator reality shaping makes physical actuators behave like the simulator's idealized ones <Cite id="actuator-reality-shaping-2026" />, ZEST picks joint gains from actuator models to move skills onto Atlas zero-shot <Cite id="zest-2026" />, and Boston Dynamics reports fewer fidelity issues because Atlas's model and hardware are extremely close <Cite id="bd-atlas-hard-work-2026" />.

## Where each family breaks

Poorly tuned randomization yields overly conservative policies <Cite id="spi-active-2025" />. A student cannot recover state its sensors never observe <Cite id="isaac-lab-2025" />. Identified models miss what was never excited, such as friction the robot has not yet touched <Cite id="cue-dynamics-2026" />, and a scanned twin can rank policies differently from reality <Cite id="kadian-2019" />. Broader skill coverage and affordable real-robot data collection remain open for correction methods like ASAP <span className="max-sm:[&_[role=tooltip]]:-left-28"><Cite id="asap-2025" /></span>.
