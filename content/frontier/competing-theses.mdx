Modern robotics has not converged. Underneath the shared vocabulary of foundation models and generalist policies, the field is running at least six different bets about what gets built next, and the bets are expensive enough that labs must choose: NVIDIA pretrained Cosmos 3 on up to 31 trillion tokens, using clusters as large as 2,048 GB200 GPUs <Cite id="cosmos-3-2026" />, and Physical Intelligence has staked its whole program, from π0 to π0.7, on the scaling thesis <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. What keeps these from being taste debates is that each thesis, stated precisely, predicts something the world can check. This module states the six theses, names who is betting on each, gives the strongest published evidence on both sides, and records the observation that would falsify each one.

Two cautions before the details. First, the theses are not mutually exclusive, and the current frontier systems are already hybrids: π0.7 is an end-to-end <Term id="vision-language-action-model">vision-language-action model</Term> that also reasons hierarchically, consults a lightweight <Term id="world-model">world model</Term> for subgoals, and accepts RL post-training <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. The disagreement is about where the marginal bet belongs, not about which single architecture wins outright. Second, every falsification criterion below depends on measurement the field cannot yet do well; [The Evaluation Crisis](/data-hardware/evaluation-crisis) is the reason a claim like "10x more data, no gain" is harder to establish than it sounds.

## End-to-end VLA scaling

The claim: keep the architecture general, scale data and parameters, and generalist robot intelligence falls out the way generalist language modeling did. The intellectual frame is Rich Sutton's 2019 essay The Bitter Lesson, the argument that general methods leveraging computation beat hand-built structure over the long run <Cite id="sutton-bitter-lesson-2019" />; Brooks answered six days later, in a reply he kept seventy-six words shorter than Sutton's post <Cite id="brooks-better-lesson-2019" />. Physical Intelligence's π line is the clearest published arc, NVIDIA's EgoScale team supplied the cleanest quantitative evidence, and Bessemer's investor analysis stakes the capital side: scaling laws are emerging, and data is the moat <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" />.

The evidence for is real and recent. EgoScale fit a log-linear <Term id="scaling-law">scaling law</Term>, R² = 0.9983, from egocentric human-video pretraining hours to downstream robot performance, and the full recipe lifts average success by 54% on a 22-DoF hand <Cite id="egoscale-2026" />. π0.5 cleaned kitchens in homes it had never seen, and π0.7 recombined trained skills into untrained tasks, exactly the emergent behavior the scaling camp predicts <Cite id="pi05-2025" /> <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. The evidence against is structural. Brooks answered Sutton directly back in 2019: the celebrated image-labeling win rode on a convolutional front end designed by humans for translational invariance, and his 2025 essay extends the argument to speech, whose end-to-end systems consume telephone-engineering spectral processing rather than raw waveforms <Cite id="brooks-better-lesson-2019" /> <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />. His 2025 essay adds the tactile argument, covered in [Dexterity](/frontier/dexterity): vision-only training gives a robot no force channel, and no amount of video supplies one <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />. Ken Goldberg quantifies the data problem as a 100,000-year gap and argues good old-fashioned engineering can bootstrap robots into the data collection that closes it <Cite id="goldberg-data-gap-2025" />, and Karcini and co-authors argue policy scaling alone misses the interfaces that turn physical data into supervision at all <Cite id="karcini-position-2026" />. The kill condition is the cleanest of the six: a plateau in real-world success rate despite a 10x data increase with no architectural change. The one caveat, documented in [Generalization](/frontier/generalization), is that the measured law so far governs a proxy loss, not success rate across unseen environments.

## A hierarchical planner over skills

The bet here is division of labor: a vision-language model reasons about goals and decomposes them into subtasks, and learned skills or a low-level policy execute the plan. As [Hierarchical Approaches](/manipulation/hierarchical) documents, the 2026 version of this thesis is not the 2022 SayCan architecture, where a separate LLM dispatched a fixed skill library <Cite id="saycan-2022" />; the hierarchy has moved inside the network or into a paired fast system. The named proponents are Google DeepMind, whose Gemini Robotics ER 2 is explicitly built as the high-level brain that plans multi-step tasks and hands motor execution to a lower-level VLA <Cite id="gemini-robotics-er2-2026" />; Figure, whose Helix splits slow reasoning, a 200 Hz visuomotor policy, and a 1 kHz <Term id="whole-body-control">whole-body controller</Term> across S2, S1, and S0; and NVIDIA, whose GR00T line keeps the same dual-system shape.

The strongest evidence for the thesis is long-horizon behavior. Helix 02 sequenced 61 loco-manipulation actions across a continuous four-minute dishwasher unload, ordered correctly, with implicit error recovery <Cite id="helix-02-2026" />, and π0.7 absorbs new tasks through the top of its hierarchy: language coaching fine-tunes the high-level policy, which then runs the task autonomously <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. The evidence against comes from the same family of results. π0.7's compositional generalization emerges from one network, suggesting the skill library can be implicit rather than engineered <Cite id="pi07-2026" />, and the original separate-planner lineage already demonstrated the brittleness of the handoff: a planner that can only choose among trained skills stalls the moment the scene calls for none of them <Cite id="saycan-2022" />. The thesis dies if a flat end-to-end model, with no high-level inference pass at all, outperforms the best hierarchical system on long-horizon tasks.

## World-model-based training

The claim is that a learned model of how the world evolves can replace most real-robot data: plan in it, or train the policy inside it, and the data bottleneck stops binding. NVIDIA's Cosmos 3 and Meta's V-JEPA 2 are the largest such bets; the longer Dreamer lineage is covered in [What Is a World Model?](/world-models/taxonomy).

The case for it is sample efficiency. V-JEPA 2 plans zero-shot reaching, grasping, and pick-and-place on Franka arms in labs it never saw, after action-conditioned post-training on less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot video <Cite id="vjepa2-2025" />. And the idea is already load-bearing inside the scaling camp's flagship: π0.7 generates its subgoal images with a lightweight world model initialized from BAGEL <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. The case against is cost and fidelity. Cosmos 3 was pretrained on up to 31 trillion tokens, on clusters as large as 2,048 GB200 GPUs <Cite id="cosmos-3-2026" />, and no world model has yet demonstrated physics predictions accurate enough for contact-rich manipulation, where a one-millimeter error flips stick to slip. Karcini and co-authors argue world models alone are insufficient for the supervision problem regardless <Cite id="karcini-position-2026" />. It dies if world-model training fails to beat VLA scaling alone on real-world contact-rich tasks.

## RL fine-tuning on imitation

The claim here has the strongest current numbers behind it: pretrain on demonstrations, then close the reliability gap with reinforcement learning on the real robot. π*0.6 with Recap more than doubled throughput and passed 90% success on espresso, laundry, and box assembly after RL on the robot's own experience <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. RL-100 reports 100% success across 1,000 evaluation episodes on eight tasks, including a juicing robot that served customers for seven hours without failure <Cite id="rl-100-2025" />. ENPIRE goes further and hands the improvement loop itself to coding agents, reaching 99% on dexterous tasks such as pin-box organizing <Cite id="enpire-2026" />. The mechanics behind these results are covered in [RL Fine-Tuning of Policies](/manipulation/rl-finetuning).

The evidence against is about scope, not efficacy. Every one of these results is per-task: hours of on-robot training buy a specialist, and neither paper claims the improvement transfers to unseen tasks <Cite id="pistar06-2025" /> <Cite id="rl-100-2025" />. The generalist comparison makes the limitation concrete: π0.7 matches specialist throughput on exactly the Recap tasks by distilling specialist experience into the generalist <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. If <Term id="imitation-learning">imitation</Term>-plus-RL is the whole recipe, then deployment cost scales with the number of distinct tasks forever. The recipe stops being the path and becomes a niche tool if RL-finetuned specialists consistently fail to generalize without another round of per-task training.

## Teleoperation as a bridge

The most pragmatic of the six: deploy teleoperated or human-supervised robots for real work now, harvest the data the work generates, and fade the human out as autonomy improves. Nucleus deploys humanoids under human supervision rather than full autonomy on exactly this logic <Cite id="nucleus-supervised-2026" />, Physical Intelligence's Recap turns human coaching with corrections into more than doubled throughput <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />, and Bessemer names the economics: the data flywheel, in its words turning robot data into better decisions, better models, and better deployments <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" />. The two strongest verified deployment records are both supervised programs of this kind: Agility's 65,000+ operating hours across nine facilities and Figure's 1,250+ hours at BMW Spartanburg <Cite id="technology-org-deployed-2026" />. The rigs that feed this pipeline are compared in [Teleoperation Rigs](/data-hardware/teleop-rigs).

The evidence against is that the bridge may not reach the far side. <Term id="teleoperation">Teleoperation</Term> is expensive and slow, and Bessemer's own sources say so: Ian Glow argues you will never get the scale or diversity you need from teleop alone <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" />. Meanwhile egocentric human video is demonstrating a cheaper source with a measured scaling law and no robot in the loop at all <Cite id="egoscale-2026" />. The bridge dies if autonomous policies reach better than 99% success trained without any teleoperation data, because then it has no reason to exist.

## Humanoid versus task-specific

The sixth thesis is a pair, because the two sides falsify each other. The humanoid side, backed by Figure, Tesla, 1X, Apptronik, and Unitree, bets that a human body is the right interface to a world built for humans, so one general form factor addresses every task. The task-specific side, argued by Rodney Brooks, whose Robust.AI builds warehouse robots, and embodied by Agility's logistics-focused Digit, bets that purpose-built systems win on cost and reliability for any task you can name in advance.

The humanoid evidence is the strongest it has ever been. Helix 02 runs whole-body loco-manipulation, including a livestreamed eight-hour autonomous shift on a factory floor <Cite id="helix-02-2026" /> <Cite id="figure-8hr-shift-2026" />, and Gemini Robotics 2 controls full humanoids from feet to fingertips with a single model <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2-2026" />. The task-specific evidence is that the verified deployment records all belong to narrow applications, that Brooks calls practical humanoids within decades pure fantasy thinking <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />, and that the hardware has not earned the benefit of the doubt: no human-like robot hand has survived real-world deployment at industrial durability. Both directions of the kill condition are the same measurement: cost per task. If task-specific robots keep winning it in the same applications, the humanoid thesis dies; if a humanoid reaches parity across a broad task range, the task-specific thesis does.

## What would settle it

Read back down the falsification column and a watchlist falls out. A 10x or 100x data increase with no architectural change, measured against real-world success rate, answers the scaling question. A flat model beating the best hierarchical system on long-horizon tasks answers the hierarchy question. A world model that improves real contact-rich performance beyond VLA scaling answers the world-model question. RL specialists transferring without retraining, autonomy above 99% trained with no teleoperation data, and humanoid cost-per-task parity answer the rest. None of these observations is cheap, and several are years away; but each is a concrete, checkable event rather than a matter of taste. Until they arrive, the honest position on all six is the one the evidence column already encodes: the proponents are named, the counter-evidence is on the record, and the field has placed real capital on both sides of every row. [The Bear Case](/frontier/bear-case) takes the capital side of that sentence seriously, and its watchlist tracks the status of each of these observations.
