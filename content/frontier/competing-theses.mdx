The competing theses are the rival bets on how robot intelligence gets built: end-to-end VLA scaling, hierarchy, world models, RL fine-tuning, teleoperation as a bridge, and the humanoid form factor. The bets are expensive. Figure has committed \$3.5B of compute to training Helix <Cite id="helix-2-5-2026" />, and Physical Intelligence has staked its program, from π0 to π0.7, on scaling <Cite id="pi07-2026" />.

## The theses can coexist, and π0.7 already combines several

Each card states one thesis, its bet and the result that would prove it wrong. Tap "Just make it bigger", then "Let the robot imagine first", to read the evidence on both sides. The theses can coexist: π0.7 is an end-to-end <Term id="vision-language-action-model">vision-language-action model</Term> that also reasons hierarchically. It can take optional subgoal-image prompts from a separate <Term id="world-model">world model</Term> initialized from BAGEL, which the paper describes as lightweight, and it distills experience collected by RL-trained π*0.6 specialists <Cite id="pi07-2026" />.

<span id="end-to-end-vla-scaling" />
## Scaling has early laws, such as EgoScale's log-linear fit to validation loss

The claim: keep the architecture general, scale data and parameters, and generalist robot intelligence follows, as it did for language. Rich Sutton's 2019 essay The Bitter Lesson frames it: general methods that leverage computation win in the long run <Cite id="sutton-bitter-lesson-2019" />. Brooks answered six days later, in a reply that paraphrases Sutton and describes itself, including its closing comment, as "seventy six words shorter than Sutton’s post" <Cite id="brooks-better-lesson-2019" />. Bessemer's investor analysis argues that "scaling laws are emerging" and that capital is the moat <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" /> ([VLA models](/manipulation/vla-models/)).

EgoScale fit a log-linear <Term id="scaling-law">scaling law</Term> (R² = 0.9983) from human-video pretraining hours to validation loss, which it reports correlates strongly with downstream robot performance. Its full recipe lifts average success by 54% on a 22-DoF hand <Cite id="egoscale-2026" />. Figure reports a human-to-humanoid transfer law on robot-action prediction, with Index pretraining lifting zero-shot success in 30 unseen homes from 9% to 56% <Cite id="helix-2-5-2026" />. Lin et al. found single-task success following "a roughly power-law relationship with the number of environments and objects" <Cite id="lin-data-scaling-laws-2024" />. In unseen homes π0.5 cleaned kitchens, and π0.7 recombined trained skills into untrained tasks <Cite id="pi05-2025" /> <Cite id="pi07-2026" />.

## Brooks and Goldberg cite engineered priors and a 100,000-year data gap

Brooks answered Sutton directly: image-labeling systems use a human-designed convolutional front end for translational invariance <Cite id="brooks-better-lesson-2019" />. His 2025 essay extends the point to speech preprocessing rooted in telephone engineering, listing FFTs and Mel filter banks while noting that implementations use different selections. He also acknowledges that leaving as much as possible to learning was critical to the gains in speech and image tasks. Vision-only training, he adds, gives a robot no force channel ([dexterity](/frontier/dexterity/)) <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />. On compute, he notes that "self driving cars require about 2,500 Watts of power for computation", while "a human brain only requires 20 Watts" <Cite id="brooks-better-lesson-2019" />. Ken Goldberg puts the data problem at a 100,000-year gap and argues good old-fashioned engineering can bootstrap robots into collecting the data <Cite id="goldberg-data-gap-2025" />. Karcini and co-authors argue policy scaling misses the interfaces that turn physical data into supervision <Cite id="karcini-position-2026" />. The kill condition is a plateau in real-world success despite 10x more data with no architectural change. The laws so far govern a proxy loss or a single task ([generalization](/frontier/generalization/)).

<span id="a-hierarchical-planner-over-skills" />
## Hierarchical systems run long tasks, while single networks blur the line

The bet here is division of labor: a vision-language model decomposes goals into subtasks, and learned skills or a low-level policy execute them. SayCan (2022) had a separate LLM dispatch a fixed skill library <Cite id="saycan-2022" />; today the hierarchy sits inside the network or in a paired fast system ([hierarchical approaches](/manipulation/hierarchical/)). Google DeepMind builds Gemini Robotics ER 2 as a high-level brain that plans multi-step tasks and hands motor execution to a lower-level VLA <Cite id="gemini-robotics-er2-2026" />. In Figure's Helix 02, S2 interprets scenes and language and sequences behaviors. S1 converts perception into full-body joint targets at 200 Hz, and S0 executes at 1 kHz as the <Term id="whole-body-control">whole-body controller</Term> <Cite id="helix-02-2026" />. NVIDIA's GR00T N1 is "a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model with a dual-system architecture" <Cite id="gr00t-n1-2025" />.

The evidence for is long-horizon behavior. Figure reports that Helix 02 completed a continuous four-minute dishwasher unloading-and-reloading task without resets or human intervention, in 61 loco-manipulation actions <Cite id="helix-02-2026" />. In π0.7, language coaching fine-tunes the high-level policy, which then runs the new task autonomously <Cite id="pi07-2026" />. Against it, π0.7's compositional generalization emerges from one network <Cite id="pi07-2026" />, and SayCan's authors found their system bounded by "the range and capabilities of the underlying skills" <Cite id="saycan-2022" />. Gemini Robotics 1.5 already "interleaves actions with a multi-level internal reasoning process" inside one VLA, blurring the line <Cite id="gemini-robotics-15-2025" />. The thesis dies if a flat model with no high-level inference pass outperforms the best hierarchical system on long-horizon tasks.

<span id="world-model-based-training" />
## World models plan from under 62 hours of robot video but still miss contact

The claim is that a learned model of how the world evolves can replace most real-robot data: plan in it, or train the policy inside it. NVIDIA's Cosmos 3 and Meta's V-JEPA 2 are the largest such bets ([what is a world model?](/world-models/taxonomy/)). V-JEPA 2 plans zero-shot reaching, grasping and pick-and-place on Franka arms in unseen labs after post-training on less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot video <Cite id="vjepa2-2025" />. DreamZero, a world action model that serves as the policy, reports over 2x better generalization to new tasks and environments than state-of-the-art VLAs <Cite id="dreamzero-2026" /> ([generative video](/world-models/generative-video/)).

The case against is cost and fidelity. Cosmos 3 was pretrained on up to 31 trillion tokens, on clusters as large as 2,048 GB200 GPUs <span className="block" data-cosmos-source-placement="line-start"><Cite id="cosmos-3-2026" />,</span> and no world model yet predicts contact physics well enough for manipulation, where a one-millimeter error flips stick to slip. Karcini and co-authors argue world models alone cannot solve the supervision problem <Cite id="karcini-position-2026" />. It dies if world-model training fails to beat VLA scaling alone on real-world contact-rich tasks.

<span id="rl-fine-tuning-on-imitation" />
## RL fine-tuning reaches 90% to 100% per task, but each result is a specialist

The claim: pretrain on demonstrations, then close the reliability gap with RL on the real robot. HIL-SERL reached near-perfect success in 1 to 2.5 hours of training <Cite id="hil-serl-2024" />. π*0.6 with Recap more than doubled throughput and reached the 90%+ range on all tasks except diverse laundry. Its first Recap iteration lifts laundry over 90%, and box assembly finishes at about 90% for folding and labeling <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. RL-100 reports 100% success across 1,000 evaluation episodes on eight tasks, including a juicing robot that served customers for about seven hours without failure <Cite id="rl-100-2025" />. ENPIRE hands the improvement loop to coding agents, reaching 99% on dexterous tasks such as pin-box organizing <Cite id="enpire-2026" /> ([RL fine-tuning](/manipulation/rl-finetuning/)).

The evidence against concerns transfer. Each result is per-task: hours of on-robot training buy a specialist, and neither paper claims transfer to unseen tasks <Cite id="pistar06-2025" /> <Cite id="rl-100-2025" />. Physical Intelligence reports that one π0.7 model matches the RL-trained specialists on the evaluated laundry-folding, espresso-making, and box-building tasks, sometimes exceeding their throughput, after distilling Recap experience with strategy metadata. The paper measures throughput as successful episodes per hour; inference speed is a separate measure <Cite id="pi07-2026" /> <Cite id="pi07-blog-2026" />. The recipe becomes a niche tool if RL specialists keep failing to generalize without per-task retraining.

<span id="teleoperation-as-a-bridge" />
## Teleoperation feeds deployment data, but its share of vendor hours is unreported

The claim: deploy <Term id="teleoperation">teleoperated</Term> or human-supervised robots for real work now, harvest the data, and fade the human out as autonomy improves. Nucleus deploys humanoids under human supervision on this logic <Cite id="nucleus-supervised-2026" />, and 1X lets NEO owners schedule an expert for chores it does not know, "helping NEO learn" <Cite id="1x-neo-2026" />. Sergey Levine argues that surrogates for real robot data usually end up "a lousy spoon with holes or an ineffective blunt fork" <Cite id="levine-sporks-2025" />. Recap, the π*0.6 method, learns from expert teleoperated interventions <Cite id="pistar06-2025" />. Bessemer’s investor outlook quotes Voxel51’s Brian Moore and Foxglove’s Adrian Macneil on a data flywheel: “better decisions, better model improvements, and better deployments faster than everyone else.” Bessemer lists both companies in its portfolio <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" />. Agility reports 65,000 hours of Digit production experience <Cite id="agility-digit-production" />, and Figure reports 1,250+ runtime hours at BMW Spartanburg <Cite id="figure-bmw-production-2025" />. Neither quantifies teleoperation's share of those hours ([teleoperation rigs](/data-hardware/teleop-rigs/)).

Bessemer quotes the Zeromatter CEO, Ian Glow: "you'll never get the scale or diversity you need from teleop alone." He recommends internet data or simulators with reinforcement learning, and Bessemer cautions that world models are expensive and sim-to-real manipulation remains an open research problem <Cite id="bessemer-robotics-2026" />. Egocentric human video is a cheaper source with a measured scaling law <Cite id="egoscale-2026" />. Generalist reports that its base models are "trained without any robot data" and reach 99% average success with "only 1 hour of robot data" per task <Cite id="generalist-gen1-2026" />. The bridge dies if autonomous policies pass 99% success trained without any teleoperation data.

<span id="humanoid-versus-task-specific" />
## Cost per task decides between humanoid and task-specific robots

The sixth thesis is a pair, because the two sides falsify each other. The humanoid side, backed by Figure, Tesla, 1X, Apptronik, and Unitree, bets that a human body is the right interface to a world built for humans. The task-specific side, argued by Rodney Brooks, who describes his company's warehouse robots and his work at Robust.AI <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />, bets that purpose-built systems win on cost and reliability for any task named in advance. Amazon has deployed its millionth robot <Cite id="amazon-million-robots-2025" />. Agility sits between them, deploying its Digit humanoid in narrow logistics work <Cite id="agility-digit-production" />.

Helix 02 runs whole-body loco-manipulation <Cite id="helix-02-2026" />. Figure's May 2026 package-sorting livestream, planned as one eight-hour shift <Cite id="adcock-figure-livestream-2026" />, ran for 200 hours by Figure's account <Cite id="helix-2-5-2026" />. Gemini Robotics 2 controls full humanoids from feet to fingertips with a single model <Cite id="gemini-robotics-2-2026" />. In his 2025 essay, Brooks calls plug-compatible humanoids replacing people in manual jobs at lower prices and just as well "pure fantasy thinking" within decades. He argues that articulated fingers lack the robustness, force and lifetime for industrial applications, and that human-like hand designs have not reached deployment. He reports earlier factory deployments of Baxter and Sawyer, and forecasts task-specialized robots still called humanoids over the next fifteen years <Cite id="brooks-dexterity-2025" />. Both kill conditions are one measurement: cost per task. If task-specific robots keep winning it in the same applications, the humanoid thesis dies; if a humanoid reaches parity across many tasks, the task-specific thesis does.

<span id="what-would-settle-it" />
## Six observations would settle the theses, and GEN-1 nearly meets one

The cards' "wrong if" lines form a watchlist: [real-world success](/data-hardware/evaluation-crisis/) flat after a 10x or 100x data increase; a flat model beating the best hierarchy on long horizons; a world model beating VLA scaling on contact-rich tasks. The rest are RL specialists transferring without retraining, autonomy above 99% with no teleoperation data, and humanoid cost-per-task parity. GEN-1 nearly meets the teleoperation test, but uses 1 hour of robot data per task <Cite id="generalist-gen1-2026" />. [The bear case](/frontier/bear-case/) tracks each observation on its watchlist.
